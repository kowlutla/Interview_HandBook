HLD: Design Trade-offs and Judgment interview questions

The "why did you choose X over Y" questions that probe senior engineers: consistency vs availability per feature, SQL vs NoSQL, push vs pull, sync vs async, monolith vs microservices, build vs buy, single vs multi-region, and more. Each answer gives a decision rule, a concrete example and the cost of the choice. Also covers scaling stages, ambiguity, design reviews, tech debt and behavioural architecture questions.

How to use this page

Trade-off questions have no single right answer. Interviewers grade the reasoning: do you state the options, name the deciding factor, give a concrete example, admit the cost and say what would change your mind? A good template for any answer is: "It depends on X. If X is A, I choose P because ...; the cost is ...; I would revisit if ...". Each answer below follows that spirit: a decision rule, an example, a cost. Never answer with "it depends" alone.

Every answer here starts with a short Say this line you can speak in about 20 seconds, then explains the idea from scratch with a worked example, so you can study it rather than memorise it. The "Quick revision table" at the end compresses all of it into one page for the night before.

Say: "My default is ___. I switch to ___ when ___. The price I pay is ___, and I would watch ___ to know if I was wrong."

Consistency, availability and data-model trade-offs

How do you decide between consistency and availability for a feature? Senior

Say this: "I ask what is worse for the user: a wrong answer or an error. If wrong data means lost money, legal exposure or physical harm, I choose consistency and accept errors during a partition. If an error loses a sale and a stale value is a minor annoyance, I choose availability and reconcile later. I decide per feature, not per company."

What the terms mean. A network partition is when some of your servers cannot talk to each other (a cable cut, a bad router, a zone isolated). Consistency here means every read sees the latest successful write. Availability means every request to a healthy node gets a real answer. The CAP theorem says that while a partition is happening you can keep only one of the two. Partitions are rare, but you must decide in advance what the system does when one happens, because it will happen.

A worked example. Two replicas, A and B, each hold a balance of 100. The link between them breaks. A customer withdraws 80 at A, and at the same moment another withdraws 80 at B.

  • CP choice (consistency): B knows it cannot reach A, so it refuses the write ("try again later"). Balance stays correct, one customer sees an error.
  • AP choice (availability): both accept. Both customers are happy, but the account is now at minus 60 once the link heals. You need a reconciliation process and maybe an overdraft fee.

For a bank the second outcome is unacceptable. For a shopping cart, the equivalent anomaly (two versions of the cart) is trivially merged, so refusing the write is the worse outcome.

The everyday version. Even with no partition there is a constant trade between latency and consistency (the PACELC view): waiting for a second replica to confirm costs milliseconds. Strong consistency is a latency cost on every request, not only during rare failures.

FeatureChoiceWhy
Bank balance and transferConsistency (CP)Double spend or lost money is unacceptable. Better to show "try again" than a wrong balance.
Shopping cartAvailability (AP)An unavailable cart loses sales. Merging two divergent carts is easy (union of items).
Likes / view counterAvailability, eventualBeing off by a few for seconds hurts nobody.
Inventory at checkoutConsistency at the last stepBrowse with stale counts, but reserve with a strict atomic decrement so you do not oversell.

Cost: CP features show errors or higher latency under failure; AP features need conflict-resolution code and users occasionally see odd states. What the interviewer probes next: "one product, both kinds?" Yes. A shop is AP for browsing and cart, CP for payment and stock reservation. Saying "we chose AP for the whole system" is a red flag.

Why would you make a shopping cart available rather than consistent? Intermediate

Say this: "Because a refused 'add to cart' is a lost sale, while a briefly inconsistent cart is easy to merge. The cart can be eventually consistent. The order at checkout cannot."

Why it exists as a design. Amazon's Dynamo paper (2007) was motivated by exactly this: customers must always be able to add items, even when servers or links fail. The system accepts the write on whichever replica is reachable, even if that replica has not seen the latest cart.

How the merge works, with an example.

  1. Cart starts as {book}. Replica 1 and replica 2 both hold it.
  2. A partition separates them. On replica 1 the user adds a pen: {book, pen}. A retry hits replica 2 and adds a mug: {book, mug}.
  3. The partition heals. The system sees two versions that neither descends from the other (it tracks this with version vectors or vector clocks).
  4. It keeps both and merges on read by union: {book, pen, mug}. Nothing the user added is lost.

The known anomaly. If the user removed the book on replica 1 while replica 2 still had it, the union brings the book back. A "resurrected item" is a mild annoyance, whereas refusing the customer is a real loss. That asymmetry is the whole argument.

Rule: choose availability when writes are easy to merge (add-only sets, counters, last-writer-wins on harmless fields) and an error costs revenue. Cost: you must write and test merge logic, and tolerate anomalies. The cart is not the order: at checkout the price, stock and payment are made strongly consistent. Pitfall: last-writer-wins with wall clocks silently drops one writer's change when clocks drift, so for carts prefer union semantics.

Why must a bank balance be strongly consistent, and what does it cost? Intermediate

Say this: "Because two concurrent withdrawals against a stale balance can both succeed and overdraw the account. I make each account's writes serial, replicate synchronously to at least one standby, and accept extra latency and refusing writes on the minority side of a partition."

What goes wrong without it (a race). Balance is 100. Request 1 reads 100, request 2 reads 100 (from a lagging replica or just before request 1 commits). Each checks "100 is at least 80", each writes 20. Two withdrawals of 80 were paid out from a balance of 100. This is a lost update or write skew problem.

How it is prevented.

  • All writes for one account go to a single primary (one shard owns the account), so they are naturally ordered.
  • The debit is a conditional update in one transaction: UPDATE account SET bal = bal - 80 WHERE id = ? AND bal >= 80. If zero rows change, reject. Two such statements cannot both win because the row lock serialises them.
  • A transfer debits one account and credits another inside one transaction, or, if they live in different services, through a saga with an immutable ledger of entries (never update a balance without a journal row).
  • The commit is acknowledged only after at least one standby has the log record (synchronous replication), so a primary crash cannot lose a confirmed transfer.

Cost. Each write waits for a replica acknowledgement: a few milliseconds in the same region, much more across regions. During a partition, the side that cannot reach a quorum refuses writes. Per-account write throughput is limited, which is fine because one account rarely needs thousands of writes per second. Hot accounts (a merchant collecting payments) are the exception, and are usually handled by splitting into sub-ledgers and summing.

Pitfall: "strong consistency" does not mean "use a distributed lock". Locks across services expire, leak and stall; ledger plus idempotent operations plus a saga is sturdier.

How would you build a likes counter, and which trade-off do you make? Intermediate

Say this: "I keep the per-user fact exact, 'did I like this', and let the aggregate count lag. For a hot post I shard the counter or aggregate asynchronously so one row is not the bottleneck."

The problem. A single likes column updated on every like is a hot row. Each update needs a row lock, so a viral post with 5,000 likes per second serialises on one row, lock waits grow, latency spikes and the database suffers for unrelated queries too.

Three designs, from simple to scalable.

  • Exact, synchronous: one update per like. Perfect accuracy, fine up to maybe hundreds of updates per second on one row, falls over on a viral post.
  • Sharded counter: keep N sub-counters per post (for example 16 keys likes:123:0..15). A like increments a random one, so contention drops about 16 times. A read sums the 16 values (or reads a cached sum). Cost: reads are more expensive.
  • Async aggregation: a like writes one record to a log or table with a unique key (user_id, post_id), which both dedupes and stores the truth. A consumer batches these and updates the cached count every second or so. The displayed number is eventually consistent.

Tiny example. 10,000 likes arrive in one second. With async aggregation the consumer reads them as one batch and does count += 10000 once, instead of 10,000 row locks.

Rule: exact for the personal fact (the heart stays filled after refresh, "read your own writes"), approximate for the public aggregate (it shows 1.2M anyway). Cost: the count can be briefly wrong, and it can disagree with the list of likers until the aggregator catches up. Pitfall: unlike must be idempotent too, or retries double-count.

How do you handle inventory without overselling while keeping browse fast? Senior

Say this: "I split the path. Browsing reads a cached, possibly stale count. Checkout does an atomic conditional decrement, with a time-limited reservation for hot items. That keeps browse fast and the last step correct."

Why split. Browse traffic is maybe 100 times checkout traffic. Making every product page read hit the strictly consistent stock row would melt the primary. But showing "3 left" when there are really 2 is harmless. Only the moment of commitment needs to be exact.

The checkout step, in order.

  1. User clicks buy. Run one atomic statement: UPDATE stock SET qty = qty - 1 WHERE sku = ? AND qty > 0.
  2. Check the affected row count. 1 means you got the item. 0 means sold out, show an apology. The database serialises competing updates, so the last unit goes to exactly one buyer.
  3. Continue to payment. If payment fails or times out, put the unit back (compensate).

Reservations. For high demand drops, decrementing at "add to cart" locks stock in abandoned carts. The fix is a reservation with a TTL: hold the unit for 10 minutes (a row with an expiry, or a Redis key with TTL), convert to a sale on payment, and release on expiry. This is how ticket sites work.

Hot SKU. If 50,000 people hit the same SKU at launch, the single row becomes the bottleneck. Options: queue purchase requests per SKU and process them in order, pre-split the stock into buckets (100 units as 10 buckets of 10, pick one at random), or use a virtual waiting room that admits users at the rate the system can process.

Cost and judgement. Strict decrement on hot items adds contention. Some businesses deliberately oversell slightly (airlines, hotels) because an apology or rebooking costs less than empty seats. That is a business decision, so ask the product owner before building the strictest version. Pitfall: read-then-write in application code (if qty > 0 then update) without the condition in the same statement reintroduces the race.

How do you choose strong vs eventual consistency per feature, and how do you explain the middle options? Intermediate

Say this: "It is a spectrum. I pick the weakest guarantee the user will not notice. Most 'eventual' features really only need read-your-writes, which is cheap to provide."

Why a spectrum exists. With one primary and several asynchronous replicas, a replica can be a few hundred milliseconds behind. If you read from a replica right after writing to the primary, you may not see your own write. The guarantees below are named fixes for specific, user-visible weirdness.

GuaranteePlain meaningHow it is usually provided
EventualIf writes stop, all copies converge. No promise about when.Default async replication.
Read-your-writesAfter I post a comment, I see it.Read from the primary for a few seconds after my write, or send my reads to a replica that has reached my write's log position.
Monotonic readsI never see data go back in time (a comment appears, then vanishes on refresh).Pin a user to one replica (hash of user id).
CausalA reply never shows before the post it answers.Carry causal metadata (version vectors, dependency tokens).
Strong (linearizable)Every read sees the latest committed write, as if there were one copy.Single leader with sync replication, or consensus (Raft, Paxos).

Worked example. A user changes their display name. With plain eventual consistency they refresh and see the old name, think it failed, and click save again. Adding read-your-writes (route that user's profile reads to the primary for 10 seconds) fixes the experience, while everyone else can still read lagging replicas. That is one line of routing logic, not a new database.

Where strong is truly needed: account balances, unique usernames or emails (two people must not both claim one), leader election, distributed locks, stock at the final decrement.

Cost: stronger guarantees cost latency and availability; weaker ones cost application code to cope with anomalies and more explaining to support teams. Pitfall: saying "eventual consistency" without saying how long. Quote the typical lag and the worst case.

SQL vs NoSQL: how do you choose for a given workload? Basic

Say this: "I default to a relational database such as Postgres. I move to NoSQL only when a specific requirement pushes me there, such as a very high write rate with simple key lookups, or documents that are always read as a whole. I would state the access pattern and numbers first."

What each family is. A relational (SQL) database stores rows in tables with a fixed schema, supports joins, constraints, multi-row transactions and ad hoc queries. NoSQL is a loose name for stores that give up some of that for scale or flexibility: key-value and wide-column (DynamoDB, Cassandra), document (MongoDB), graph (Neo4j), and search engines (Elasticsearch).

Why default to SQL. Requirements change. Today you only read orders by id; next quarter someone wants "all orders by customer in a region last month grouped by product". A relational database answers new questions with a new query. A key-value store answers only the questions its keys were designed for, so a new question can mean a new table plus a backfill. A single well-tuned Postgres handles thousands of writes per second and terabytes of data with read replicas, which covers most products.

PressureLean towardsExample
Multi-row invariants, reporting, evolving queriesSQLOrders, payments, billing
Massive write rate, known access pattern, simple key lookupsWide-column or key-value (Cassandra, DynamoDB)Event/time-series storage, sessions, messages
Self-contained documents read as a wholeDocument store (MongoDB)Product catalogue, CMS content
Relationship traversalGraphFraud rings, recommendations
Full-text and faceted searchSearch engine (Elasticsearch)Product search, log search

Worked example. A chat app stores 2 billion messages a day, always read as "latest 50 messages in conversation X". The only query is by (conversation_id, time). That fits a wide-column store: partition by conversation, sort by time, scale by adding nodes. The same company keeps users, billing and subscriptions in Postgres because those need transactions and reporting.

Cost of NoSQL: you design tables around queries up front, give up or hand-build joins and secondary indexes, and often get weaker transactions. Changing the access pattern later is painful. Cost of SQL: scaling writes beyond one primary needs sharding, which you manage yourself (or use a distributed SQL system).

Trap: "NoSQL is faster" or "SQL does not scale". Both are false as blanket claims. Say what access pattern and scale you have, with numbers.
When is it justified to use more than one database in a system? Intermediate

Say this: "Only when two workloads have genuinely incompatible needs, and each extra store must earn its place. I keep one system of record and treat the others as rebuildable copies."

What polyglot persistence is. Using several kinds of datastore, each for what it is best at: Postgres for transactions, Elasticsearch for full-text search, Redis for caching and counters, S3 for files, a warehouse for analytics.

Why it is tempting, and why it hurts. Each store does its job better than a general database could. But each one also brings backup, upgrades, security patches, monitoring, access control, on-call knowledge and, most importantly, a data-sync problem. The search index is a derived copy of the orders table. If an update reaches Postgres but not Elasticsearch, search shows stale results, and nobody notices until a customer complains.

Worked example. An e-commerce site: orders in Postgres (system of record); product search in Elasticsearch fed by change data capture from Postgres; sessions and rate limits in Redis. Three stores, three clear reasons. Adding MongoDB "for the catalogue" when Postgres JSONB columns would do is a store without a requirement behind it.

Rules of thumb.

  • Write down the requirement the extra store meets that existing ones cannot (for example "sub-second faceted search over 20 million products").
  • Single source of truth: data is written to one place first; others are projections that can be rebuilt from it.
  • A team of five should rarely run more than two or three technologies.

Cost: operations multiplied, drift between copies, and skills spread thin. Probe: "how do you keep Elasticsearch in sync?" Answer: change data capture or the outbox pattern into a queue, idempotent indexing, and a periodic full reindex as repair.

Normalize vs denormalize: how do you decide? Intermediate

Say this: "I normalize the system of record so every fact lives once, and I denormalize the specific read paths that are hot and tolerate slight staleness."

What the words mean. Normalized data stores each fact in exactly one place and links to it by id: a comment row holds author_id, and the name lives in the users table. Denormalized data copies facts to where they are read: the comment row also holds author_name and author_avatar_url.

Why normalize first. When the author changes their name, you update one row and every comment is right. There is no way for two copies to disagree. That is why transactional data (orders, accounts) is normalized.

Why denormalize some reads. Reading a comment list normalized means a join with users for every request. At 5,000 requests per second that join work adds up, and in NoSQL stores joins do not exist at all.

Worked example. A product page needs product, price, seller name and average rating, which live in four tables. Option A: join four tables on every view. Option B: keep a product_page_view row per product, refreshed by events whenever any source changes; the page is one lookup.

-- normalized read (4 joins)
SELECT ... FROM product p JOIN price pr ... JOIN seller s ... JOIN rating r ...
-- denormalized read (1 lookup)
SELECT * FROM product_page_view WHERE product_id = ?

Cost of denormalizing: write amplification (a seller rename updates thousands of copies), a sync mechanism (triggers, change data capture, events), and a bounded staleness you must accept. Copies can drift, so plan a rebuild job. Decision rule: denormalize when the read-to-write ratio is high (say 100 to 1 or more), the query is on a latency-critical path, and staleness of seconds is acceptable. Do it as a derived read model, never as the only copy.

Schema-on-write vs schema-on-read? Intermediate

Say this: "Schema-on-write validates and shapes data when it is stored; schema-on-read stores raw data and interprets it at query time. I use the first for trusted business data and the second for the raw landing zone, then promote cleaned data into typed tables."

Schema-on-write (relational tables, warehouses). The table says amount DECIMAL NOT NULL. A record with "abc" there is rejected at insert. Bad data stops at the door, queries are fast and predictable, but changing the shape needs a migration, and a source that sends a surprise field gets rejected.

Schema-on-read (data lakes with raw JSON or CSV files, document stores). Ingestion accepts anything and never fails. The structure is applied when someone queries it, for example by a query engine reading JSON files and casting amount to a number.

Worked example. A source system adds a field in March and renames another in July. With schema-on-write, ingestion breaks in March until a migration ships. With schema-on-read, ingestion never breaks, but a report written in January silently returns nulls for the renamed field after July, and nobody knows until finance asks why revenue dropped.

Schema-on-writeSchema-on-read
Bad dataRejected earlySurfaces late, at query time
Change a fieldMigrationJust start writing it; readers adapt
Query speedFast, indexed, typedSlower, parsing on every read
Best forTransactions, trusted metricsRaw landing zone, exploration

Rule: schema-on-write for data that drives money or decisions; schema-on-read where sources change often and you want to keep everything. The usual pipeline is raw (schema-on-read) to cleaned (typed, validated) to curated tables. Cost of schema-on-read: "the schema lives in the heads of the readers", which becomes a data swamp. Mitigate with a schema registry, data contracts and automated quality checks.

Optimistic vs pessimistic concurrency control? Intermediate

Say this: "Optimistic assumes conflicts are rare: work without locks and detect a conflict at write time with a version check. Pessimistic assumes conflicts are likely: lock first. Low contention and long user think time means optimistic; hot rows and short critical sections mean pessimistic or an atomic conditional update."

The problem both solve. Two users edit the same record. If both read, change and write back, the later write silently overwrites the earlier (a lost update).

Pessimistic. Lock the row when you read it: SELECT ... FOR UPDATE. The second user waits until the first commits. No conflicts can happen, but others are blocked, and two transactions locking rows in opposite order can deadlock.

Optimistic. Add a version column. Read the row with its version, then write only if it is unchanged:

UPDATE doc SET body = ?, version = version + 1
WHERE id = 7 AND version = 3;   -- 0 rows updated means someone else got there first

Walk-through. Alice and Bob both open document v3. Alice saves: the version matches, it becomes v4. Bob saves with version 3: zero rows match, the app tells Bob "this changed, review and retry" (or merges automatically). No lock was held while they were typing.

Decision rule. Profile edits and document editing: optimistic (conflict chance is small, and you must never hold a lock while a human thinks). Selling the last 10 tickets or debiting a busy account: pessimistic, or better a single atomic conditional UPDATE ... WHERE qty > 0, because under heavy conflict optimistic retries pile up and waste work.

Costs and pitfalls. Pessimistic: blocked threads, deadlocks, a stalled lock holder freezing others. Optimistic: retry logic, possible starvation of one writer under heavy conflict, and you must remember to include the version in every writer, including batch jobs.

Soft delete vs hard delete? Intermediate

Say this: "Soft delete keeps the row and marks it deleted, which allows undo and audit but needs filtering everywhere and a purge job. Hard delete really removes it. I soft delete things users expect to restore and hard delete personal data on request, with a retention period in between."

What each looks like. Soft: UPDATE orders SET deleted_at = now() WHERE id = 5, and every query adds WHERE deleted_at IS NULL. Hard: DELETE FROM orders WHERE id = 5.

Why soft delete is attractive. "Restore from trash" works, auditors can see history, and foreign keys from other tables keep pointing at a real row (an old invoice still shows its customer).

What goes wrong with it.

  • Someone forgets the filter once and deleted data leaks to a screen or an export. Use database views or ORM-level default filters to make it hard to forget.
  • Unique constraints break: a user deletes their account and cannot re-register the same email because the soft-deleted row still holds it. Fix with a partial unique index WHERE deleted_at IS NULL.
  • Tables and indexes grow forever, slowing queries.
  • Privacy law (GDPR right to erasure) may require the data to really disappear.

Worked policy. Documents: soft delete, purge after 30 days. Account personal data: on a deletion request, anonymise or hard delete within the legal window, keeping only non-identifying records (amounts for tax). Log and session tables: hard delete by retention.

Cost: the purge job, extra filter on every query, and backups that still hold deleted data. Be ready to explain to compliance how long backups retain it and that restoring a backup must re-apply pending deletions.

UUID vs auto-increment IDs? Intermediate

Say this: "Auto-increment for one database and internal ids. Time-ordered distributed ids (UUIDv7, ULID, Snowflake) when many writers, sharding or ids in public URLs are involved. I avoid random UUIDv4 as the clustered key of a large table."

The idea. Every row needs a unique id. Auto-increment lets the single database hand out 1, 2, 3... A UUID is a 128-bit identifier any machine can create alone with practically no collision chance. A time-ordered id puts a timestamp in the high bits, so ids sort roughly by creation time.

Why index locality matters. Most databases store a primary-key index as a B-tree. Sequential ids always insert at the right-hand edge of the tree: pages fill up and the hot page stays in memory. Random UUIDs insert at random positions, splitting pages all over the tree, evicting cached pages and bloating the index. On a big MySQL InnoDB table (where the primary key is the clustered data order) this can visibly slow inserts.

Worked scenario. You expose /orders/1042. A competitor loops through 1043, 1044... and learns your daily order count, and a bug in authorisation becomes trivially exploitable. A random or time-ordered public id removes that. Common pattern: keep a compact internal bigint key for joins and indexes, plus a separate public UUID column with a unique index.

Cost of distributed ids: bigger keys and indexes; Snowflake-style ids need worker-id assignment and sane clocks (clock going backwards can produce duplicates unless handled). Time-ordered ids reveal creation time, which may matter for privacy.

Event sourcing vs plain CRUD: when is event sourcing worth it? Senior

Say this: "CRUD stores current state and overwrites it. Event sourcing stores the immutable list of events and derives state by replaying them. I would not start with it unless the history itself is a core requirement; an audit table gets most of the value at a fraction of the complexity."

How it works, with an account example.

CRUD:           accounts(id=7, balance=130)                 -- history is gone
Event sourced:  7: Opened 100 | Deposited 50 | Withdrew 20   -- state = fold of events = 130

To read the current state you replay the events for that entity. Replaying thousands every time is slow, so you store periodic snapshots (balance 130 as of event 3) and replay only later events. Other views (a "monthly statement" table, a search index) are projections built by consuming the event stream; you can rebuild or add a new projection by replaying history, which is a major benefit.

Choose it when history is the product or the requirement: ledgers, audit trails with legal weight, "show the state as of last Tuesday", undo/redo, debugging by replaying production, or many different read models from the same facts. Otherwise choose CRUD: most apps do not need it.

Costs.

  • A steep learning curve for the team.
  • Event schema evolution forever: events recorded in 2024 must remain readable in 2030, so you need upcasters or versioned events.
  • Read models are eventually consistent, so "write then read" needs care.
  • Ad hoc queries are harder (you query projections, not the log).
  • GDPR deletion conflicts with immutability; one approach is crypto-shredding (encrypt per-user data and delete the key).

Cheaper alternatives. CRUD plus an append-only audit table, or change data capture from the database log, gives history without making events the source of truth. Also keep in mind event sourcing is not the same as using Kafka or CQRS; they are related but separable.

Say: "I would not start with event sourcing unless the audit history is a core requirement. An audit log table gets most of the value at a fraction of the complexity."
Vertical scaling vs sharding: when do you shard? Senior

Say this: "Scale up first, then add read replicas and caching, and shard last. Sharding is a one-way door that changes every query, transaction and migration. I shard only when a ceiling remains that scaling up and replicas cannot fix, mainly write throughput or data size."

The terms. Vertical scaling means a bigger machine (more CPU, RAM, faster disks). Sharding means splitting data across several independent databases, each owning part of the key space (for example user ids 0 to 9 million on shard 1, the next range on shard 2).

Why not shard early. A single modern machine can have hundreds of GB of RAM and tens of TB of NVMe storage and serves a surprising load. Once sharded:

  • A query across shards ("all orders this month") must be sent to every shard and merged.
  • A transaction spanning two shards needs two-phase commit or a saga.
  • Joins between sharded tables are limited or impossible.
  • Schema changes and reports touch N databases.
  • Resharding later, when one shard fills, is a major project.

The ladder, cheapest and most reversible first.

  1. Fix queries and indexes.
  2. Connection pooling.
  3. Cache hot reads.
  4. Read replicas for read load.
  5. Scale up the primary.
  6. Archive or partition old data inside one database.
  7. Shard (or move one workload to a distributed store).

When to shard. Write throughput beyond one primary's capacity; a dataset too large for one node plus its backup and restore time (restoring 30 TB can take a day); or the price of the biggest instance rising steeply. Pick the shard key carefully: it should spread load evenly and keep most queries within one shard (for example tenant_id in a B2B product). A poor key (like creation date, where all new writes hit the newest shard) creates a hot shard.

Cost of vertical: a single point of failure, resize downtime, and a hard ceiling. Cost of sharding: everything above. Consider managed distributed SQL (Spanner, CockroachDB, Vitess for MySQL) before hand-rolling sharding in application code.

Data flow and compute trade-offs

Push vs pull: how do you decide? Intermediate

Say this: "Push when updates are rare and consumers need them fast; pull when consumers are many, slow or unpredictable, or should control their own pace. Pull gives natural back pressure, which is why Kafka consumers pull."

What they mean. In push the producer sends data to the consumer the moment something changes (a webhook, a mobile push notification, writing a post into followers' feeds). In pull the consumer asks when it wants data (polling an API, a feed assembled when you open the app, a Kafka consumer calling poll).

Why the choice matters: who is in charge of the pace. With push, a fast producer can bury a slow consumer. Imagine a producer sending 5,000 events per second to a service that can process 1,000. With push the consumer's memory fills and it crashes unless you add buffers and retries. With pull, the consumer reads at 1,000 per second, a backlog grows in the log (which is cheap and visible as "lag"), and nothing breaks.

Fan-out on write vs fan-out on read? Senior

Say this: "It is a choice of where to spend work. Fan-out on write copies each new post into every follower's timeline, so reads are cheap but a celebrity causes a write storm. Fan-out on read assembles the feed when you open it, so writes are cheap but reads are slow. For feeds, reads dominate, so I precompute for normal accounts and compute on read for the huge ones, then merge. That hybrid is what large social networks have described publicly."

How to read this diagram. A new post enters on the left and is routed by author size. The top box (normal author) copies the post id into every follower's timeline when the post is written. The bottom box (celebrity author) stores the post once and does no copying. On the right, a reader's feed request combines the precomputed list with the celebrity posts fetched at read time, then merges them. The moving dashes show the post travelling in, then the two sources flowing into the reader's feed.

Worked numbers. A normal user has 200 followers. A post means 200 small writes, trivial. A celebrity has 20 million followers. One post means 20 million timeline writes, which can take minutes and floods your cache. With fan-out on read, opening the app by someone following 500 accounts means up to 500 lookups of recent posts plus a merge, every time, which is slow and expensive at 100,000 opens per second.

The hybrid, step by step. (1) Set a follower threshold (say 100,000, tuned from data). (2) Below it, a worker pushes the post id into each follower's timeline list (store ids only, not whole posts). Synchronous vs asynchronous processing: how do you choose? Basic

Say this: "Do synchronously only what the caller must know before continuing. Everything else (emails, thumbnails, analytics, search indexing) goes through a queue. The cost of async is duplicates, ordering questions and no immediate result."

The difference. Synchronous: the caller waits for the whole operation and gets the final result. Asynchronous: the caller hands the work to a queue, gets an acknowledgement ("accepted"), and the work finishes later.

Worked example: placing an order.

  1. Synchronous, in the request: validate the cart, charge the card, reserve stock, write the order. The user needs a definite yes or no.
  2. Publish an OrderPlaced event to a queue and return "order confirmed".
  3. Asynchronously, consumers send the confirmation email, update recommendations, notify the warehouse, update analytics.

Response time is now the sum of the critical steps only. If the email provider is down or slow, checkout is unaffected, and the email retries later.

What you pay for async.

  • The user cannot see the final result at once. You need a status endpoint, polling, or a push notification (a video upload says "processing").
  • At-least-once delivery means the same message can arrive twice, so consumers must be idempotent (processing it twice has the same effect as once).
  • Ordering is limited (usually per key or partition only).
  • Failures need a retry policy and a dead-letter queue for poison messages.
  • Debugging a flow across a queue needs trace ids propagated in messages.

Pitfall: making a call async because it sounds scalable. If the user needs the answer to continue (is this username free?), forcing it through a queue only adds delay and complexity.

Queue vs direct call between services? Intermediate

Say this: "A direct call when I need the answer now; a queue when the work can wait, bursts are expected, or several consumers react to one event. A direct call with timeouts, backoff retries and a circuit breaker is often enough and much simpler."

Direct call (HTTP or RPC). Service A calls B and waits. Simple, immediate result, easy tracing. The price is coupling in time: if B is down, A fails; if B is slow, A's threads pile up waiting and A becomes slow too, spreading failure upward.

Queue (or log). A writes a message to a broker and moves on. B reads it whenever it is able to.

  • B can be down for ten minutes and nothing is lost; messages wait.
  • A traffic burst is absorbed: 10,000 messages arrive in a second, B drains them at 500 per second.
  • Adding a new consumer (say fraud analysis) needs no change to A.

Worked comparison. Price lookup during checkout: direct call, you need the number right now. "Resize this uploaded image": queue; the user does not need the thumbnail in the response, and uploads spike during campaigns.

Cost of the queue: one more piece of infrastructure to run and secure; duplicates (design idempotent consumers); no immediate result; ordering and poison-message handling; and a new thing to monitor. The key alerts are queue depth (how many waiting) and message age (how long the oldest waited), since a growing backlog means consumers cannot keep up.

Decision rule: if the caller's next step depends on the result, call directly (and protect it). If not, and you want resilience or fan-out, queue it. Do not add a queue only because you fear failure; first ask whether timeouts and retries would solve it.

Batch vs stream processing? Intermediate

Say this: "I ask what an hour-old answer costs. If nobody acts within hours, batch: it is simple, cheap and easy to re-run. If a decision loses value in seconds, like fraud detection, stream. Micro-batches every few minutes are a cheap middle."

What they are. Batch processes a bounded chunk of data at a scheduled time (nightly billing run, daily revenue report). Stream processes events one by one or in tiny windows as they arrive, giving seconds of latency.

Worked example. A card is used in London, and 8 seconds later in Singapore. A nightly batch job would flag it after the money has gone. A stream processor keeps per-card state ("last location and time") and flags the second use immediately. Meanwhile the monthly statement is correctly a batch job: it needs complete data and runs once.

BatchStream
LatencyMinutes to hoursMilliseconds to seconds
CorrectnessExact; data is complete at run timeMust handle late and out-of-order events
Re-runningEasy, just run againNeeds replayable logs and care with state
OperationsRuns, then stopsAlways on, state to checkpoint and manage

Why stream is harder. An event timestamped 12:00:05 may arrive at 12:00:20 after one stamped 12:00:15. The engine must decide how long to wait (a watermark) before closing a window, and what to do with stragglers. Exactly-once results require checkpointed state and idempotent or transactional sinks.

Cost: complexity and always-on infrastructure. The old lambda architecture (separate batch and stream paths computing the same thing) doubles the logic and invites disagreement; prefer a single engine that does both when you can. Many teams get 90 percent of the value from a batch job every five minutes.

Cache vs recompute: when is a cache worth it? Intermediate

Say this: "Cache when the work is expensive, results are reused many times, and some staleness is tolerable. Do not cache unique, fast-changing or must-be-exact data. The cache must never be needed for correctness, and I would measure the hit rate to prove it is worth the complexity."

Why a cache pays. Suppose a product page costs 80 ms of database work and is viewed 10,000 times per minute, but changes hourly. Recomputing means 800 seconds of database time per minute (it cannot even keep up). Cached with a 60-second TTL, you do the work about once a minute and serve the rest from memory in 1 ms.

When it does not pay. A per-user bank balance (unique, must be exact), or a query whose parameters almost never repeat (hit rate near zero), or something that is already 2 ms to compute.

The costs.

  • Invalidation: knowing when the cached copy is wrong. Simplest is a TTL (accept staleness up to N seconds); better is deleting the key when the source changes.
  • Stale reads that confuse users or support.
  • Cold start and stampede: after a restart or expiry of a popular key, thousands of requests miss at once and hit the database together. Mitigate with request coalescing (one fetch, others wait), jittered TTLs, and pre-warming.
  • Memory cost and one more component that can fail.

Rule of thumb for the interview: estimate the hit rate. At 90 percent, the database sees one tenth of the load, usually a big win. At 30 percent you carry the complexity for little gain. And the system should still work, only slower, if the cache is empty or down; if it falls over without it, you built a hidden single point of failure.

Precompute vs compute on demand? Intermediate

Say this: "Precompute when the query is expensive, asked often, and its inputs change less often than it is asked. Compute on demand when the space of possible questions is huge or data must be exact. Often the best answer is a hybrid: precompute the common cases, compute the long tail lazily and cache it."

What precomputing means. Doing the work ahead of time and storing the answer: materialized views, leaderboards, recommendation lists, search indexes, daily aggregate tables. It trades storage and write-time work for fast, cheap reads.

Worked example: a sales dashboard. "Revenue per day per region for the last 90 days" over 2 billion order rows takes 40 seconds if computed on demand and is asked by 300 managers each morning. Precompute a daily_revenue(region, day, total) table every hour: the dashboard loads in 20 ms, costs one scan an hour instead of 300 an hour, and is at most an hour stale.

When on-demand is better. Analysts can filter by 15 dimensions in any combination: there are millions of possible aggregates and most will never be requested, so precomputing all of them wastes storage and refresh time. Also when the user needs data that is exact to the second.

Cost: a refresh pipeline to build and monitor, staleness, work wasted on results nobody reads, and a backfill plan when the definition changes. Hybrid: precompute the top few common aggregates, compute rarer ones on demand and cache them (this is how many analytics systems and OLAP engines with rollups work). Decide by comparing: how often is it read versus how often does the input change, and how costly is one computation.

The underlying problem. HTTP is request-response: the client asks, the server answers. "Tell me when something happens" needs a trick. These five options are different tricks, with different costs.

REST vs gRPC? Intermediate

Say this: "REST over JSON at the edge and for external consumers, because it works everywhere and caches well. gRPC internally when throughput, latency or streaming matter and I control both ends. Many systems use both."

What each is. REST exposes resources over HTTP (GET /orders/42) usually with JSON, human readable and testable from a browser or curl. gRPC is a remote-procedure-call framework: you describe services and messages in a .proto file, tools generate typed client and server code, and calls travel as compact binary Protocol Buffers over HTTP/2.

service Orders { rpc GetOrder (OrderId) returns (Order); }
message OrderId { int64 id = 1; }

Why gRPC is faster, honestly. Binary encoding gives smaller payloads and cheaper parsing; HTTP/2 multiplexes many calls on one connection; generated clients remove hand-written glue and catch contract mistakes at compile time; streaming (server, client or both) is built in. For a service making 50,000 internal calls per second the difference in CPU and bandwidth is real.

GraphQL vs REST? Intermediate

Say this: "GraphQL lets each client ask for exactly the fields it needs in one request, which helps when several frontends with different needs consume a rich data graph. REST is simpler, cache-friendly and easier to rate limit, so it wins for simple or public APIs."

The problem GraphQL solves. With REST, a mobile screen showing a user, their last 3 orders and each order's items may need three or four round trips (/users/1, /users/1/orders, /orders/9/items...), or one fat endpoint that returns far more than the screen needs (over-fetching), wasteful on slow mobile networks.

How GraphQL answers it. A single endpoint takes a query shaped like the response:

{ user(id: 1) { name orders(last: 3) { id items { title } } } }

The server returns exactly those fields. The web app can ask for more, the mobile app for less, without new endpoints.

What it costs, specifically.

  • N+1 queries on the server: resolving 3 orders then each order's items one by one gives 1 + 3 database calls (or 1 + 100). Fix with batching, such as DataLoader, which collects ids and issues one query.
  • Caching: queries are usually POSTs to one URL, so CDNs and browser caches do not help by default; use persisted queries or client-side normalised caches.
  • Abuse: a client can send a deeply nested expensive query. You need depth limits, cost analysis and timeouts.
  • Authorisation per field rather than per endpoint, which is easy to get wrong.
  • Error handling and monitoring are less uniform: HTTP 200 can carry errors.

Rule: GraphQL for many differing clients over connected data; REST for simple, public, cacheable APIs. A backend-for-frontend (a small server per client type that composes REST calls) is another way to give each client its shape without adopting GraphQL.

What design choices make an API idempotent, and why does it matter? Senior

Say this: "Networks time out, and a client that got no response cannot tell whether the server processed the request, so it retries. Without idempotency a retried payment charges twice. I use idempotent verbs where possible, and an idempotency key stored together with the result for everything else."

What idempotent means. Doing an operation twice has the same effect as doing it once. "Set the price to 10" is idempotent; "add 10 to the balance" is not.

The failure scenario. Client sends POST /payments. The server charges the card, but the response is lost in the network. The client times out and retries. Without protection, the customer is charged twice.

Techniques.

  • Naturally idempotent verbs: PUT (replace with this state) and DELETE. Prefer "set to state X" over "increment" where practical.
  • Idempotency key (the Stripe pattern): the client generates a UUID and sends it as a header. The server flow:
  1. Look up the key. If found with a stored result, return that stored result and do nothing.
  2. If not found, insert the key as "in progress" (a unique insert, so a concurrent duplicate blocks or gets a conflict).
  3. Do the work, then save the result against the key in the same transaction as the effect.
  4. Expire keys after a window such as 24 hours.
  • Natural unique constraint: a unique index on (order_id, attempt) so the database itself rejects a duplicate insert.
  • Conditional update: ETag or version check (If-Match) so a stale retry fails.

Edge cases that interviewers probe. Two simultaneous requests with the same key (the unique insert makes one wait or return 409). The same key with a different body (reject with an error, since it signals a client bug). A crash between doing the work and saving the key (hence the same transaction; if the effect is an external call, pass the key to the downstream provider too).

Cost: key storage, expiry, and extra latency of the lookup. The same applies to queue consumers, because delivery is at-least-once: dedupe on a message id or make the handler naturally idempotent.

JWT vs server-side sessions? Intermediate

Say this: "For a single web application I use server-side sessions in a cookie: simplest and revocable instantly. For many services or third-party clients I use short-lived JWT access tokens plus a revocable refresh token kept server-side."

Server session. After login the server creates a random session id, stores the user's state in Redis or a database, and sends the id in an HttpOnly cookie. Each request: look up the id. Logging out or banning a user means deleting the record, effective immediately. The cookie itself reveals nothing. The cost is a store lookup per request and a highly available session store.

JWT (JSON Web Token). A signed token containing claims (user id, roles, expiry). Any service with the public key can verify it locally with no lookup, which suits many microservices and cross-domain APIs. The catch: it is valid until it expires. If a token is stolen or the user is banned, you cannot revoke it without keeping a deny-list, which brings back a lookup.

The practical pattern. Access token as JWT with a 5 to 15 minute lifetime; refresh token (opaque, stored server-side, rotated on use) to obtain new access tokens. A ban then takes effect within one access-token lifetime, and you can revoke the refresh token at once.

Costs and pitfalls. Key rotation and distribution (publish keys via a JWKS endpoint); never put sensitive or fast-changing data in claims (permissions can be stale for the token lifetime); accept only the algorithms you expect (a classic bug is accepting "none"); and avoid storing tokens where page scripts can read them. An HttpOnly, Secure, SameSite cookie is safer than local storage.

Rate limiting at the gateway vs inside the service? Intermediate

Say this: "Both, for different jobs. The gateway enforces coarse, cheap limits by identity and volume before traffic reaches my servers. The service applies cost-aware and resource-aware limits, because only it knows which operations are expensive."

What rate limiting is for. Protecting capacity from floods and bugs (a client stuck in a retry loop), enforcing fairness between tenants, and enforcing plan limits that you sell (1,000 requests per minute on the basic plan).

At the gateway. It sees every request first. Limits per API key, per IP and per tenant plan are cheap to compute and need no business knowledge. Putting them here keeps overload from ever reaching your fleet, and keeps the logic in one place.

Inside the service. The gateway cannot tell that POST /export costs 1,000 times more than GET /ping. The service can limit by operation (3 exports per hour), by concurrent jobs per tenant, or protect a fragile downstream dependency with a bulkhead (at most 20 concurrent calls to the legacy system).

How a limiter typically works. A token bucket: a bucket holds up to N tokens and refills at R per second; each request takes one token, and if the bucket is empty the request is rejected. This allows short bursts yet enforces an average rate. In a fleet, counters are kept in a shared store such as Redis (an atomic increment with expiry), or approximated locally per node.

Failure behaviour. If the shared counter store is down, do you fail open (allow traffic) or closed (block)? Fail open is usual for availability, with local in-memory limits as a backstop; fail closed only for abuse-sensitive endpoints such as login. The extra Redis call in the hot path costs about a millisecond and adds a dependency.

Response: HTTP 429 Too Many Requests with a Retry-After header, so well-behaved clients back off. Pitfall: limiting by IP alone penalises many users behind one corporate NAT, and per-user limits are bypassed by attackers who rotate accounts; combine signals.

Architecture, ownership and operations trade-offs

Monolith vs microservices for a small team? Senior

Say this: "I start with a well-structured modular monolith and split a service out only when a concrete pain justifies it: independent scaling, a team blocked by shared deploys, different compliance needs, or a different technology fit. Microservices mostly solve an organisational problem, many teams deploying independently."

The terms. A monolith is one deployable application; a modular monolith keeps it organised into modules with enforced boundaries (each owning its own tables and exposing a narrow interface). Microservices are many small, independently deployable services, each owning its data, talking over the network.

What a five-person team pays for four services.

  • Calls that were function calls become network calls: they can time out, fail partially and add latency.
  • Distributed tracing, per-service pipelines, dashboards, alerts and on-call.
  • Versioned contracts: changing a field now needs coordinated releases.
  • No cross-service transaction: what was one SQL transaction becomes a saga.
  • Local development needs several things running.

The benefit, independent deploys and scaling, mostly appears when many teams would otherwise step on each other. Several well-known companies have publicly described consolidating services back after finding the overhead outweighed the gains.

Worked example. An e-commerce backend with orders, catalogue, payments and notifications, built as one app with four packages. Each package owns its tables and exposes an interface; other packages may not touch its tables. Later, if the image-processing path needs 20 times the CPU, that module is extracted. Because the boundary already existed, the extraction takes weeks, not months.

Cost of the monolith: one deployable (a bad change affects all of it), builds lengthen as it grows, and boundaries erode unless enforced (use architecture tests or module systems). Cost of microservices: all above plus the distributed monolith trap, where services must deploy together and call each other in long chains, so you get all the cost and none of the independence.

Say: "I would build a modular monolith and extract the first service when a specific team or scaling need makes the split pay for itself."
What signals tell you it is time to split a service out of a monolith? Senior

Say this: "Concrete pain, not fashion: very different scaling needs, teams blocking each other, a component with different availability or security requirements, a better-fitting runtime, or build times that stay painful after normal optimisation. I extract along data ownership lines using the strangler pattern."

The signals, with what each looks like.

  • Uneven scaling. Image processing needs 20 times the CPU of everything else. Scaling the whole monolith 20 times to feed one hot path wastes money; a separate service scales alone.
  • Teams block each other. Merge conflicts, a shared release train, "we cannot ship because their change is not ready". This is the classic, strongest reason, because it is an organisational bottleneck.
  • Different reliability or compliance needs. Payment code isolated to shrink the audit scope (for example card-data rules), or a component that must stay up when the rest is being deployed.
  • Different runtime. A Python machine-learning model next to a Java application.
  • Build and test times that are painful even after caching and parallelising.

Counter-signals. "It would be cleaner", "everyone does it", or "we might scale later" are not reasons. Cleanliness inside the codebase is achieved with modules, not with a network.

How to extract safely, in steps.

  1. First make the boundary clean inside the monolith: a narrow interface, and the component's tables used only through it.
  2. Stand up the new service with its own database (copy and keep in sync by events or change data capture during transition).
  3. Route a small percentage of calls to it (strangler pattern), compare results, then ramp up.
  4. Remove the old code and the old tables once confident.

Pitfall: extracting a service that shares tables with the monolith leaves hidden coupling. If the split requires many joins across the boundary, the boundary is in the wrong place.

Build vs buy: how do you decide? Senior

Say this: "Build what differentiates the business; buy or adopt open source for commodity. I count the full cost, including maintenance, on-call and the opportunity cost of the engineers, and I check the exit cost before committing to a vendor."

Why this is a trade-off at all. Building looks free because the engineers are already paid. It is not: those engineers could be building features customers pay for. Buying looks expensive because the invoice is visible, but it often costs less than the hidden expense of building and running the same thing.

Rule of thumb by category. Authentication, payments, email delivery, search, observability, feature flags: usually buy. Your core ranking, pricing or recommendation logic, if it is what customers choose you for: build.

A cost comparison, in numbers (illustrative). Suppose a vendor charges 4,000 per month for feature flags. Building a basic version takes one engineer 8 weeks, then maintenance of roughly 20 to 30 percent of build effort per year, plus on-call and security patches, plus a UI that product managers will ask for. At a loaded engineer cost of, say, 15,000 per month the build costs about 30,000 up front plus around 10,000 per year, for a weaker product. Buying wins until usage grows enough that the bill dominates.

Method.

  1. List requirements, split into must-have and nice-to-have.
  2. Estimate 3-year total cost for both (licence growth with usage; build, run and opportunity costs).
  3. Check exit cost: how hard is it to leave the vendor (data export, API shape, how much your code depends on their quirks)? Wrap the vendor behind your own interface.
  4. Run a time-boxed proof of concept with your real workload.
  5. Set a review date: a bill that was trivial at 1 million events can dominate at 10 billion.

Risks of buying: lock-in, price rises, vendor outage or shutdown, limited customisation, security and data-residency review. Risks of building: delays, a permanent maintenance burden, and bus factor (only one engineer understands it).

Managed service vs self-hosted? Intermediate

Say this: "A small team should almost always use managed services for stateful systems, because an engineer-week of operations costs more than the price premium. I self-host when scale makes the premium large, when I need features the managed offering lacks, or for strict sovereignty reasons, and only with people who have the skills."

What you get from managed (Amazon RDS, managed Kafka, hosted Elasticsearch): automated backups and point-in-time recovery, patching, failover, monitoring and easy scaling. Creating a replicated Postgres with backups takes minutes, not days.

What it costs. A price premium over raw compute (often noticeable at scale), limited tuning (you may not get superuser, kernel settings or some extensions), versions that lag, some lock-in, and sometimes less visibility when something is wrong.

What self-hosting gives. Full control, custom configuration, potentially much lower cost at large scale, and portability. In return you own upgrades, failure recovery, capacity planning, security hardening, backup verification and the 3 a.m. pager.

Worked example. A team of six needs Kafka. Running it themselves means learning broker sizing, partition rebalancing, ZooKeeper or KRaft quorum, rolling upgrades and disk-full incidents. A managed offering costs, say, 30 percent more than raw instances, but frees about one engineer-week a month. Unless the bill reaches a size that justifies a dedicated platform person, managed wins.

Decision triggers to self-host: the premium exceeds the cost of a platform team's time; a required feature or version is unavailable; or regulation requires you to control the hardware or region.

Cost to remember: managed does not remove responsibility. You still own schema design, capacity limits, query performance, cost control, and tested restores (a backup you have never restored is a hope, not a backup).

Single region vs multi-region? Senior

Say this: "Multi-region buys survival of a whole-region outage and lower latency for distant users, at a large price in complexity and money. I go multi-AZ first, derive the need for a second region from RTO, RPO and the business cost of downtime, and escalate one step at a time."

Terms. A region is a geographic cloud area (for example Frankfurt); an availability zone (AZ) is an isolated data centre inside it. Multi-AZ protects you from a data-centre failure and costs little. Multi-region protects from a region-wide failure and costs much more.

What multi-region costs. Data must be replicated across regions, and cross-region latency is typically tens to over a hundred milliseconds, so synchronous replication hurts. If both regions accept writes you face conflicts to resolve. Infrastructure roughly doubles; deployments, configuration and testing become much more complex; and failover must actually be rehearsed.

How to read this diagram. Read left to right as a ladder of disaster-recovery options. Multi-AZ survives a data-centre failure and is the default. A backup in another region survives a regional loss but takes hours to restore. A warm standby recovers in minutes. Active-active, with both regions serving traffic, recovers fastest and is the hardest and costliest. Each step right lowers RTO and RPO but adds cost and complexity.

How to decide. Ask the business: what is an hour of downtime worth, what RTO (time to recover) and RPO (data you can lose) are needed? Then compare with how often whole regions fail versus how often you cause outages with bad deploys. Most outages are self-inflicted, which multi-region does not fix; better deploy safety (canaries, rollbacks) often reduces risk more per dollar.

Hidden traps. The control plane must not be a single-region dependency: DNS, authentication, configuration and the deployment system need to work from the surviving region. Several large cloud outages hurt multi-region customers because of exactly this. Also test failover regularly; an untested runbook fails.

Other reasons for a second region: data residency laws (EU data in the EU) and latency for far-away users, which can be served by CDN and read replicas without full active-active writes.

How do you explain RTO and RPO when choosing a disaster-recovery strategy? Intermediate

Say this: "RPO is how much data I can afford to lose, as time; RTO is how long I can be down. Tighter targets cost much more, so I get the business to put a price on downtime and pick the cheapest strategy that meets both numbers."

Definitions with a timeline. Say the disaster hits at 12:00, and the last good backup was 11:00. If service returns at 14:00 with data as of 11:00, then RTO = 2 hours (disaster to recovery) and RPO = 1 hour (disaster back to the last recoverable point). RPO is set by how often you copy data (backup frequency or replication lag); RTO by how fast you can bring up compute, restore data and redirect traffic.

StrategyTypical RTOTypical RPOCost
Backup and restoreHoursHours (last backup)Low
Pilot light / warm standbyTens of minutesMinutesMedium
Active-activeNear zeroNear zeroHigh

Figures are indicative. In a pilot light only the data layer runs in the second region and the rest is scaled up on demand; warm standby runs a scaled-down full copy.

Why tight targets are expensive. RPO near zero needs synchronous replication, so every write waits for the remote copy (latency) and a replica outage can block writes. RTO of seconds needs a hot standby with automated failover, health checks that do not flap, and rehearsed runbooks.

How to pick. Ask: what does one hour of downtime cost, and what does losing 10 minutes of data cost? An internal reporting tool might tolerate RTO of a day; a payment gateway might need minutes and near-zero loss. Price each strategy and compare with the loss. Pitfalls: untested backups do not count, so schedule restore drills and time them; and remember dependencies (if your database recovers in 10 minutes but the secrets store takes an hour, the RTO is an hour).

One API gateway, or a backend-for-frontend per client? Intermediate

Say this: "Start with one gateway for cross-cutting concerns. Add a backend-for-frontend per client type only when clients genuinely need different payloads and the shared API is getting twisted by client-specific conditions. Keep BFFs thin."

The two roles. An API gateway is the single front door: it terminates TLS, authenticates, rate limits and routes to services, all the concerns every client shares. A backend-for-frontend (BFF) is a small server owned by one client team (web, mobile, partners) that calls several backend services and composes exactly the response that client needs.

Why a BFF appears. The mobile home screen needs a trimmed summary built from four services. The web page needs a richer one. Without a BFF, the shared API sprouts flags (?client=mobile&fields=...) and branching, or mobile makes four slow round trips. A BFF lets the mobile team shape one tailored call and change it without negotiating with everyone.

Example flow. Mobile app calls GET /home on the mobile BFF. The BFF calls profile, orders and recommendations services in parallel, trims fields, and returns one compact JSON. The gateway in front handled auth and rate limits.

Cost. Each BFF is another service to own, deploy and monitor. The danger is business logic creeping into it, which then duplicates across BFFs and drifts. Rule: BFFs compose and adapt; domain rules stay in domain services. If you have only one client type, you do not need one. GraphQL is an alternative that gives each client its own shape from a single server.

Should microservices share a database? Intermediate

Say this: "By default no. A shared schema couples services invisibly. Each service owns its data and others use its API or consume its events. A shared database is acceptable only as a temporary step during a migration or as a read-only reporting replica, with a plan to remove it."

The problem. Service A and service B both read and write the customer table. Team A renames a column, and B breaks in production, because the contract was never written down: it was a table. You also cannot deploy, scale or choose a different database technology for A without B. The whole point of microservices, independent change, is gone.

What "own your data" looks like.

  • Only service A touches A's tables. B asks A through an API ("get customer 7"), or keeps its own copy of the few fields it needs, updated by events (CustomerRenamed).
  • The service's schema is private; the API and events are the public contract, versioned deliberately.

What you give up. No cross-service joins (compose in code or in a read model), no cross-service transactions (use sagas), and reporting needs a separate pipeline (events or CDC into a warehouse). Duplicated data can go stale; accept bounded staleness and name an owner for each fact.

Pragmatic exceptions. During a monolith split, both sides may share tables temporarily while data moves. A read replica for analytics is fine because nothing writes to it. One database server hosting separate schemas per service is also fine, as long as services cannot reach each other's schemas (separate credentials), because it keeps logical ownership while saving cost.

Probe: "How do you do a report that joins orders and customers?" Answer: stream both to an analytics store, join there.

Distributed transaction (two-phase commit) vs saga? Senior

Say this: "Inside one database, use a normal transaction. Across services, use a saga: a sequence of local transactions with compensating actions if a later step fails. Two-phase commit gives atomicity but blocks on coordinator failure, holds locks across the network, and many services and queues do not support it."

The problem. Booking a trip touches three services: reserve flight, reserve hotel, charge card. Each has its own database, so no single transaction can cover them all. If the card is declined after the flight and hotel were reserved, you must undo them.

Two-phase commit (2PC). A coordinator asks every participant "can you commit?" (prepare), and if all say yes, tells them all to commit. Participants hold locks while waiting. If the coordinator dies after prepare, participants are stuck holding locks until it recovers. It is also slow (several round trips) and needs every resource to support it.

Saga. Each step commits locally and has a compensating step that semantically undoes it:

1. ReserveFlight   (compensate: CancelFlight)
2. ReserveHotel    (compensate: CancelHotel)
3. ChargeCard      (compensate: Refund)
Charge fails -> run CancelHotel, then CancelFlight

Two styles. Choreography: each service reacts to events from the previous one; no central brain, good for short, simple flows, but hard to see the whole flow. Orchestration: a coordinator (a workflow engine or state machine) tells each service what to do next and tracks state; better for many steps, timeouts and visibility.

Costs you must accept. The system is temporarily inconsistent (the flight is reserved before payment). Compensations are business actions that must not fail permanently (a refund needs retries and an alert if stuck). Steps must be idempotent since messages repeat. Some actions cannot be undone (an email sent), so order steps so the irreversible ones come last. Use the outbox pattern so a step's database write and its event publish cannot diverge.

Rule: if you can keep the data in one database, do; a local ACID transaction is far simpler than any saga.

How do you decide whether to take on or pay down tech debt? Senior

Say this: "Debt is a loan. Taking it is fine if I know the interest and have a repayment trigger. I take it deliberately where speed has a deadline value, never in areas where interest compounds: security, the data model and public API contracts. I repay by interest per cost, mostly in code I am touching anyway."

What debt is. A shortcut that makes today faster and tomorrow slower: skipped tests, hard-coded config, a duplicated module, an outdated library. The slowdown it adds each month is the interest; the effort to fix is the principal.

Taking it on well. Write it down: what shortcut, why, the trigger to repay ("when we pass 10 tenants" or "before the Q3 launch"), and an owner. Good reasons: a launch tied to a contract, or an experiment that may be thrown away. Bad: "we are busy", with no record.

Where not to borrow. Debt in the data model (a wrong key or tenancy design), public API contracts and security is expensive to unwind, because other systems and stored data depend on it. Debt in an internal admin screen is cheap.

Prioritising repayment, with a worked example. Score each item as interest divided by cost to fix. Item A: a flaky test suite costs 6 engineers about 2 hours a week each, 12 hours per week, and takes 4 days (32 hours) to fix. Payback in under 3 weeks: do it. Item B: renaming a poorly named module costs nothing ongoing and takes 3 weeks: skip it, or do it opportunistically.

  • Fix debt in code you are about to change anyway (the boy-scout rule).
  • Reserve a standing slice of capacity (10 to 20 percent is a common guide).
  • Tie large items to outcomes: "this migration cuts deploys from 2 hours to 15 minutes and removes 3 incidents a quarter".

Pitfall: the big-bang cleanup project that delivers nothing visible for months and is cancelled; prefer incremental, measurable steps.

How do you decide whether to rewrite a system or refactor it? Senior

Say this: "My default is not to rewrite. Rewrites take longer than estimated and the old code embeds years of edge-case knowledge. I prefer incremental replacement with the strangler fig pattern: route one capability at a time to the new implementation and keep a rollback."

Why rewrites fail. The old system keeps getting features and bug fixes while you build the new one, so you chase a moving target. The new one must reproduce all the quiet behaviour (that odd tax rule, that customer's special format) which nobody documented, and you rediscover each as a bug. Joel Spolsky's well-known essay calls the full rewrite one of the worst strategic mistakes a software company can make.

When a rewrite is justified. The technology is truly dead or unsupportable (no hires, no security patches); the architecture blocks a business requirement that cannot be reached incrementally; and the system is small enough to replace within a few months.

How to read this diagram. Clients always talk to the routing layer, never directly to either system. A per-feature flag decides whether a request goes to the old system (red arrow) or the new one (green, moving arrow). Over time you flip more flags, so the green path carries more capabilities and the old box shrinks until it gets no traffic and is switched off.

Strangler fig, step by step.

  1. Put a routing layer (gateway, proxy or feature flag) in front.
  2. Pick one slice (for example "invoice PDF generation") and build it in the new system.
  3. Run both in parallel: send a copy of traffic to the new one (shadow traffic) and compare outputs without affecting users.
  4. Shift a small percentage of real traffic, watch metrics, ramp up, keep a quick rollback.
  5. Repeat for the next slice. When the old system gets no traffic, switch it off (and actually do so; many migrations never finish this step).

Cost: you run two systems for a long time, need the routing seam, and may need data synchronisation (dual writes or change data capture) between them. The benefit: you deliver value every few weeks and can stop at any point.

How do you estimate cost versus benefit of a technical option? Intermediate

Say this: "I put rough numbers on both sides, compare payback time and reversibility, and state my assumptions out loud so the interviewer can challenge them."

The two sides.

  • Benefit: revenue protected or gained, incidents avoided (downtime hours times cost per hour), engineer hours saved per month, latency improvement times its effect on conversion, infrastructure savings.
  • Cost: build time (engineers times weeks), ongoing run cost (infrastructure plus on-call plus maintenance, often around 20 to 30 percent of build cost per year as a rough rule of thumb, not a law), migration risk and opportunity cost (what else those people could do).

Worked example: add a Redis cache. Cost: about two engineer-weeks plus a small monthly bill. Situation A: the database is at 85 percent CPU and the next instance size costs 3,000 more per month. If the cache removes 70 percent of read load, you avoid that upgrade (36,000 per year) and gain headroom; payback is a few weeks. Situation B: the database is at 15 percent CPU. The cache buys nothing now and adds invalidation bugs; do not build it. The same technology, opposite answers, decided purely by the numbers.

Compare options on three axes.

  1. Payback time (how soon the benefit covers the cost).
  2. Reversibility: prefer cheap, reversible moves, and demand stronger evidence for irreversible ones.
  3. Risk: what if the estimate is wrong by 2 times? Does the answer still hold?

Pitfall: counting only the build cost. Ongoing cost and the complexity tax (every new component adds failure modes and onboarding time) usually exceed the initial build over a few years. Also quantify uncertainty with a range, and say what measurement would settle it.

Simple solution vs scalable solution: how do you avoid both over-engineering and under-engineering? Senior

Say this: "I design for about 10 times current load, know what I would do for 100 times, and build only the first. I keep cheap seams for the next step, and I decide the irreversible things (data model, tenancy, IDs, API contracts) now."

Over-engineering is building for scale or flexibility you do not need: a startup with 200 users running Kafka, Kubernetes and sharded databases spends its runway on infrastructure instead of customers. Knuth's "premature optimization is the root of all evil" was about micro-optimising code, but the lesson applies to architecture.

Under-engineering is just as real: no indexes, no backups, a single instance with no failover for a paid product, or baked-in assumptions (one tenant, one currency, one language) that force a rewrite when the first enterprise customer arrives.

The test. Ask: "What specific number or requirement forces this complexity today, and what would it cost to add it later?"

Cheap to add later (wait)Costly to change later (decide now)
A cachePrimary key and ID strategy
A read replicaTenancy model (shared vs isolated data)
A queue for slow workPublic API contracts and event schemas
A CDNShard key, if you will shard
Horizontal scaling of stateless tiersTime zones, currency and money representation

Seams that keep options open. Stateless services (so you can add instances), a repository layer around the datastore, config instead of hard-coded values, versioned APIs, and migrations in version control. These cost almost nothing now and make the later 10x step days of work, not months.

Worked example. A new B2B product expects 50 customers in year one. Single Postgres with a replica and nightly backups is right. But put a tenant_id on every table from day one: retrofitting tenancy into a mature schema is a painful migration, while the column costs nothing.

Scaling and evolution

How does an architecture evolve as load grows 10x, then 100x? What breaks first at each stage? Senior

Say this: "I measure, find the current bottleneck, fix the cheapest thing, and repeat, because the bottleneck moves. Roughly: fix queries, cache, replicate reads, scale out the stateless tier, make slow work async, and only then partition data."

The narrative, starting from one app server and one database.

StageWhat breaks firstTypical fix
Baseline (hundreds of req/s)Missing indexes, slow queries, N+1 queriesProfile, add indexes, fix queries, connection pooling
10xDatabase CPU and connections; single app server is a single point of failure; slow static assetsLoad balancer plus stateless app servers, read replicas, caching hot reads, CDN, move slow work to a queue
30xReplication lag, cache hit-rate problems, one hot table, deployment frictionPartition large tables, better cache keys, separate read-heavy and write-heavy paths, split off the noisiest component
100xSingle write primary is the ceiling; hot keys; operational complexity; blast radius of one bad deployShard or move write-heavy data to a distributed store, async pipelines, multi-AZ then multi-region, cell-based architecture, rate limiting and load shedding, strong observability

Why this order. Each step is cheaper and more reversible than the next. An unindexed query that costs 2 seconds will not be helped by 10 more servers; it will just crash the database faster. A cache helps only if the hot data is a small fraction of the whole.

A worked trace. At 200 requests per second the p99 is 2 s. Profiling shows three queries take 60 percent of database time. Adding two indexes brings p99 to 600 ms. A cache for the catalogue cuts database load about 70 percent. Now the single app server saturates, so you add a load balancer and three stateless instances. Later the report endpoint blocks threads, so it moves to a queue. Only when the primary's write rate approaches its limit do you discuss sharding.

People limits. At 100x the organisation also has to be split into teams, which is when service boundaries start to pay off (Conway's law: the architecture mirrors the communication structure). Pitfall: describing the final architecture without the stages; interviewers want the order and the evidence for each move.

Your single-database app is slowing down as traffic grows. What do you do, in order? Senior

Say this: "Measure first, then work from the cheapest, most reversible fix to the most expensive: queries and indexes, pooling, caching and replicas, async writes, archiving, scaling up, and only last sharding."

  1. Measure: slow query log, top queries by total time (frequency times duration), connection count, CPU, lock waits, cache hit ratio, and whether the machine is bound by CPU, disk or memory.
  2. Fix the worst queries: add or correct indexes, remove N+1 patterns, paginate, select only needed columns. One bad query is often 50 percent of the load.
  3. Add a connection pooler so thousands of app threads do not each hold a database connection (each connection costs memory and context switching).
  4. Cache read-heavy, stable results, and send reporting and analytics to a read replica.
  5. Move heavy writes that need no immediate result to a queue and batch them.
  6. Archive or partition old data so the hot working set fits in memory.
  7. Scale up the instance if needed: cheap and quick.
  8. Only then consider sharding or a different store for the one workload that needs it.

Worked example. The orders page takes 3 s. The slow log shows SELECT * FROM orders WHERE customer_id = ? ORDER BY created_at DESC doing a full table scan of 40 million rows. Adding an index on (customer_id, created_at) takes it to 5 ms. That single step beat any amount of hardware, and nothing else was needed for months.

Skipping to sharding while an unindexed query causes the load is the classic mistake. Probe: "what if reads are fine but writes are the problem?" Then batch and queue writes, review indexes (every index slows writes), and consider partitioning before sharding.

What stops a stateless tier from scaling horizontally, and what do you do about it? Intermediate

Say this: "Hidden state in server memory or on local disk, and shared bottlenecks behind the tier, especially database connections. I externalise state and check the downstream limits."

What "stateless" buys. If any server can handle any request, a load balancer can add or remove instances freely, and a crashed instance loses nothing. State pinned to one instance breaks that.

Hidden stateSymptomFix
Sessions in memoryUser logged out when routed elsewhereRedis session store or signed tokens
Uploads on local diskFile missing on the other serverObject storage (S3-style)
In-process caches that must agreeDifferent servers show different valuesShared cache, or short TTLs and accept per-instance differences
Local scheduled jobsJob runs once per instance (emails sent 10 times)Leader election, a distributed lock, or a job queue

The shared-bottleneck trap. The app tier scales by CPU, but the database has a connection limit. 100 instances each with a pool of 20 connections is 2,000 connections, which can exceed what the database handles well (often a few hundred active). Fix with smaller pools, an external pooler (such as PgBouncer), or caching in front. Other shared limits: downstream API quotas, a single Redis node, a license-limited service.

Rule: before adding instances, confirm the bottleneck is actually in that tier. Adding app servers in front of a saturated database makes things worse.

A trade-off question: how do you handle a hot key or hot partition? Senior

Say this: "First decide whether the hot traffic is reads or writes. Reads: cache and replicate the hot item. Writes: split the key and aggregate. Each option has a price I can name."

What a hot key is. Data is spread by key across nodes, assuming even traffic. But popularity is skewed: one celebrity account, one flash-sale SKU, one tenant. Its node sits at 100 percent while others idle, and adding nodes does not help because that key still lives on one.

Hot traffic isOptionPrice
ReadsReplicate the item to many cache nodes, or add a tiny in-process cache with a 1 to 5 second TTLBrief staleness; invalidation
Reads (cache miss storm)Request coalescing: one fetch fills the cache, other waiters share itSlight added wait for followers
WritesSplit the key: append a random suffix 0..N-1 (sku42#7), write anywhere, sum on readReads touch N keys; exactness is harder

Worked example. A flash sale of 1,000 units gets 100,000 requests in a minute on one SKU row. Reads of "remaining stock" are served from a 2-second local cache. Purchases go into a per-SKU queue that applies conditional decrements in order, or stock is pre-split into 10 buckets. The shop stays up and nobody oversells.

Pitfall: splitting keys without a plan for reads, so the summing cost moves the bottleneck. Also detect hot keys with metrics (top-N key access) so you find them before they page you.

When do you choose to degrade or reject traffic rather than scale? Senior

Say this: "Scaling takes minutes and has limits; overload hits in seconds. So I decide in advance what to sacrifice, rejecting low-priority traffic, switching off expensive features and serving stale data, so the core function survives."

Why overload collapses everything. When a service is saturated, queues grow and latency rises past the client timeout. Clients time out and retry, which adds load. Requests that finish are already abandoned by the caller, so work is wasted ("goodput" falls toward zero). Everyone fails, not just the extra users.

What shedding looks like, with an example. At 120 percent of capacity:

  1. Reject anonymous and bot traffic first (return 503 or 429 fast, with Retry-After).
  2. Turn off expensive optional features: recommendations, exact counts, personalised ranking.
  3. Serve stale cached pages instead of recomputing.
  4. Protect critical paths such as checkout and login by reserving capacity for them.

Users see a plainer site, but checkout still works.

Supporting mechanisms. Timeouts shorter than the caller's; bounded queues (reject when full rather than grow forever); retry budgets with exponential backoff and jitter; circuit breakers; priority classes. Autoscaling still matters, but treat it as the slow response, with shedding as the fast one.

Cost: some users get a degraded or rejected response, and you must test the degraded modes, since untested fallbacks fail. Agree with the product owner beforehand which features are expendable.

Judgment, ambiguity and design reviews

How do you handle ambiguous or incomplete requirements? Senior

Say this: "I clarify the goal, ask only the questions that change the design, state my assumptions explicitly and propose defaults. I pin down the irreversible decisions early and defer the reversible ones."

  1. Clarify the goal, not just the feature: who is the user, what outcome do they need, what is the deadline, what does success look like?
  2. Ask the numbers that change the design: users, requests per second, data size and growth, read/write ratio, latency target, availability target, consistency needs, geography, compliance. If nobody knows, estimate with a range and state it.
  3. List assumptions explicitly and get them confirmed ("I assume 1 million daily users, 99.9 percent availability, eventual consistency is fine for the feed").
  4. Separate must-have from nice-to-have and design for the must-haves with extension points for the rest.
  5. Find the irreversible decisions (data model, public API, tenancy) and nail those down; defer reversible ones.
  6. Reduce risk early: a spike or prototype on the scariest unknown, a thin vertical slice shipped behind a flag.

Why numbers matter, with an example. "Build a notification system." If it is 1,000 users, one server and a cron job will do. At 50 million users with a spike of 5 million sends in a minute, you need queues, rate limits per provider and priority classes. The same sentence, two completely different designs; the numbers decide.

In an interview, spend the first few minutes on this out loud. It signals seniority more than any specific technology.

Trap: jumping straight to boxes and arrows, or asking twenty questions with no proposed defaults. Ask a few, then propose: "Unless you tell me otherwise I will assume X."
How do you explain a technical trade-off to a non-technical stakeholder? Intermediate

Say this: "I translate it into money, time, risk and customer experience, avoid jargon, offer two or three options with a recommendation, and say what would change my mind."

Structure.

  1. The decision needed, in one sentence.
  2. Options, each with cost, time and risk.
  3. Your recommendation and why.
  4. What you need from them, and by when.

Example. "We can make checkout always available, but then two people might occasionally buy the last item and we would have to apologise to one. Or we can guarantee no double sales, but during rare outages checkout would show an error for a few minutes. Based on your order volume I expect about 5 apologies a month versus maybe 10 minutes of errors a year. I recommend the strict option because apologies cost more than the short outage."

Notice what it does: no mention of CAP, replicas or partitions; it turns the trade-off into outcomes the person already cares about, with estimated frequencies.

Habits that work. Quantify where possible and be honest about uncertainty ("I am fairly sure, I will confirm after a benchmark"). Do not hide the downside of your preferred option; stakeholders who discover it later stop trusting you. Use one analogy at most. Check understanding by asking them to restate the decision.

You are asked to review someone else's design. What do you ask? Senior

Say this: "I review the thinking before the boxes: the problem and numbers, the alternatives considered, who owns the data, what happens on failure, what breaks at 10 times load, how we operate it, and what is hard to undo. I ask questions rather than dictate."

  • Problem and scope: What problem is this solving, for whom? What is explicitly out of scope? What are the numbers (load, data size, growth)?
  • Alternatives: What else did you consider and why was it rejected? A design with no alternatives has not been thought through.
  • Data: Who owns each piece of data? What is the source of truth? Consistency needs? What is the schema migration path?
  • Failure: What happens when each dependency is slow, down or returning garbage? Retries, timeouts, idempotency, partial failure, duplicate messages?
  • Scale: What breaks first at 10 times load? Any single point of failure or hot key?
  • Operations: How do we deploy, roll back, monitor, alert and debug? What is the runbook? What does it cost?
  • Security and privacy: authn and authz, tenant isolation, sensitive data, audit.
  • Evolution: How do we change the API or schema without downtime? What is hard to undo?
  • Simplicity: What could we remove? Is each component justified by a requirement?
  • Rollout: Feature flags, canary, migration plan, success metrics.

Example of a high-value question. "The order service publishes an event after saving to the database. What happens if the process crashes between the two?" This exposes a dual-write bug in one sentence and leads to the outbox pattern.

Style matters. Ask questions rather than dictate, separate blocking concerns from preferences ("must fix" vs "nit"), propose an alternative when you object, and praise what is good. The goal is a better design, not winning.

How do you separate one-way-door from two-way-door decisions? Senior

Say this: "A two-way door can be reversed cheaply, so I decide fast with limited information. A one-way door cannot, so I slow down, prototype, write a design doc and get review. Where possible I turn one-way doors into two-way doors."

The idea. The phrase is popularised by Amazon. The question to ask of any decision: "if this is wrong, what does it cost to undo, and who is affected?"

Two-way (reversible)One-way (hard to reverse)
A cacheThe primary key scheme
A library behind an interfacePublic API contracts that customers code against
A feature behind a flagThe tenancy model
Instance sizesThe shard key

Making one-way doors reversible. Abstraction layers around the datastore; dual-writing to old and new during a migration; versioned APIs; feature flags; an exit plan for vendors (export format tested). Example: picking a message broker. Hide it behind a small publish/subscribe interface and keep payloads in an open format, and switching costs weeks. Scatter vendor-specific features through 40 services and it is a year.

Cost: over-abstraction for flexibility you never use is itself a smell; abstract at the points where a change is plausible and expensive, not everywhere. Also do not agonise over two-way doors: the cost of slow decisions is real.

What makes a good design document, and how do you drive a decision when people disagree? Senior

Say this: "A good doc states the problem, goals and non-goals, numbers, the proposal, honest alternatives, risks and a rollout and rollback plan, and stays short enough to be read. For disagreement I agree on criteria first, settle facts with a time-boxed prototype, and if still split the owner decides and we record why."

Skeleton.

  1. Problem and context; goals and non-goals.
  2. Requirements with numbers (load, latency, availability).
  3. Proposed design, with one diagram and the key data flows.
  4. Alternatives considered, each with real pros and cons and why not chosen.
  5. Risks, open questions, security and privacy notes.
  6. Rollout, monitoring, rollback; success metrics.

Resolving disagreement, as a procedure.

  1. Separate facts from preferences. "Will it handle 5,000 writes per second?" is a fact: benchmark it. "I prefer X" is a preference.
  2. Agree on criteria and weights first (latency, cost, delivery date, operability, team skills), then score options. The debate moves from people to criteria.
  3. Time-box a prototype for factual disputes, one or two days, with a pre-agreed pass mark.
  4. Decide. If still split, the named owner decides (disagree and commit). The dissenters get their concerns recorded, with a tripwire ("if p99 exceeds 500 ms we revisit").
  5. Record the decision, the reasoning and the conditions to revisit, as a short architecture decision record in the repo.

Keep it about the design, not the person, and make sure the quieter engineers' objections get heard.

How do you choose between two technologies you could both use (for example Kafka vs RabbitMQ, Postgres vs MongoDB)? Senior

Say this: "I use criteria, not taste: fit with the access pattern, required guarantees, scale now and in two years, operational burden and team experience, ecosystem, cost and exit cost. Then a short proof of concept on my real workload, and I record the trade-off I accepted and the trigger to revisit."

Criteria checklist.

  • Access pattern and data model fit.
  • Guarantees needed: ordering, durability, transactions, replay.
  • Scale now and in 2 years (numbers, not adjectives).
  • Operations: managed offering, upgrades, monitoring, on-call. A technology your team knows beats a theoretically better one it does not.
  • Ecosystem, client libraries, hiring.
  • Cost, maturity, and cost to leave.

Worked example: Kafka vs RabbitMQ. RabbitMQ is a traditional broker: flexible routing, per-message acknowledgement, messages removed once consumed, good for task queues at moderate volume. Kafka is a distributed append-only log: messages are retained and can be replayed, ordered per partition, many independent consumer groups, very high throughput; consumers track an offset. Replay and multiple consumers reading the same stream point to Kafka; simple job distribution with priorities and routing points to RabbitMQ or a managed queue. If the team has used neither and volume is small, a managed queue is the lowest-risk pick.

Run a proof of concept with your real message sizes, volume and failure scenarios (kill a node), not a vendor benchmark. State the decision, the main trade-off accepted, and the trigger to revisit (for example "if we need replay, we migrate to Kafka").

What data do you use to validate or reverse a design decision after launch? Intermediate

Say this: "I define success metrics before building, compare against a baseline, set a review date and pre-agreed tripwires, and keep a decision log so future engineers know why, not only what."

Metrics to choose in advance.

  • Latency percentiles (p50, p95, p99), never only averages: an average of 100 ms can hide 1 percent of users waiting 5 s.
  • Error rate and availability against the stated target.
  • Saturation: CPU, memory, connections, queue depth and message age.
  • Cost per request or per customer.
  • Delivery metrics: deploy frequency, lead time, incident count.
  • The product metric the design was meant to move (conversion, search success rate).

Worked example. You added a cache to cut catalogue latency. Before launch you record p99 of 800 ms and database CPU of 70 percent. The goal: p99 under 200 ms and CPU under 40 percent. Tripwire: "if hit rate stays under 60 percent after 2 weeks, we remove it". Result shows hit rate at 91 percent, p99 at 150 ms: keep. If hit rate was 35 percent, you reverse the decision with evidence instead of defending it from habit.

Habits. Keep a decision log (short records with context, decision, consequences). Review at the set date. Treat a missed tripwire as information, not failure.

Quick estimate: how would you justify a design choice with back-of-envelope numbers? Intermediate

Say this: "I convert users to requests per second, apply a peak factor, compare to what one machine can do, and let the number choose the architecture. I show the arithmetic."

Worked example. 5 million daily active users, each making 20 reads and 2 writes per day.

  1. Reads: 5M x 20 = 100M per day. A day has about 86,400 seconds (round to 100,000 for easy maths), so about 1,000 to 1,200 per second on average.
  2. Peak is usually 3 to 5 times average, so plan for about 5,000 reads per second.
  3. Writes: 5M x 2 = 10M per day, about 115 per second average, maybe 500 at peak.
  4. Compare: a single tuned relational primary handles 500 writes per second easily. 5,000 reads per second fit on a few replicas, or mostly a cache.
  5. Conclusion: no sharding needed, so choose the simple design.

If writes were 100 times higher (about 11,000 per second sustained), the conclusion changes, and you would discuss partitioning or a write-optimised store. Storage works the same way: 10M writes a day x 1 KB is 10 GB a day, about 3.6 TB a year, which a single node can hold but you must plan retention and backup.

Habits. State the peak factor, round aggressively, keep units visible, sanity-check against known capacities, and say which number would flip your decision.

Behavioural architecture questions

How do you structure a strong answer to any behavioural architecture question? Senior

Say this: "I use situation, task, action, result, tuned for seniors: context and stakes, the constraints, the options I weighed and why, my decision, the result in numbers, and what I would do differently. The options part is what interviewers most want."

The shape (about 2 to 3 minutes).

  1. Context and stakes (20 seconds): the system, scale in numbers, team size, the business pressure.
  2. The problem and constraints: what was failing or required, deadline, budget, what you could not change.
  3. Options you weighed: two or three, each with its trade-off.
  4. Your decision and why, including what you personally did versus what the team did (say "I", not only "we").
  5. Result with numbers: latency from 800 ms to 120 ms, cost down 40 percent, incidents from 6 a quarter to 1.
  6. Reflection: what you would do differently, what you learned.

Why it works. Interviewers are testing judgement. Anyone can say what they built; a senior explains what they did not build and why. The "I vs we" split shows ownership, and the numbers show you measured.

Preparation. Prepare four or five stories in advance (scaled a system, a failure or outage, a decision you regret, a disagreement, a migration or rewrite) and reuse them across questions, rehearsing each to fit 2 minutes. Be truthful: interviewers probe with "why not X?" and "what went wrong?", and fabricated detail collapses under the second question.

"Tell me about a system you scaled." How do you answer? Senior

Say this: "I pick a system where I can give before and after numbers and an ordered sequence of bottlenecks, and I explain how I found each one, why I did things in that order, and what I deliberately did not do."

Before: one service, one database, 200 requests per second, p99 of 2 seconds at peak. Diagnosis: profiling showed 60 percent of database time in three queries and a thread pool exhausted by a slow downstream call. Steps: added indexes and fixed N+1 queries (p99 to 600 ms), added a cache for the read-heavy catalogue (database load down about 70 percent), moved report generation to a queue, added read replicas, set timeouts and a circuit breaker. Result: sustained 2,000 requests per second with p99 under 300 ms and lower cost per request. Lesson: measure first; the biggest wins were the cheap ones.

(Illustrative numbers; use your own real ones.)

What makes this answer strong.

  • Diagnosis before action: you profiled, you did not guess.
  • Order: cheap and reversible first (queries, cache) and expensive last.
  • A "did not do": "we did not shard because the numbers did not need it" shows restraint.
  • What broke next: after the cache, the connection pool became the limit. Showing the bottleneck moving proves you really lived it.
  • Your role: which parts you owned.

Probes to expect: "How did you know the cache hit rate would be good?" (measured key distribution), "what about invalidation?" (TTL plus delete on write), "what would you do at 10 times that?".

"Tell me about a design decision you regret." How do you answer well? Senior

Say this: "I choose a real decision with real consequences, show the sensible reasoning at the time, the signals I missed, the impact, how I corrected it, and the process change I made. No blame, no fake weakness."

Components of a good answer.

  1. The decision and why it seemed right then.
  2. The signals you missed or ignored.
  3. The real impact (time, money, incidents).
  4. How you noticed and what you did to fix it.
  5. What you changed in how you decide now.

Illustrative example: "We split a five-person team's product into eight microservices early on because it was fashionable. The result was slow feature delivery, constant cross-service debugging and a shared release process. I should have pushed for a modular monolith. When we saw lead time double, I proposed merging four services, did it incrementally over a quarter, and release lead time fell from days to hours. Now I require a written justification and a concrete trigger before any new service is created."

Avoid blaming others, a decision with no consequence ("I worked too hard"), and ending without the lesson. The interviewer is checking for self-awareness and learning, not for a clean record.

"How do you decide to rewrite?" or "Tell me about a migration you led." Senior

Say this: "I quantify the pain, show that incremental fixes cannot reach the goal, estimate honestly with a buffer, and run a staged plan with a rollback. The migration is done only when the old system is switched off."

Frame it with the decision tests. Is the pain measurable (incidents, lead time, cost)? Could refactoring reach the goal? Is the team able to keep the old system running meanwhile? If yes to measurable and no to incremental, a migration is justified.

The mechanics to describe.

  • Strangler routing: a seam in front sends traffic per capability to old or new.
  • Shadow traffic and output comparison: the new system processes real requests silently and diffs results against the old.
  • Data migration: a dual-write phase or change data capture, a backfill, and a consistency check before cutover.
  • Percentage-based cutover: 1, 10, 50, 100 percent with metrics gates and a fast rollback.
  • Feature freeze on the old path, so the target stops moving.
  • Definition of done: old system turned off and data archived. Many migrations forget this and run two systems forever.

Mention a risk that actually materialised (for example hidden consumers of the old database) and how you handled it. Strong closing: what you measured to confirm success.

"Tell me about a time you disagreed with a technical decision." Intermediate

Say this: "I argue with data, propose a smaller reversible step, and then commit to the outcome. I show respect for the other view and I can say what changed or confirmed my position."

Example shape. You proposed a queue between two services; a senior colleague preferred direct calls for simplicity.

  1. You did not just repeat your view: you ran a load test showing direct calls failed when the downstream slowed.
  2. You shared the data neutrally and acknowledged the cost of a queue (new infrastructure, duplicates).
  3. You proposed a smaller step: timeouts and a circuit breaker first, and add the queue if error rates crossed a stated threshold.
  4. You got agreement. Later metrics supported one side (say what honestly, even if it was theirs), and you adjusted.

What it demonstrates: evidence over opinion, a reversible compromise, and the ability to disagree and commit. Avoid stories where you were simply right and the other person foolish; they signal poor collaboration.

"Tell me about an outage you caused or handled, and what changed afterwards." Senior

Say this: "I cover impact, detection, mitigation first, then root cause as a chain of contributing factors rather than one person, and the concrete system fixes. The tone is blameless."

Structure.

  1. Impact: users affected, minutes, money.
  2. Detection: how you found out and how long it took. Customer-reported is a finding in itself.
  3. Mitigation first: rollback, failover, shed load. Stop the bleeding before hunting root cause.
  4. Root cause: a chain, not a name. Example: a config change (trigger) plus no canary (gap) plus a missing alert (gap) plus an unbounded retry (amplifier).
  5. Fixes: alerts, tests, canary deploys, runbooks, removing a single point of failure, each with an owner and date.

Show system thinking. The fix addressed the class of failure, not just this instance. If asked about a public incident, stick to what is known: for example, the 2012 Knight Capital loss was linked to a deployment that left old code active on one server, and the lesson drawn is about deployment consistency and kill switches.

Pitfall: telling a hero story where you saved the day but never explaining why it could happen. Interviewers want the learning.

"You have two weeks and a requirement that needs a six-week proper solution. What do you do?" Senior

Say this: "I do not just say yes or no. I clarify what the two-week outcome must prove, propose a thin slice that meets the real need, keep the irreversible choices right, record what I cut as explicit debt with a repayment date, and tell stakeholders plainly what the shortcut risks."

Steps.

  1. Clarify the real need. Is two weeks a demo, a pilot with 10 customers, or a hard contract date? The answer decides what can be cut.
  2. Cut scope, not quality, where it matters. Support one tenant or one region; hard-code or run manually the parts not yet exercised; use managed services; put it behind a feature flag.
  3. Keep one-way doors right. Data model, API contract and security get done properly, since they are hard to change after customers rely on them.
  4. Record debt: what was cut, the risk, the trigger and date to repay, and an owner.
  5. Be honest with stakeholders: "this will work for 10 customers; at 100 it will need the hardening in weeks 3 to 6".
  6. Protect time afterwards to harden it; otherwise the temporary becomes permanent.

Cost: some rework and a known-fragile area, accepted knowingly. This answer shows you balance business urgency with engineering responsibility.

"How do you raise the quality of design decisions across a team?" Senior

Say this: "Lightweight design docs with an alternatives section, short architecture decision records in the repo, reviews framed as questions, shared failure-mode checklists, and incident reviews that feed back into those checklists. I keep the process proportionate."

Mechanisms and why each helps.

  • Design docs for non-trivial changes: writing exposes gaps, and an alternatives section forces comparison instead of the first idea.
  • Architecture decision records (ADRs): short files in the repo (context, decision, consequences) so future engineers know why.
  • Design reviews framed as questions, with a shared checklist (failure modes, idempotency, data ownership, rollout, observability) so quality does not depend on who happens to review.
  • Post-incident reviews, blameless, whose findings update the checklist.
  • Pairing juniors with seniors on design, and letting juniors present, since explaining a decision is how judgement grows.

Measure by outcomes: fewer repeated incident types, fewer late surprises in review, shorter time to onboard. Keep it proportionate: a reversible change needs a paragraph in the pull request, not a document, or people will route around the process.
