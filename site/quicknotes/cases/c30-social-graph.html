Case sheet 30: Design a Social Graph (Friends and Followers)

Who follows whom, mutual friends, suggestions, and the account with 100 million followers. Condensed from the full handbook case.

1 Problem · 2 Requirements · 3 Estimation · 4 API · 5 Data · 6 Architecture · 7 Deep dives · 8 Scaling · 9 Failures · 10 Impress · 11 Q&A · 12 Links

1. Problem in 3 lines

In one line: store 200 billion "A follows B" edges for 1 billion accounts, and answer "is A following B", "list followers", "counts", "mutual friends" and "people you may know" fast, while one celebrity with 100 million followers must not melt a single shard.
  • The graph is wide and shallow: billions of nodes, but almost every query touches one user and their direct neighbours. So we use sharded adjacency lists, not a graph database.
  • A follow is a one-way edge. A friend is derived: both people follow each other.
  • Other features (feed, notifications, search, privacy) call this service thousands of times per second.
Ask the interviewerAssume
How many users?1 B accounts, 500 M daily active users (DAU)
Follow or friend?Both: one directed edge model, friends derived
How skewed?Average 200 follows each. Median account about 100 followers; a few thousand accounts over 10 M; the biggest about 100 M
Limits?Follow cap 7,500, friend cap 5,000, followers uncapped
Latency?Point check p99 under 10 ms, list page under 50 ms, mutual friends under 100 ms; suggestions may be hours old
Consistency?The actor sees their own follow at once; others and counters may lag seconds; blocks must act fast
Private accounts?Yes, as an edge state "pending", not a separate system

2. Requirements

Functional

  • Follow and unfollow (idempotent: repeating changes nothing)
  • Approve or reject a request, remove a follower, block and unblock
  • Does A follow B (and B follow A) in one call
  • Followers and following lists, paged, newest first
  • Follower and following counts
  • Mutual friends and their count
  • People-you-may-know suggestions
  • Publish graph-change events for feed, notifications and search

Non-functional

  • Read heavy: about 200 reads per write
  • Highly available: a slightly stale list is fine, an error page is not (exceptions: the actor's own view and blocks)
  • Scales by adding shards; no query touches all shards
  • Skew tolerant: one celebrity cannot overload one partition
  • Durable: an acknowledged follow is never lost
  • Self-healing: the several copies of the data converge after crashes

Out of scope

  • Feed ranking and delivery (case 6)
  • Content storage
  • The ML model that scores suggestions (we show where it plugs in)
  • Full user search, trust and safety classifiers

4. API (a few lines)

POST   /v1/users/{id}/follow        body {request_id}  -> {state: following | requested}
DELETE /v1/users/{id}/follow        idempotent unfollow
DELETE /v1/users/{id}/follower      remove a follower
POST / DELETE /v1/users/{id}/block
GET /v1/users/{id}/relationship?with=VIEWER  -> following, followed_by, friends, blocked, pending
GET /v1/users/{id}/followers?limit=50&cursor=OPAQUE   (same for /following)
GET /v1/users/{id}/counts           -> {followers, following}
GET /v1/users/{id}/mutual?limit=20  -> {count, sample}
GET /v1/suggestions?limit=20
Internal: POST relationships:batchGet (up to 500 targets), GET followers:scan (1,000 per page)
  • The actor id comes from the login token, never from the request body.
  • request_id plus the key (src, dst) makes follow safe to retry.
  • The cursor is a keyset cursor (it remembers the last key), not an offset. Offsets skip or repeat rows when the list changes and get slower as they grow.
  • The batch call exists so a page of 50 profile cards costs a few calls, not 50.

5. Data model and store choices

TableKeyWhy

The version (a timestamp or per-edge counter) lets late or repeated messages settle: an old "add" arriving after a newer "remove" is dropped.

Store optionVerdict
Graph databaseGreat for deep traversals, hard to shard at 200 B edges, hot nodes. Keep for fraud or analytics, not serving

We add an in-memory cache for lists and counters, Kafka for graph-change events, and a batch engine for suggestions.

6. Architecture and main flows

Simple version first: one database, one table follows(follower, followee, created_at), two indexes, COUNT(*) for counts and a self-join for mutual friends. It fails at scale because 200 B rows do not fit a machine, COUNT for 100 M followers scans 100 M entries, and one hot account hits one range.

Follow flow

  1. Gateway authenticates and rate limits (follow spam is the top abuse).
  2. Write service checks blocks and the cap, then writes edges_out and the outbox row in one transaction on the actor's shard. A private target gives state pending.
  3. Return at once. The actor's own cache entries are updated now (read-your-writes for the actor only).
  4. A relay publishes EdgeAdded(A, B, version) to Kafka, partitioned by target so events for one account stay ordered. The outbox avoids the dual-write problem.
  5. The worker upserts the in-edge row, writes friend rows if the reverse edge exists, and bumps two counters. Counter deltas carry the event id so repeats are ignored.
  6. Feed and notification services read the same event. Unfollow is the same flow with a delete (tombstone), protected by the version.

Read paths

  • Relationship (A, B): two point reads on the out-edge table, (A,B) and (B,A). Never the in-edge table, so a celebrity is not hit.
  • Following list: range scan of A's partition, 50 per page, usually from cache.
  • Followers list: scan B's buckets and merge (one bucket for normal accounts).
  • Counts: one cache read; maintained incrementally and reconciled nightly.

7. Deep dives

7.1 Sharding and keeping two tables in step

Shard byList costVerdict
hash(src, dst)Every list hits all shardsRejected: bad tail latency
hash(owner id), each table by its own ownerOne shard per list; relationship check at most twoChosen; celebrity partitions fixed with buckets
Range of user_idOne shardRejected: newest signups pile on one shard
Graph partitioning (keep communities together)Often one shardRarely worth the complexity; no help for celebrities

Choice: 1,024 logical shards (hash(user_id) mod 1024) mapped to 64 nodes by a small shard map, 16 per node. Adding capacity moves whole logical shards (about 15 GB each, minutes), so keys are never rehashed.

Why not two-phase commit for the two edge rows? At 1,750 follows per second it is possible, but it adds latency, ties availability to two shards, and blocks on a slow one. We commit the truth locally, copy asynchronously and idempotently, and repair. The window is seconds, and nobody but the actor can tell: the actor sees the follow through read-your-writes. If events are lost, an anti-entropy job compares partitions (checksums) and rebuilds edges_in and friends from edges_out, never the other way round.

7.2 Mutual friends

Follower networks (one-way): "people I follow who also follow B". Do not load B's 100 M followers. Loop over the smaller side: for each account f that A follows (typically 200, at most 7,500) check whether edge (f, B) exists, batched by shard (about 64 parallel calls). Cost depends on A, not on B's fame. Caching: cache the inputs (friend lists of active users, up to 40 KB), not the pair answers.

7.3 Friend suggestions

The main signal is friend of a friend: the more mutual friends, the likelier two people know each other. A ranker adds shared groups, employer, contacts and recent views. Online is too heavy: 200 friends x 200 friends is 40,000 candidates per request. So it is a daily batch job.

OptionStrengthWeakness
Offline friend-of-friend count plus ranker (chosen)Predictable cost, easy to debug, cacheableHours stale; new users start empty (use contacts)
Online for small graphsFreshUnpredictable tail latency
Graph embeddings plus nearest-neighbourFinds candidates beyond two hopsNeeds ML infrastructure; hard to explain
Random walksGood for sparse usersMore compute per user
  • Pipeline: edge snapshot, two-hop join (emit A, C, via B), count paths per (A, C), score and filter, keep top 50 in a key-value store.
  • The problem: a middle node with d friends makes d(d-1) pairs. Fix: cap or sample nodes above about 1,000 friends and weight each path by 1 / log(degree of the middle node) (Adamic-Adar).
  • Job size (estimate): 100 M active users x 20,000 sampled pairs = 2 trillion emissions; 1,000 cores at about 1 M per second each finish in about 2,000 seconds.
  • Serve time: re-filter live so followed, blocked or deactivated people never show; each suggestion carries a reason like "12 mutual friends".

7.4 Celebrity hot nodes

Three separate hot spots, three remedies.

Hot spotWhat breaksFix
In-edge writes20,000 follows per second land on one partitionBucket the key: (dst_id, hash(src) mod B). B = 1 normally; 16 above about 1 M followers; 64 for the biggest. 20,000 / 16 = 1,250 per second per shard
CounterOne key incremented 20,000 times per second16 sub-counters, sum on read (cache 1 s); batch increments in memory about 500 ms
Reads and giant listsMillions read one profile; 800 MB listCache with 1 to 5 s TTL, replicate hot keys across cache nodes, page scans in 1,000 rows from replicas, cap list depth in the UI

Choice: threshold-based buckets, bucket count stored in the user's metadata row. Bucketing every account (say B = 4) wastes the benefit for 99% of accounts. A dedicated celebrity cluster adds isolation but still needs buckets. Changing B means a background job rewrites that account's in-edge rows from edges_out, with dual reads during the cutover.

8. Scaling and bottlenecks

  • Cache: per-user lists, friend lists, counters, relationship bits; read-through, about one hour TTL for lists, seconds for celebrity counters. The actor's own entries are updated on write.
  • Replicas: cache misses go to replicas; a short "recently wrote" flag routes the actor to the primary for a few seconds.
  • Coalescing and batching: concurrent misses for one key share one fetch; batch calls group lookups by shard.
  • Writes: 1,750 per second is small. Kafka partitions by target; a burst becomes a short delay in derived tables, never a user error.
  • Resharding: move whole logical shards (about 15 GB each). If 1,024 is not enough at 10x, split each in two.
  • At 10x (3.5 M reads per second, 2 trillion edges): 4,096 to 8,192 logical shards, a per-host cache tier, streaming suggestion updates plus embeddings.
  • Build in phases: sharded relational with out and in tables; then cache, replicas, outbox and friend table; then suggestions, repair and counter service; then buckets and multi-region.

Multi-region: a home region per user. All edges_out writes for A go to A's home; other regions hold async replicas. The event travels to B's home to update edges_in and counters. No two regions write the same edge, so no conflicts; versions settle unfollow and block races. On disaster, promote a replica and lose at most a few seconds of edges.

10. Things to say to impress

Say this:
  • "The graph is wide and shallow, so I use sharded adjacency lists, not a graph database."
  • "edges_out is the single source of truth. Everything else (in-edges, friends, counters, suggestions) is derived, eventually consistent and rebuildable."
  • "A follow is one local transaction with an outbox row. No two-phase commit on the hot path."
  • "Relationship checks read the actor's own out-edge partition, so a celebrity never sees that read."
  • "For mutual friends I iterate the smaller side, so cost never depends on the celebrity's size."
  • "Strong where safety needs it (blocks, the actor's own view), eventual everywhere else."
  • "Hot node has three separate problems: write partition, counter key, and read key. Each has its own fix."
Traps: reaching for a graph database by reflex; COUNT(*) online; offset paging; loading a celebrity's follower list as one object; trusting only derived tables for blocks; running two-hop suggestions online; forgetting the actor id must come from the token.

11. Interview Q&A

Why not use a graph database such as Neo4j?

Graph databases shine at multi-hop traversals and ad-hoc pattern queries, such as fraud rings. Our serving queries are almost all one hop from a known user id: list neighbours, check one edge, count. Those are key-range scans, which a sharded key-value or relational store does cheaper and with simpler scaling. Cutting a billion-node, 200-billion-edge graph across machines without a natural cut is hard, and a celebrity node concentrates load. I would keep a graph engine for offline analytics or a safety subgraph, and use sharded adjacency tables for serving.

How do you keep the out-edge and in-edge tables consistent?

The out-edge table is the source of truth. I write it with an outbox row in one local transaction on the actor's shard. A relay publishes the event and a worker applies the in-edge row. Every step is idempotent, keyed by (src, dst) and a version, so redelivery is safe. Events can still be lost, so an anti-entropy job compares partitions and rebuilds the derived table from the truth. The actor sees the right state through read-your-writes, and others only see a delay of seconds. This avoids two-phase commit on the hot path.

How do you compute mutual friends quickly?

With a friend cap of 5,000, I keep a derived friend table so each user has a short sorted list. The service fetches both lists, usually cached and each on one shard, and intersects them in one pass over at most about 10,000 entries, which is microseconds. For a one-way follower graph I loop over the smaller side, the accounts the viewer follows, and do batched point lookups on whether each follows the target. The cost depends on the viewer, not on a celebrity's follower count. If only the count is needed, a sketch or bitmap is enough.

A celebrity with 100 million followers gets 20,000 follows per second. What breaks and how do you fix it?

Three things break: the in-edge partition sits on one shard that cannot take 20,000 writes per second, the follower counter is a single key incremented that often, and reads of the profile are hot. I bucket the in-edge key as (account, hash(follower) mod 16), so writes spread to about 1,250 per second per shard. I split the counter into sub-counters and batch increments over a short window. I cache profile and count reads with a short TTL and replicate hot keys. Relationship checks are unaffected because they use the follower's own out-edge partition.

How do you shard the graph, and what are the downsides?

I hash the owner's user id into 1,024 logical shards and map them to physical nodes. Out-edges are owned by the source and in-edges by the target. A list query is a single-shard range scan, and adding capacity moves whole logical shards without rehashing keys. The downsides: each follow writes to two shards, so I need asynchronous propagation and repair; and hash sharding ignores graph locality, so a two-hop query touches many shards. I batch lookups per shard and move two-hop work offline, where a dataflow engine handles the shuffle.

How do you generate friend suggestions at this scale?

Offline. A daily job reads an edge snapshot, joins it with itself to emit every friend-of-friend path, counts paths per candidate, combines them with other signals in a ranker, and stores the top 50 per user in a key-value store. The risk is the quadratic blow-up from high-degree nodes, so I cap or sample neighbours of nodes above about 1,000 friends and weight each path by the inverse log of the middle node's degree. At serve time I filter again so people already followed, blocked or deactivated never appear, and each suggestion shows a reason like the mutual friend count.

How do you keep follower counts accurate?

I never run a count query online. Each event applies a delta to a counter, sharded for hot accounts, and the worker dedupes by event id. Counters can still drift if an event is lost or applied twice, so a nightly job recounts rows per partition from the edge table and corrects the stored value. The UI rounds large numbers, so small error is invisible. Where exactness matters, like the 7,500 follow cap, I count the actor's own out-edge partition, which is small and consistent.

A user follows, unfollows and follows again, and the events arrive out of order. What happens?

Each edge write carries a version, such as a hybrid timestamp assigned on the actor's shard. The in-edge and friend upserts apply a change only if its version is higher than what is stored. A delayed "add" with an old version is ignored after a newer "remove" was applied, so the final state matches the actor's last action. For deletes I keep a tombstone with its version until stale events cannot arrive any more, which the queue retention bounds. Partitioning events by target also keeps most of them in order.

How do blocks work, and why can they not be eventually consistent?

A block is a safety decision, so it is a strongly consistent write on the blocker's shard, with removal of edges in both directions done by events. Propagation takes seconds, so any service that decides whether one user may see another's content checks the block row at read time, from the primary or a replica known to be fresh. The block bit has a very short cache TTL and is invalidated synchronously on write. Derived views such as suggestions exclude blocked pairs both at batch time and again at serve time.

How do you delete an account with millions of edges?

First I mark the account deleted, which hides it at once because every read path filters on account status. Then an asynchronous job streams its edge rows from edges_out (its followees) and from the in-edge buckets (its followers), deletes the matching rows on the other side, decrements counters in batches, and removes the user from friend and suggestion tables. For an account with 100 million followers the job runs in throttled pages so it does not cause a tombstone storm that slows other partitions. Progress is tracked so it can resume after a crash.

How would the design change for a symmetric friendship network?

A friendship needs a request-and-accept handshake and is one logical relationship, but we can still store two directed rows in the friend table, written on accept. With a cap on friends, lists are small and the celebrity problem mostly goes away, replaced by a separate "page" or "follower" concept for public figures. Mutual friends become the central query and are cheap because of the cap. The accept step needs either a cross-shard transaction or the same asynchronous pattern with a state machine (requested, accepted, declined), and the same repair job guarantees both rows exist.

Real-time: follower counts and lists on a big account look wrong for a few minutes. What do you check?

I look at the derived-table freshness first: Kafka consumer lag and the age of the oldest unapplied event for that account's partition. If lag is high, workers are slow or a bucket is hot, so I scale workers or check whether the account crossed the bucket threshold without being bucketed. If lag is normal, I compare edges_out and edges_in for the account with the repair tool and check the mismatch metric. Counts are the cheapest to fix because the nightly job can recount, and the UI already rounds big numbers. I would not touch edges_out, since it is the truth.
