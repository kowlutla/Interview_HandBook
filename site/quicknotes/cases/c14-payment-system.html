Case Sheet 14: Design a Payment System

One-page revision of the Handbook worked solution. Same numbers, plain words. The hard part is being correct when messages repeat or get lost, not speed.

1 Problem2 Requirements3 Estimation4 API5 Data6 Architecture7 Deep dives8 Scaling9 Failures10 Impress11 Q&A12 Links

1. Problem in 3 lines

One line: build a payment service for a shop or marketplace that charges cards through an outside provider, never charges twice, never loses a cent in its books, and can prove every number to an auditor.
  • We do not build the card network. We call a PSP (payment service provider, such as Stripe or Adyen), which talks to Visa, Mastercard and the banks.
  • Our job: be correct around a slow, outside system that can time out, repeat messages and reply late.
  • Money is special: doing something twice, or not knowing whether you did it, is a financial and legal problem.

Clarifying questions to ask (and the assumed answer)

AskAssume
Build the card network, or talk to one?Talk to a PSP. No acquiring bank.
Payment methods?Cards, plus one redirect or bank-transfer method to show the asynchronous case.
Authorize then capture, or one step?Both. Authorize = reserve funds. Capture = actually take them (often at shipment).
Refunds, partial capture, chargebacks?Refunds and partial capture yes. Chargebacks (the bank forces a reversal after a dispute) are recorded; the dispute workflow is a stretch goal.
Scale?10 million payments a day, about 116 per second, peak 10 times that.
Correctness or availability?Correctness. "Try again in a minute" is fine. A double charge is not.
Do we store card numbers?No. Stay out of the heaviest PCI compliance scope.
Say this: "I will not build the card network. I integrate with a PSP, and my job is to be correct around an unreliable, asynchronous outside dependency. In payments the difficulty is retries, timeouts and duplicate messages, not throughput."

2. Requirements

Functional

  • Create a payment (amount, currency, card token, merchant)
  • Authorize, capture (full or partial), void, refund
  • Take results from PSP webhooks (calls the PSP makes to us later)
  • Keep an append-only double-entry ledger
  • Reconcile with PSP files and bank statement
  • Payouts to merchants, wallets
  • Read payment status and history

Non-functional

  • No double charge (headline)
  • No lost payment: if charged, we eventually know
  • Ledger: debits equal credits, rows never edited
  • Durable: zero data loss for the ledger
  • 99.95% create, 99.99% status reads
  • Checkout under 2 s at p99 when the PSP is healthy (PSP takes 300 ms to 1.5 s)
  • Audit trail kept for years (often 7 or more)

Out of scope

  • Acquiring and issuing
  • Card network routing
  • Tax
  • Company-wide accounting ledger
  • Training fraud models (only the hook)
  • Subscriptions and dunning
  • Regulatory filing

4. API

POST /v1/payments           Idempotency-Key: required (client UUID)
  { order_id, merchant_id, amount: 4999, currency: "USD",   // integer cents
    payment_method_token, capture: "manual"|"automatic" }
  -> 201 { payment_id, status: authorized|requires_action|processing|failed, next_action }
POST /v1/payments/{id}/capture   Idempotency-Key  { amount }   // partial allowed
POST /v1/payments/{id}/void      Idempotency-Key
POST /v1/payments/{id}/refunds   Idempotency-Key  { amount, reason }
GET  /v1/payments/{id}           -> state + history
GET  /v1/merchants/{id}/balance  -> ledger-derived balances
POST /webhooks/psp/{provider}    signed, always answers 2xx fast
Events out: payment.authorized, payment.captured, payment.failed, refund.succeeded
  • Every money-changing call needs an idempotency key: a unique label for one intent, repeated on every retry of it.
  • Amounts are integers in minor units (4999 = 49.99). Floats cannot hold 0.10 exactly. JPY has 0 decimals, KWD has 3.
  • Status is honest, not a promise. "processing" is a legal answer when the PSP has not replied.
  • The server makes the payment id: time-sortable and not guessable.

5. Data model and store choices

Choice: a relational database with ACID transactions. The idempotency guarantee is literally a unique index, and we need multi-row atomic writes. A key-value store would make us rebuild both in code.
TableHoldsWhy
paymentsid, merchant, order, amount, currency, status, version, psp_referenceCurrent state. Updated with optimistic locking on version.
payment_eventspayment, seq, from, to, cause, actor, timeAppend-only audit trail. Unique (payment, seq).
idempotency_keys(merchant, key) unique, request hash, status, stored response, lock timeDedupe. Written in the same transaction as the payment.
psp_attemptsattempt, operation, PSP key, outcome (success, declined, unknown)Written before each PSP call so a crash cannot forget it.
ledger_accounts / transactions / entriesaccounts, one txn per money event, debit and credit rowsImmutable. Rows of one txn sum to zero.
outboxtopic, payload, published_atMakes "update state and send message" atomic.
psp_webhook_inbox(provider, event id) unique, raw bodyDedupe and replay of webhooks.
recon_itemsfile line, reference, amount, matched paymentReconciliation workspace.

Sharding: hash of merchant_id (or payment_id). A payment, its events, key, outbox and ledger rows share one shard, so the critical transaction stays local. We avoid cross-shard transactions (that is the two-phase commit problem) and use sagas instead. Eventually consistent stores are only for rebuildable views such as search and dashboards.

6. Architecture and main flow

Main flow: authorize, then capture

  1. Client sends create with an idempotency key. One transaction inserts the key row (unique) and the payment in created. A repeat key returns the stored result.
  2. Risk (fraud) hook is called. If it says no, the payment becomes failed with no PSP call.
  3. State moves to authorizing and an outbox row is inserted in the same transaction. Our intent is saved before anything leaves.
  4. The relay publishes the job. A PSP worker calls the PSP with a PSP-level key built from payment id and operation, so repeating is safe.
  5. Result: authorized, requires_action (3-D Secure, the bank asks the shopper to approve), declined, or still unknown.
  6. Capture repeats the pattern. On confirmation (reply or webhook), one transaction sets captured, appends the ledger transaction and writes an outbox event.

Why the simple version breaks: a crash after the PSP charged but before we saved loses track; a client retry double-charges; holding a database transaction open over a slow PSP call ties up connections; webhooks come twice and out of order.

7. Deep dives

7.1 Idempotency, layer by layer

One line: "exactly once" does not exist over a network. We build at-least-once delivery plus idempotent effects, which gives exactly-once effects.
LayerKeyWhat stops the duplicate
Client to APIclient key K1Unique (merchant, K1). Repeat returns stored response. Same key with a different body returns 422.
Worker to PSPK2 from payment id + operationPSP replays the original result instead of charging again.
Bus consumersevent idProcessed-ids table, in the same transaction as the effect.
Webhooks and ledger(provider, event id); (payment, kind, step)Unique constraints. Replays hit the constraint and do nothing.
Key situationBehaviour
Never seenInsert as in_progress, do the work, store response, mark done (one commit).
Done, same requestReturn the exact stored response.
In progress (concurrent duplicate)409 with a retry hint. Never run a second copy.
Different request body422, a client bug.
In progress, lock expired (crash)Resume from the recorded state. Do not restart.

Choice and why: use a database unique index, not "check then insert" in code, because two concurrent retries both pass a check. Keys are per merchant and kept 24 hours to 7 days; the (merchant, order) unique constraint is the backstop.

7.2 State machine, outbox and sagas

ToolSolvesHow
State machine + compare-and-setLate, duplicate, out-of-order webhooksUPDATE ... WHERE version = ? AND status = ?. Zero rows changed means someone else moved it. A backward transition is logged but ignored.
Transactional outbox"Update DB and publish" not atomicWrite the message to an outbox table in the same transaction. A relay (polling or change capture) publishes it. It may publish twice, so consumers are idempotent.
SagaSteps across services with separate databasesLocal transactions, each with a compensating action: reserve stock (release), authorize (void), capture (refund). Compensation is not rollback; a refund is a new ledger transaction.

Choice and why: sagas and outbox, not two-phase commit. The PSP cannot take part in 2PC, and 2PC would hold locks across slow calls and tie service availability together.

7.3 Double-entry ledger

OptionProCon
Mutable balance columnSimpleLoses history, hides bugs, lost updates on hot rows
Append-only double-entry (chosen)Self-checking (debits = credits), replayable for auditors, inserts do not fightBalances must be derived (cached running balance plus daily snapshot)
Event sourcing as only storeGreat auditComplex read models and fixes; we keep events beside state and ledger

Example: capture 100.00 with 3.00 commission. Debit PSP receivable 100.00. Credit merchant payable 97.00. Credit platform revenue 3.00. A refund of 30.00 is a new transaction (debit merchant payable, credit PSP receivable); the old rows stay. Rules: balanced per transaction and per currency, insert-only (no UPDATE or DELETE rights), mistakes fixed by reversing entries, unique (payment, kind, step) so retries do nothing. The PSP fee is usually booked later from the settlement file. A nightly job recomputes balances and pages on any mismatch. Hot accounts are split into sub-accounts (for example 16) and summed on read.

7.4 Timeouts and unknown outcome

Option on timeoutResult
Treat as failedOrder released but shopper may be charged. Bad.
Treat as successOrder shipped with no money. Bad.
Treat as unknown, retry with same key or query by reference (chosen)Learns the truth. Shopper sees "processing".

Also: do not retry hard declines or 400 errors; retry 5xx, timeouts and resets; slow down on 429; add a circuit breaker per PSP. Put the retry budget in one layer (the worker), or 3 x 3 x 3 retries turn one click into 27 calls. Unused authorizations expire at the issuer, usually after about 7 days.

7.5 Reconciliation

Compare three views: PSP settlement file, bank statement, our ledger. Match on PSP reference, then amount and date. Matches close. Differences are exceptions: in PSP file but not ours (missed webhook), in ours but not PSP (investigate), amount mismatch (partial capture, FX, fees: adjusting transaction), duplicate in PSP file (a real double charge: auto-refund and raise an incident). Fixes are new ledger entries. Runs daily, intraday for high value. Target auto-match rate above 99.9%.

8. Scaling and bottlenecks

Consistency: inside a shard the state, ledger and outbox commit together. Across services it is eventual, with explicit states ("payment pending"). The money path is CP: if the shard is down, return 503 rather than guess. At 10 times scale (100M per day, 12,000 per second): more shards (16 to 128), a dedicated ledger service, multiple PSP accounts, streaming reconciliation, and cells so one failure hits only some merchants.

10. Things to say to impress (senior and lead)

Say this:
  • "A timeout is unknown, not failed. I record intent before the call and resolve by asking the PSP with the same key."
  • "Idempotency is layered: client, PSP, bus consumers, webhooks and ledger each have their own unique constraint."
  • "I never hold a database transaction across a PSP call. Outbox for messages, sagas for multi-service flows, and compensation is a refund, not a rollback."
  • "The ledger is append-only and double entry. Balances are derived and checked nightly. Fixes are reversing entries."
  • "Reconciliation is the control that turns 'we believe' into 'we can prove', and its match rate is a health metric."
  • "Keep card data out: hosted fields and tokens, never store the CVV, scrub logs. PSP fees (2 to 3 percent, about 10 million dollars a day here) dwarf server cost, so approval rate is the real optimization."
Traps: using floats for money; check-then-insert idempotency; retrying at every layer; trusting webhook order or body; editing ledger rows; retrying hard declines; routing a retry to a second PSP without proof; calling the PSP inside a DB transaction.

Also mention: PCI scope (hosted fields, tokens, isolated vault only if forced), 3-D Secure as an async flow that may never finish (time out the payment after about 30 minutes), fraud hook with about 100 ms budget, SLOs (stuck payments zero, ledger imbalance zero, webhook lag p99 under 30 s, outbox lag under 5 s), alert on approval-rate drop per PSP. Phasing: one PSP, idempotency, state machine, basic ledger first; outbox, saga, automated reconciliation next; sharding, second PSP, multi-currency, payouts after. Wallets are just ledger accounts: internal spends are one local transaction with balance >= amount; payouts are external and irreversible, so use unique payout ids, a reserve for refunds and chargebacks, and second approval for large ones.

11. Interview Q&A

How do you guarantee a customer is never charged twice?

I use layered idempotency. The client sends a unique key for each payment intent, and the API has a unique index on (merchant, key), so any repeat gets the stored response. Our worker calls the PSP with a deterministic key built from the payment id and operation, so a retry after a timeout is the same request and the PSP returns the first result. Webhooks are deduplicated by the provider event id, and ledger transactions are unique per payment, kind and step. Finally, daily reconciliation against the PSP files catches any duplicate that slipped through, and we refund it automatically. No single layer is trusted alone.

The PSP call timed out. What do you do?

I treat it as unknown, not failed. The attempt row was saved before the call, so I retry with the same PSP key after exponential backoff with jitter, or I ask the PSP for the payment by our reference. Either one tells me what really happened. The payment stays in capturing and the shopper sees "processing". If the retry budget runs out, it moves to unknown / review and a webhook or reconciliation settles it. I never release the order or mark it paid without proof, because each guess can lose money.

Why not use a distributed transaction (two-phase commit) across order, payment and inventory?

Two-phase commit needs every party to support prepare and commit and to hold locks while the coordinator decides. That links their availability and slows everything. The most important party, the PSP, does not support it at all. So I use an orchestrated saga: each step is a local transaction with a compensating action (release stock, void the authorization, refund the capture). Every step is idempotent and the orchestrator saves its progress so it can resume after a crash. The in-between states are explicit in the state machine.

Why a double-entry ledger instead of updating balances?

Double entry checks itself: debits must equal credits, so money cannot appear or vanish without a visible imbalance. Because rows are append-only, we can rebuild any balance at any past time, which auditors need. Inserts also avoid the lost-update and lock contention you get on a hot balance row. A correction is a reversing transaction, so history shows both the mistake and the fix. The balance becomes a cached, derived number that a nightly job recomputes from entries to detect drift.

How do you handle webhooks that arrive twice, late or out of order?

I save the raw event in an inbox table with a unique key of provider and event id, and answer 2xx fast so the PSP stops retrying. Processing is asynchronous. Duplicates hit the unique key and are dropped. For ordering, the state machine allows only forward transitions and applies them with compare-and-set on a version, so a late "authorized" after "captured" is recorded but does not move state back. For important transitions I re-fetch the payment from the PSP rather than trust the body, which also defeats forged webhooks. Signature check and a timestamp window are required.

How do you make the database update and the event publish atomic?

With a transactional outbox. The state change and an outbox row are written in one local transaction, so both happen or neither does. A relay reads unpublished rows, by polling or change data capture, publishes them, then marks them sent. A crash between publish and mark causes a duplicate, never a loss, and consumers handle duplicates through a processed-ids table. This gives at-least-once delivery with effectively exactly-once effects, without a transaction across the database and the broker.

If everything is idempotent, why do you still need reconciliation?

Idempotency covers the failures we thought of. Real systems also fail in ways we did not plan for: a webhook nobody retried, a PSP bug, a manual fix, a fee change, a returned bank transfer. Reconciliation is the independent control that compares our ledger with the PSP settlement file and the bank statement. It matches on PSP reference, then amount and date, and classifies each difference as missing, extra, amount mismatch or duplicate. Fixes are new adjusting ledger entries. The match rate also works as a health metric for the whole system.

How do you keep the system out of PCI scope?

Card numbers never reach our servers. The browser sends the card straight to the PSP through hosted fields or a redirect, and the PSP gives back a token. We store and use only the token, plus safe details such as brand, last four digits and expiry. We never store the CVV and we scrub card data from logs and traces. That puts us in the lightest compliance category, though we still protect secrets, access and transport. If a vault is truly required, it is a small isolated service with its own audit and the rest of the system stays token-only.

How would you scale writes for a very hot ledger account, like platform revenue?

Appending entries is cheap. The contention comes from the single running balance row that every transaction must lock. I split the hot account into N sub-accounts, for example by payment id modulo 16, so writes spread over rows and shards. The logical balance is the sum of the sub-accounts, read from a snapshot plus later entries. Accounts that need overdraft protection, such as wallets, keep one row per user, which is rarely hot, and use a conditional update. Revenue accounts have no overdraft check, so they can be split freely.

What do you do when the fraud service is down?

This is a policy set ahead of time, not an argument during the outage. A common rule is to fail open for small amounts with tighter local velocity limits, and fail closed or send to review above a threshold. A false decline on a 5 dollar order costs far less than approving a 5,000 dollar fraudulent one. Either way, the payment records that the risk check was skipped and why, so it is auditable and can be re-scored once the service returns.

Production incident: approval rate suddenly drops. What do you check?

First split the drop by PSP, by issuer BIN range (the first digits of the card, which show the bank) and by country. A fall on one PSP or one issuer points to them, not to users. Then check our own changes: recent deploys, the fraud model or rule changes, and the share of declines that are hard versus soft. Check the circuit breaker and timeout rates, since timeouts show up as unknown outcomes. If one PSP is bad, route new payments to the backup PSP. Keep the idempotency and state rules in place while doing this, so the switch cannot cause double charges.

How would you support multiple currencies without rounding errors?

Every amount is an integer in minor units with an ISO currency code, and the ledger balances per currency. Mixing currencies in one entry is rejected. A conversion is an explicit pair of transactions through FX clearing accounts, recording the rate, its source and time, so gain or loss is visible. We lock the quoted rate for the life of the authorization and use a documented rounding mode, often half-even, with deterministic handling of the leftover cent. Reconciliation allows a small tolerance on converted amounts and flags larger drift. Currencies with different decimals, such as JPY with none, come from a currency table.
