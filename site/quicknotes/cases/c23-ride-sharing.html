Case sheet 23: Design a Ride-Sharing Service

One-page cheat sheet: an Uber-style service for 20 million trips a day. Full write-up: Handbook case study 23.

ProblemRequirementsEstimationAPIDataArchitectureDeep divesScalingFailuresImpressQ&ALinks

1. Problem in 3 lines

One line: match a rider with a nearby driver in seconds while both phones move, never promise one driver to two riders, and price the trip from live supply and demand.
  • It is really four problems: a location firehose (driver positions), a map query (who is near here), a small, critical trip core (who is assigned to whom) and an analytics loop (surge price and arrival time).
  • Each part gets a different store and a different consistency choice. Saying this out loud early earns most of the credit.

Clarifying questions to ask:

AskAssume
Scale?100 M monthly riders, 5 M registered drivers, 20 M trips per day, about 500 cities.
Match speed?Request to driver assigned: p50 under 5 s, p99 under 15 s (not counting the driver's tap time). Driver gets about 15 s to accept.
Location freshness?Driver app reports about every 4 s. A position older than 30 s means "unavailable".
ETA accuracy?Within about 20% for 80% of requests. No wild outliers.
Price?Base fare times a surge multiplier per small map area. Shown first, then locked.
Ride types?Single-rider on-demand only. Pooling and scheduled rides are out of scope.
Global?Yes, but cities are mostly independent. This is the most useful fact in the problem.

2. Requirements

Functional

  • Driver goes online or offline and streams location while online.
  • Rider sees nearby cars, a fare estimate and a pickup ETA.
  • Rider requests a ride; system finds a driver, offers the trip, binds the two on accept.
  • Both see each other live during pickup and trip.
  • Trip states: requested, offered, accepted, arrived, in progress, completed, cancelled. Each change is recorded.
  • Fare is computed at the end from real route, time and the locked surge. Payment is triggered.
  • Cancel with rules (maybe a fee).

Non-functional

  • Matching and live updates in seconds.
  • High availability on the request path (a rider who cannot book goes to a competitor).
  • Strong consistency only for assignment: one driver per trip, one trip per driver.
  • Write-heavy location ingestion that never delays trip requests.
  • Graceful fallback if surge, ETA ML or maps are down.
  • Trip and money records are durable.

Out of scope: pooling, scheduled rides, driver onboarding, payment internals (see case 14), turn-by-turn navigation, fraud models beyond hooks, map tile serving.

4. API

POST /v1/drivers/me/status      { "state": "online" | "offline" }
POST /v1/drivers/me/location    { lat, lng, heading, speed_mps, accuracy_m, ts, seq }  -> 202, no body
GET  /v1/rides/estimate?pickup=..&dest=..   -> fare range, surge, surge_token, pickup_eta_s
POST /v1/rides   { pickup, dest, product, surge_token, client_request_id } -> 201 { ride_id, state }
GET  /v1/rides/{id}      POST /v1/rides/{id}/cancel
POST /v1/offers/{id}/accept | decline        (driver)
POST /v1/rides/{id}/arrived | start | complete
push: driver_location, ride_state, offer (expires_in_ms: 15000)
  • client_request_id makes ride creation safe to retry: a double tap on bad mobile data does not order two cars.
  • surge_token is a short-lived signed value holding the multiplier the rider saw. The server honours the shown price without trusting a number from the client.
  • Location returns 202 with no body: it is high volume, loss tolerant, and the next update replaces it.
  • seq is a per-driver counter so old or duplicate updates are dropped cheaply (phone clocks are unreliable).
  • User ids come from the auth token, never from the body.

Trap: do not write the 375 K location updates into the trip database, and do not make them durable synchronously. They are a cache of "where the driver is right now".

6. Architecture and main flows

Shape: gateway in front; Location, Matching, Ride, ETA, Pricing and Push services behind it. Location data lives in memory and Kafka. Only the Ride service writes the durable trip store.

Main flow: request and match a ride

  1. Rider sends POST /rides with an idempotency key.
  2. Ride service saves the ride as REQUESTED in the trip store first. Now the request is safe. It hands off to Matching.
  3. Matching reads the rider's cell and ring 1 (nine cells) from the geo index, widening to ring 2 if fewer than about 30 candidates.
  4. One batched call to ETA re-ranks the candidates by road time.
  5. Offer goes to the best driver with a 15 s expiry.
  6. On accept, Ride service does the atomic assignment (a conditional write). Both apps are told and live location starts flowing to the rider.
  7. Decline or timeout: back to REQUESTED, next candidate.

Location flow: app sends a position every 4 s, gateway routes by city, Location service drops it if seq is old, updates the cell, publishes to Kafka in batches, and answers 202 without waiting for either.

7. Deep dives

7.1 Finding nearby drivers (geospatial index)

Idea: split the map into cells with an id. A point maps to its cell by arithmetic, so "who is near" is a few set lookups, not a scan. Cost is bounded by cells read (9, then 25, then 49), not by drivers in the city.

OptionGoodBad
GeohashSimple, works in any key-value store, prefix gives containment.Distorts near poles. Close points can have different prefixes across a border, so neighbours must be computed.
QuadtreeAdapts to density, good for static points.With 375 K moves per s it needs constant rebalancing and locking; hard to spread across machines.
S2Little distortion, a cell is one 64-bit integer, library finds neighbours.Needs a library; corner and edge neighbours are at different distances.
H3 (hexagons)All 6 neighbours are equally far, clean rings and smoothing (good for surge).Parent and child cells do not nest perfectly.

Choice: a fixed-resolution hierarchical grid (H3 or S2), cells about 0.5 to 1 km across, and the same system coarser for partitioning and surge. A move is "remove from old cell set, add to new one", and most updates (about 30 m at 8 m/s) do not even change cell. Quadtree stays the answer for static places (case 13). Each record carries a timestamp; queries ignore anything older than 30 s and a sweeper cleans stale entries.

7.2 Matching and exactly-one assignment

StrategyHowTrade-off
Greedy nearestOffer to the closest available driver by ETA right away.Fastest and simplest, but can steal the only good driver from the next rider.
Batched matchingCollect requests and idle drivers per city for about 2 s, solve a best-total-ETA assignment.Better overall wait under scarcity; adds up to the window in latency; more complex.
Broadcast to manyOffer to several drivers, first accept wins.Quick, but wastes driver attention and causes many "too late" rejections.

Choice: greedy by road ETA first, add 1 to 3 s batching in dense cities. Offers go to one driver at a time, 15 s expiry; ranking is a tunable score.

-- one transaction in the Ride service
UPDATE rides SET state='ACCEPTED', driver_id=:d, version=version+1
  WHERE ride_id=:r AND state IN ('REQUESTED','OFFERED') AND version=:v;
INSERT INTO driver_by_ride_active(driver_id, ride_id) VALUES (:d, :r);  -- unique driver_id
-- 0 rows or unique violation: roll back, "offer no longer valid"

The conditional update protects the ride; the unique key protects the driver. If they sit on different shards, first claim the driver with a compare-and-set (available to reserved, with an expiry), then update the ride, and release the claim if that fails (a tiny saga). A lock with a lease can cut contention, but the database condition is always the final judge. The offer timer is itself conditional, so a timer firing just after an accept does nothing.

7.3 ETA

ApproachLatencyAccuracyNote
Straight line / fixed speedmicrosecondspoor (rivers, one-way streets)Only for first filtering.
Dijkstra / A* on live graphtens of msgoodToo slow: 30 ETAs x 900 per s = 27,000 routes per s.

Hierarchies + ML residualabout 2 to 5 msbestChosen. Falls back to the line above if the model fails.

Layers: (1) road graph with time-of-day speeds; for 30 drivers to one pickup run one backward search from the pickup. (2) Speeds: historical median per edge per 15-minute bucket (batch job) blended with live speeds from driver GPS after map matching. (3) An ML model predicts the leftover error (lights, pickup delay, rain). ETA service is stateless apart from its in-memory graph, split by region.

7.4 Surge pricing

Per cell, per minute, a stream job counts demand (requests) and supply (available drivers) over a sliding window of about 5 min. The ratio is smoothed with neighbouring cells, mapped through a curve (flat at 1.0 until about 1.2), clamped to a maximum, and ramped so it moves only a bounded step per interval. Smoothing and ramping stop oscillation: high price cuts demand and draws drivers, the ratio falls, the price drops, demand returns, repeat.

ApproachTrade-off
Fixed rule table by time and placeSimple and predictable, blind to events and weather.
Reactive ratio per cellChosen first. Reflects real conditions, easy to explain; lags by the window.
Forecast demand (ML)Acts before the shortage; needs good data. Add later, reactive stays as fallback.

Price lock: the estimate carries a signed surge_token (multiplier, cell, product, expiry of about 2 to 5 min). The ride request presents it, the Ride service checks signature and expiry and stores the multiplier on the ride. If the token expired and the price rose, the app shows the new price and asks again.

8. Scaling and bottlenecks

  • Ingestion: partition by city so one city needs at most tens of thousands of updates per s in one process. Batch writes, compress Kafka batches. Adaptive reporting: every 4 s moving, every 15 to 30 s when stationary, at once on status change. This also saves battery.
  • Geographic hot spots (airport, stadium, New Year's Eve): shard matching by cell prefix inside a city; batched matching absorbs bursts; airports use a virtual FIFO queue for fairness.
  • Hot keys: the surge key is read on every estimate, so cache it in each Pricing instance for 10 to 30 s.
  • City skew: size partitions by load; megacities get dedicated clusters.
  • Live location to riders: on accept, the rider's gateway connection subscribes to that driver. The Location service forwards each update only for drivers with subscribers. App interpolates between updates. If the socket drops, poll GET /rides/{id}. The pre-booking "cars nearby" view just polls every 5 s (cacheable for a second per cell).
  • Caches: nearby-drivers per cell for about 1 s; fare estimates by (origin cell, destination cell, product, minute) for about a minute, then adjusted by current surge.
  • Multi-region: the city is the unit of isolation. Each city is homed in one region with its index, matching, trip shard and surge job. Only accounts, global config and analytics cross regions. DR: replicate trip data to a second region; the live index rebuilds from fresh updates in seconds.

10. Things to say to impress

Say this:
  • "This is four problems: firehose, geo query, small transactional core, analytic loop. I will store each differently."
  • "Location is a cache of now. It lives in memory and Kafka, never in the trip database."
  • "Correctness comes from the database, not from locks or reservations: a version check on the ride and a unique active-ride key per driver."
  • "The trip is an explicit state machine. Every command is idempotent and every transition is a compare-and-set."
  • "The city is the unit of isolation, so the hot path is single-region and a failure stays inside one cell."
  • "Every dependency has a fallback: no surge means base fare, no ML means graph ETA, no Kafka means stale surge but working matching."
  • "Ordering uses seq and version, not phone clocks."
  • "Location is personal data: short retention, coarsen what other riders see."
SLOs to quote: match success at least 97% within 60 s | time to match p50 under 5 s, p99 under 15 s | 99% of online drivers with index entry under 10 s old | ETA error under 15% | double assignments 0 | request API 99.95%.

11. Interview Q&A

Why keep driver locations in memory and not in the main database?

Locations are written 375,000 times per second at peak and each value is useful for only a few seconds, because the next update replaces it. Making each write durable would need a huge write-heavy cluster to protect data that is nearly worthless after 4 seconds. So the live position sits in an in-memory index with a replica fed from the same stream. The full history is appended to Kafka and archived for ML and disputes. If an index node dies, it refills from the next round of updates within seconds. The trip database holds only the small data whose loss matters, such as who was assigned to which ride.

How do you find the closest drivers efficiently?

Divide the map into hierarchical cells (S2 or H3) and keep a set of available drivers per cell. The rider's cell id is plain arithmetic. I read that cell and its neighbours (8 for square S2 cells, 6 for H3 hexagons), and widen to the next ring if there are fewer than about 30 candidates. The cost depends on cells read, not on drivers in the city. I sort by straight distance, keep the top 30, then re-rank by road ETA because straight lines ignore rivers and one-way streets. A fixed grid beats a quadtree here because drivers move constantly and a grid never needs rebalancing.

Two riders request at the same instant and the same driver is best for both. What happens?

Both matching attempts may offer to that driver, but only one accept can win, because assignment is a conditional write. The Ride service updates the ride only if it is still open and inserts into the active-driver table, where a unique key on driver id rejects a second active ride. The loser gets "offer no longer valid" and the dispatcher moves to the next candidate. To cut waste, a driver with an open offer is marked reserved in the index for the offer time, and batched matching gives a contested driver to whichever rider lowers the total cost. Correctness depends only on the database condition, never on the reservation.

Why not straight-line distance for ETA?

A driver across a river can be 300 metres away and 20 minutes away. I use a road graph with time-of-day speeds and contraction hierarchies, so one route takes about a millisecond, and one backward search from the pickup serves all candidates at once. Live speeds learned from drivers' own GPS (after map matching) are blended with historical profiles. A model then predicts the leftover error such as traffic lights and pickup delay. If the model or live feed fails, I fall back to the graph estimate, then a distance guess, so an ETA always exists.

How is surge computed and how do you stop oscillation?

A stream job counts demand and supply per hexagon over a sliding window of a few minutes. The ratio is smoothed across neighbours, mapped through a curve to a multiplier, clamped at a maximum and ramped by a bounded step per interval. Oscillation happens because a price rise cuts demand and draws drivers, the ratio drops, price drops, demand returns. The window, spatial smoothing and bounded steps slow that loop. The rider gets a signed token with the multiplier, so the price shown is the price charged even though the live value keeps moving.

A driver accepts just as the rider cancels. What happens?

Both are conditional transitions on the same ride row. Cancel moves REQUESTED or OFFERED to CANCELLED; accept moves OFFERED to ACCEPTED. The database applies whichever arrives first and the other fails its precondition. If cancel wins, the driver's accept returns "trip no longer available" and the app clears the offer. If accept wins, the cancel is handled as a cancel of an accepted ride, possibly with a fee. Neither actor can force an invalid state and, because commands are idempotent, retries are harmless.

How does the rider see the driver's car move?

On accept, the rider's gateway connection is registered as a subscriber to that driver. Each time the Location service takes an update for a driver with subscribers, it forwards it to the gateway holding the rider's WebSocket. Only drivers on a trip are tracked, so the lookup is small even though pushes can reach 250,000 per second at peak. The app interpolates between 4-second points so the car glides. If the socket drops, the app polls the ride endpoint and reconnects. Browsing before booking is plain polling every few seconds, since slightly stale is fine.

How do you go multi-region, and what crosses regions?

The city is the unit of isolation. Each city lives in one region with its geo index, matching, trip shard and surge pipeline, so the latency-critical path never crosses regions. Only slow data crosses: the account directory, config and analytics streams. A traveller uses the new city's region and the profile is fetched on demand. For disaster recovery, trip data is replicated to a second region; on failover the location index rebuilds from fresh updates in seconds and only in-flight trips need reconciling. This also limits blast radius, since a bad deploy in one city cell does not touch others.

What do you do about bad GPS, like a position jumping across the city?

Filter at ingestion and again at use. Updates carry an accuracy value, so poor ones are dropped. A speed check against the previous point rejects teleports, for example implied speeds above about 200 km/h. The sequence number drops out-of-order duplicates. For speed learning and fares, points are map matched to roads with a model that looks at the whole sequence, which smooths jitter between tall buildings. For fares I use the matched route and fall back to the estimated route if breadcrumbs have gaps. A driver silent for 30 seconds leaves the available set.

Production incident: "no drivers found" suddenly in one city. How do you debug it?

I check the blast radius first: one city or all. One city points at that geo index partition or its gateway routing. I look at the age distribution of driver locations and Kafka and ingestion lag, since a stalled stream makes every record look stale (older than 30 s means ignored). I check whether the primary crashed and the replica took over, and whether a recent deploy changed cell size, ranking or status filtering. Mitigation is failing over to the replica or letting the index refill, which takes about 4 s after the stream resumes. Afterwards I add an alert on location freshness (target: 99% of online drivers under 10 s old).

How would you add pooled rides?

Matching must also search active trips whose route passes near the new pickup, and use ETA to compute the detour for each existing rider, accepting only if everyone's added delay stays under a limit. The trip becomes one vehicle trip with several rider legs, the "one active ride per driver" rule becomes a seat capacity rule, the state machine gets per-leg states, and pricing splits the fare. The geo index, ETA and push layers do not change, which suggests the original split was right.

Why a fixed grid and not a quadtree, given dense downtowns?

A quadtree adapts to density, which is great for static places. But here 375,000 points move each second, so the tree needs constant rebalancing, awkward locking and hard distribution across machines. A fixed grid never rebalances: a move is removing an id from one set and adding it to another, and most moves stay in the same cell. For dense areas I use a finer resolution in the hierarchy, which gives most of the benefit of adaptivity.
