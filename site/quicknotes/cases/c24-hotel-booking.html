Case sheet 24: Design a Hotel Booking System

One-page cheat sheet: search hotels by city and dates, hold a room while the guest pays, and sell more rooms than exist on purpose. Full write-up: Handbook case study 24.

ProblemRequirementsEstimationAPIDataArchitectureDeep divesScalingFailuresImpressQ&ALinks

1. Problem in 3 lines

One line: a guest searches "double room in Lisbon, 12 to 15 June", picks a hotel, holds the room for a few minutes and pays. Search must be fast and may be slightly stale. Booking must be exact.
  • Like ticket booking (case 15), but a room is sold per night. A 3-night stay needs 3 inventory slots, all or none.
  • Rooms of one type are the same, so we sell a count ("12 doubles left"), not a named seat. A room number is given at check-in.
  • Hotels may overbook on purpose, because some guests cancel or do not show up. The system is also search-heavy: about 500 searches for every booking.

Clarifying questions to ask (and the answers we assume)

AskAssume
What is sold?A room type (for example "Standard double, sea view") for a range of nights. Inventory is a count per room type per night
Catalogue size?500,000 properties x about 10 room types = 5 M room types; booking horizon 365 days
Traffic?1.5 M bookings a day; 500 searches per booking (look-to-book)
Pay now or at the hotel?Design for prepay (harder); note pay-at-property is simpler (card guarantee only)
Hold while paying?Yes, 15 minutes
Overbooking allowed?Yes, within a limit; the hotel "walks" the extra guest to another hotel at its own cost
Other channels sell the same rooms?Yes (hotel system, other sites), so our counts can be briefly out of date
Stale search OK?Yes, a little. Search may be wrong by a bit; booking may not be wrong at all
Global?Each property has one home region that owns its inventory writes; search is served from the nearest region

2. Requirements

Functional

  • Search by place, dates, guests, rooms, with filters; show the price for the whole stay
  • Property page with room types, rates and live availability
  • Hold a room for the whole stay while the guest pays
  • Pay and confirm; send a confirmation
  • Modify and cancel by the rate's policy; release rooms, refund
  • Hotel side: set total rooms, prices, overbook limit, closed dates

Non-functional

  • Never sell more than total + allowed overbook on any night; a stay gets all nights or none
  • No charge without a confirmed booking, no booking without payment, no double charge
  • Search p95 under 500 ms; hold p99 under 300 ms
  • Availability: search 99.95%, booking 99.9%; if inventory is unreachable we refuse, not guess
  • A confirmed booking is never lost

Out of scope

  • Reviews, loyalty
  • Payment provider internals (case 14)
  • Dynamic pricing algorithms
  • Channel manager protocols
  • Room number assignment, housekeeping
  • Taxes by country

3. Estimation (same numbers as the handbook)

One line: bookings are rare (about 90 per second at peak) but searches are huge (about 30,000 per second at peak). The exact part is small, so the work is in access patterns, not size.

Searches1.5 M x 500 = 750 M per day; / 86,400 sabout 8,700/s average, about 30,000/s peak (3.5x)

Key idea: two halves. Search is high-volume, read-only and approximate: solve it with a search index, caches and bitmaps. Booking is low-volume, exact and transactional: solve it with one authoritative store and atomic conditional updates. Connect them with a cheap one-way stream of change events.

4. API

GET  /v1/search?dest=lisbon&checkIn=2026-06-12&checkOut=2026-06-15&adults=2&rooms=1&cursor=...
     -> { results:[{propertyId, fromPrice, roomTypes:[{roomTypeId, rateId, totalPrice, left}]}], nextCursor }
GET  /v1/properties/{id}/offers?checkIn=..&checkOut=..   -> per-night prices, total, taxes, policy
POST /v1/holds            (Idempotency-Key)  { propertyId, roomTypeId, rateId, checkIn, checkOut,
                                               rooms, priceQuoteToken }
                          -> 201 { holdId, expiresAt, total } | 409 SOLD_OUT | 409 PRICE_CHANGED
POST /v1/holds/{id}/checkout (Idempotency-Key) { guest, paymentMethodToken } -> 202 PENDING_PAYMENT
GET  /v1/reservations/{id}      POST /v1/reservations/{id}/cancel      DELETE /v1/holds/{id}
POST /webhooks/payments         (signed provider callback)
PUT  /v1/extranet/properties/{id}/inventory  { roomTypeId, from, to, total, overbookLimit, closed }
  • left in search is a hint only. The hold call is the authority.
  • priceQuoteToken is a signed copy of the price the guest saw. If the hotel changed the rate meanwhile we return PRICE_CHANGED, never a silent new charge.
  • Checkout returns 202 because payment can finish later through a webhook. Search uses a cursor, not page numbers, because results shift as rooms sell.

5. Data model and store choices

DataStoreWhy
Inventory, holds, reservations, paymentsRelational database (PostgreSQL or MySQL), sharded by property_idA booking changes several rows and must be all-or-nothing, and a reservation ties to a payment. Writes are only hundreds per second, so NoSQL scale is not needed and we keep transactions
Property discoverySearch engine (Elasticsearch or OpenSearch)Text, geo, filters, ranking. Indexes properties, not nights
Availability bitmaps, rate limits, fast-path keysRedis, or in-memory on search nodesSingle-key operations are atomic and take microseconds; range check is one bit operation
Static pages, photos, popular resultsCDN and cachePhotos are the biggest bandwidth item
Change eventsKafka (with an outbox in the database)Feeds the search index, availability cache, email and analytics without touching the sale
room_inventory: PK (room_type_id, stay_date) | total_rooms | overbook_limit | booked | held | closed | version
                free = total_rooms + overbook_limit - booked - held     CHECK (booked + held <= total + overbook)
rates:          rate_id | room_type_id | stay_date | price | cancellation policy | prepay flag
holds:          hold_id | room_type_id | check_in | check_out | rooms | status | expires_at | idempotency_key
reservations:   reservation_id | hold_id | room_type_id | check_in | check_out | total | status | payment_ref
reservation_nights: reservation_id | room_type_id | stay_date      -- release exactly the nights it used
  • Shard by property_id. All nights of a stay belong to one room type in one property, so a booking is a single-shard transaction. Sharding by date would scatter a 3-night stay across 3 shards and make every booking a distributed transaction.
  • Prices apart from counts: prices change far more often than counts.
  • Stay date is a plain date in the property's local time zone, not a timestamp. The check-out day is not a night.

6. Architecture and main flows

Search flow

  1. Gateway checks limits and forwards to the search service.
  2. The search index returns the top 300 candidates for place and filters, ranked by quality (out of about 20,000 properties in the city).
  3. The availability cache keeps only those with a free room on every night of the stay (maybe 120 survive). If too few survive, fetch the next 300.
  4. Attach nightly prices, sum for the stay, add taxes and fees, re-rank on total price. Return a page and a cursor, under 500 ms.

Hold flow (all nights or none)

Checkout flow

  1. Create the reservation as PENDING_PAYMENT and extend the hold by a short grace (about 3 minutes) while payment is in flight.
  2. Call the payment provider with an idempotency key made from the reservation id (a repeat cannot charge twice).
  3. On success, one transaction moves held to booked on each night, marks the hold CONVERTED and the reservation CONFIRMED, and writes an outbox event.
  4. On a decline, release the hold (held minus n) and mark the reservation FAILED. If the confirm fails because the hold is gone, refund automatically.

7. Deep dives

One line: a counter per room type per night with a conditional update; a data-driven overbook limit with TTL holds; a two-stage search with bitmaps; an orchestrated payment saga.

7.1 Inventory by date and the atomic multi-night claim

ModelStrengthWeakness
A. Counter per room type per nightFits identical rooms; tiny rows; one atomic conditional UPDATE; overbooking is just a number; easy to cacheNo physical room identity; a stay is N row updates; counters must be audited
B. Row per room with date ranges plus an overlap constraintOverlap impossible by design; good for room assignmentCannot overbook; finding a free room for a range is heavier; bad for search
C. Bitmap calendar per room typeVery compact, very fast range checksWeak for counts above one and for atomic multi-key updates; not durable

Choice: A is the source of truth; C is a derived read cache built from it. We sell by type and assign rooms at check-in, and we want overbooking, which B forbids.

UPDATE room_inventory SET held = held + :n, version = version + 1
 WHERE room_type_id = :rt AND stay_date BETWEEN :checkIn AND :lastNight AND closed = false
   AND (total_rooms + overbook_limit - booked - held) >= :n;
-- app checks rows_updated == number of nights, else ROLLBACK
Concurrency optionBehaviourVerdict
Read, check in code, writeTwo guests both see free = 1 and both write: oversellNever
SELECT ... FOR UPDATE on all night rowsCorrect; waiters queue holding connectionsAcceptable baseline
Conditional UPDATECorrect in one statement; losers fail instantlyChosen
Optimistic version columnRetries whenever any other booking touched the row, even when rooms remainWorse for counters
Serializable isolationCorrect but needs retry loops; lower throughputNot needed

Why: in a counter model, two guests booking different rooms of the same type do not really conflict while rooms remain. A conditional update lets both succeed and conflicts only at the last room; a version check conflicts every time. Keep counters honest with three guards: the counter change and reservation insert in one transaction, a CHECK constraint (booked + held <= total + overbook), and a nightly job that recomputes booked from reservation_nights and repairs drift.

7.2 Overbooking and holds with a time limit

Some guests cancel or never arrive, so a hotel that sells exactly its rooms leaves some empty. Example: 100 rooms, each guest shows up with probability 0.95. Sell 100 and about 5 rooms sit empty on average. Sell 102 and the chance of overflow is about 5%. Sell 105 and it is about 37%. Each extra sale earns revenue but raises the chance of a walk, which costs the hotel perhaps 2 to 3 times the room rate. Choose the limit where extra expected revenue equals extra expected walk cost.

  • Overbook limit is data: a column on the inventory row, set by a revenue job from show rates. Peak nights get 0; slow weekdays with free cancellation may get 5 to 10%. Non-refundable prepaid rates overbook less. A platform cap and the CHECK constraint bound it even if the job has a bug.
  • Walk handling: a full property relocates the guest to a similar hotel at its cost. Track walks per property and cut its limit if it walks too often.
  • Other channels are a bigger cause of oversell than our bugs. Treat property updates as authoritative; keep a small safety buffer on slow-syncing properties.
Hold expiry approachProsCons
1. Held counter plus a sweeper (chosen as authority)One number to read; durable; simple conditional updateCapacity returns late if the sweeper is slow (seconds); needs an index on expires_at and an idempotent worker
2. Compute held on read (sum of live holds)Exact; no driftAn aggregate query per night per attempt: too slow for the hot path
3. Redis keys with TTLNative expiry, very fastNot durable on failover; counter and key expiry are not atomic. Fine as a gate, not the authority

Choice: approach 1, with a sweeper every few seconds (flip ACTIVE past expiry to EXPIRED and decrement counters in one transaction, so a crash just retries). Safety net: before answering "sold out", the booking path expires stale holds for that room type and dates and retries once. Cancel mirrors this: use reservation_nights to release exactly the nights used, guarded by WHERE status='CONFIRMED' so a repeat is a no-op. Changing dates claims the new nights and releases the old ones in one transaction.

Common mistake: payment succeeds at minute 15 and 2 seconds, just after the hold expired and someone took the last room. The grace extension reduces this and the confirm step requires the hold to still be ACTIVE or PAYING. If not, refund automatically. Never confirm a booking without an inventory claim.

7.3 Search with availability

Where availability livesProsCons
A. Query the inventory databaseAlways exact30,000 range scans per second on the transactional store; kills booking latency. Rejected
B. Inside the search index (nested nightly field)One engine does everythingEvery booking rewrites a document; near-real-time delay; nested 365-day filters are slow; hot properties reindex constantly
C. Two stages: index for candidates, bitmap cache for dates (chosen)Index changes only when static data changes; the availability store is built for range checks; scale each part aloneTwo systems to keep in step; slight staleness; may need to over-fetch in busy periods
  • Bitmap: 365 bits per room type, 1 = at least one room sellable that night. A stay is free if (bits & mask) == mask. All 5 M room types is about 228 MB. It cannot say how many are left, so "only 2 left" comes from small counters or an inventory lookup for the displayed page only.
  • Freshness: a consumer recomputes the bit from inventory change events, usually within a second or few. Wrong "free" gives a polite 409 at hold time (fine). Wrong "sold out" hides a room briefly (fine). Publish sold-out events at higher priority, since showing gone rooms hurts trust more.
  • Prices: per room type, rate plan and night, cached with a short TTL; precompute sums for common stay lengths. Static score offline, blend with price at query time.
  • Result cache: cache the first page per normalised query for 30 to 60 s; hit rates of 30 to 60% cut peak 30,000 to about 15,000 to 20,000 reaching the pipeline. Never cache holds or checkout.

Orchestrated (one order service owns PENDING_PAYMENT, PAID, CONFIRMED, FAILED, REFUNDED) beats choreographed for money, because one place knows the whole story and is easier to audit. Choreography is fine downstream for email, analytics and search updates.

8. Scaling and bottlenecks

LayerBottleneckFix
Edge / CDNPhotos, static pagesAggressive CDN caching, image variants
Search service30,000 queries/s at peakStateless pods; result cache 30 to 60 s; two stages; in-memory bitmaps; index replicas per region
Search indexGeo and facet queries over 20,000 candidates per cityFew shards, many replicas; only top candidates; precomputed quality score
Availability cacheMany updates on a busy dayBatch events; partition by property; merge repeated changes to one room type
Inventory shard300 to 500 row updates/s at peakShort conditional updates; shard by property; replicas for non-critical reads
Payment providerProvider rate limitsCircuit breaker, queue with back-pressure, second provider
After-booking fan-outEmails, hotel notices, analyticsAsync through Kafka; consumers scale alone
  • Capacity math: 500 updates/s over 16 shards is about 30 per shard per second, far below one primary's limit. Each shard holds about 7 GB of inventory, in memory. We shard for isolation and growth, not current load. Unlike a hot concert, no queue or waiting room is needed for ordinary traffic.
  • Destination peaks (festival week in one city): writes stay modest, reads concentrate. Use the result cache, hot caching per room type, and serve slightly older cached availability when overloaded. A sold-out property fails fast because the conditional update finds free = 0. A saturated room type can use a per-property token bucket on holds.
  • Multi-region: each property has a home region that owns its writes, so no cross-region conflict on one room type. Faraway guests pay an extra 100 to 200 ms on the hold call. Search is served locally from the global event stream (a second or two behind). DR is async replication to a standby; on failover the last seconds may be lost, so reconcile against payment provider records.
  • At 10x (15 M bookings a day, 300,000 searches/s): inventory still only needs more shards; pressure is in search. Partition the index by region, precompute aggregates for common (destination, stay length) pairs, and separate a ranking service.

  • Consistency split: only the inventory counter transition for a room type must be linearizable (one primary). Search, "rooms left", trip lists and analytics are eventually consistent.
  • Idempotency: hold (key unique for 24 h); checkout (key per attempt); confirm guarded by WHERE hold_id=:h AND status IN ('ACTIVE','PAYING'); cancel guarded by status='CONFIRMED'; Kafka consumers dedupe by event id, and the bit recompute is naturally repeatable.
  • Abuse and monitoring: rate limit scrapers; cap active holds per account and device; screen fraud before capture; validate and audit hotel-side bulk updates. Correctness SLO: zero nights where booked + held exceeds total + overbook. Watch cache lag (p99 under 5 s), 409 SOLD_OUT rate (high means cache too stale), hold expiry rate, walk rate, decline rate, reconciler backlog.

10. Things to say to impress

Say this:
  • "This has two halves. Search is high-volume and approximate; booking is low-volume and exact. I keep them apart and join them with a one-way event stream."
  • "Inventory is a counter per room type per night, and I book with a conditional UPDATE, then check rows updated equals nights. The database proposes nothing; it decides."
  • "I shard by property so a multi-night stay is one local transaction. Sharding by date would make every booking a distributed one."
  • "Overbooking is data, not code: a limit per room type per night, bounded by a platform cap and a CHECK constraint, with a walk workflow as the cost of losing the bet."
  • "Holds are rows with a TTL, released exactly once by a guarded status change in the same transaction as the counter change."
  • "Stale availability is acceptable because the hold step is the authority. I publish sold-out events first."
  • "I reserve before I charge, use idempotency keys, and reconcile unknown payment outcomes instead of guessing."
  • "Strong consistency only where a wrong answer double-sells a room or mischarges a card."
Common mistake: putting availability inside the search index, using a version column for counters, sharding by date, or charging the card before claiming the rooms.

11. Interview Q&A

How do you represent inventory so a three-night stay is booked atomically?

I keep one row per room type per night with the total, an overbook limit, and booked and held counters. A stay touches one row per night, all inside one property, so it is one shard and one local transaction. I run a conditional update that raises the held counter only where free is at least the rooms requested, then check that the number of rows updated equals the number of nights. If any night fails, I roll back so the guest gets all nights or none. A CHECK constraint makes it impossible to commit counters above the allowed limit even if the application has a bug.

Why a conditional UPDATE and not SELECT FOR UPDATE or a version column?

All three can be made correct, but they behave differently under load. SELECT FOR UPDATE makes waiters queue while holding connections. A version column forces a retry whenever any other booking touched the row, even when free rooms remain, which is wasteful for a counter. A conditional update puts the check in the WHERE clause, so two guests booking different rooms of the same type both succeed and only the last room causes a conflict. Losers fail at once with zero updated rows, with no waiting on client think time.

What is overbooking and how do you implement it safely?

It means selling more rooms than exist because some guests cancel or do not show up. I make it data: an overbook limit per room type per night, so free is total plus limit minus booked minus held. A revenue job sets the limit from predicted show rates, higher on flexible low-demand nights and zero on peak nights. A platform cap and the database CHECK constraint bound it, so a buggy job cannot oversell wildly. When guests overflow, a walk workflow moves one to a comparable hotel at the property's cost, and a property that walks too often gets its limit reduced.

How do holds expire without leaking rooms?

A hold is a row with a status and an expiry, and the held counter on each night reflects its rooms. A sweeper runs every few seconds, flips ACTIVE holds past expiry to EXPIRED and decrements the counters in one transaction. If it crashes, the transaction rolls back and the next run retries, so a hold cannot be released twice. As a safety net, before reporting sold out the booking path expires stale holds for that room type and dates and retries once. Checkout also extends the hold briefly, and a payment that succeeds for a lost hold is refunded.

Why not keep availability in Elasticsearch with the property data?

The two data sets change at very different speeds. Descriptions and photos change a few thousand times an hour; availability changes with every hold, booking and release, and each change would rewrite a document and wait for a refresh before it shows. Filtering nested nightly data over a year is also slow. So I use the index for candidates by geo, filters and quality, then check exact dates against in-memory bitmaps and attach prices. The cost is a little staleness and a loop to fetch more candidates when many are sold out.

How do you answer "is this property free for these nights" in microseconds?

For each room type I keep a 365-bit mask, one bit per night, set when at least one room is sellable. For a stay I build a mask with the stay's nights set and test with a bitwise AND, which is one instruction on a machine word. With 5 million room types the whole structure is about 228 MB, so each search node can hold it in memory. A consumer updates the bit from inventory change events within a second or two. The bitmap cannot say how many rooms remain, so I fetch counts only for the page being shown.

Payment succeeded but our service crashed before confirming. What happens?

The reservation is PENDING_PAYMENT with a stored idempotency key, and the provider has a record of the charge. On restart, or through the webhook, the order service learns of the success and runs the confirm transaction, which turns held into booked and marks the reservation confirmed together with an outbox event. A reconciler also sweeps pending reservations older than a minute and asks the provider for their status by key, so a lost webhook does not matter. If the hold was lost meanwhile, the confirm fails its condition and we refund. Every unclear state is settled by asking the source of truth.

How do you avoid showing availability that is already gone?

You cannot avoid it fully, and trying costs too much. The cache is a hint built from events and lags by a second or so, so a guest may pick a room just taken. The hold step is authoritative and returns a clear sold-out answer, and we then suggest similar rooms or nearby dates. I reduce the harm in two ways: sold-out events are published at higher priority than released-room events so the cache hears bad news first, and for the last few rooms I check the inventory service before showing "only 1 left".

What changes if other websites also sell the same rooms?

Then our counters are only as accurate as the property's updates, and the real risk is one room sold twice by two channels. The property or its channel manager pushes inventory to all channels, so we treat their updates as authoritative, apply them at once, and reconcile on each one. For slow-syncing properties we keep a safety buffer and stop selling when free rooms fall to a small number. The overbook limit absorbs small errors and the walk workflow handles the rest. It is partly a business problem that we can only reduce, not remove.

How would you shard this and why by property?

I shard inventory, holds and reservations by property id. A stay covers several nights of one room type in one property, so every booking transaction touches one shard and needs no distributed coordination. Sharding by date would put each night on a different shard and turn every booking into a distributed transaction. The cost is that guest-centric queries like "my trips" cross shards, so I serve them from a secondary table fed by Kafka events. Properties spread evenly by hash, and since writes are only hundreds per second the shard count is for isolation and growth.

Real time: the 409 SOLD_OUT rate suddenly jumps. What do you check?

A high 409 rate usually means search is showing rooms that are gone, so I first check the lag from inventory commit to availability cache update. If Kafka consumers are behind or crashed, the bitmaps are stale; I scale or restart consumers and watch the lag fall. Next I look for one destination or property with a demand spike, where real sell-out is just fast. I also check whether a hotel bulk update or channel sync changed many counts at once. In the meantime I can shorten the result cache time for that city and verify the last few rooms against inventory before showing them.

Real time: a hotel reports guests arriving with no room. How do you investigate?

First check whether the nightly audit shows booked plus held above total plus overbook for that night; the CHECK constraint should make that impossible on our side. If our counters are clean, the cause is most likely another channel or an overbook limit set too high for that date, so I compare the hotel's inventory updates with our timeline and look at the property's show-rate history. I confirm the walk workflow ran and the guests were relocated. Then I lower or zero the overbook limit for that property, add a safety buffer if its channel sync is slow, and record the walk against its quality metric.

How does this differ from the ticket-booking design?

Ticket booking is dominated by a spike where a million people fight over a few thousand named seats, so it needs a waiting room, a Redis gate and hot-event cells. Hotel booking has calmer write contention, since one property is rarely fought over by thousands in a second, but it has multi-night atomicity, interchangeable rooms sold by count, deliberate overbooking, and a much bigger read problem in search with date-dependent availability and price. They share the conditional update as the authority, holds with a time limit, a payment saga with idempotency, and stale-tolerant caches in front of a strict write path.
