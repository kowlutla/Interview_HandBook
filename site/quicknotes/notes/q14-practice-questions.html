0. Key definitions

TermMeaning in simple words
Mock interviewA practice interview, ideally with another person, a timer, and a board. Talking aloud is the point.
PromptThe question the interviewer gives you, such as "Design a URL shortener".
Clarifying questionA question that changes the design, such as "How many users?" or "Must reads be fresh?"
AssumptionA number or rule you state yourself when the interviewer says "you decide". Say what changes if it is wrong.
Opening moveThe first thing you do for a prompt (estimate, name the hard problem, draw the simplest design).
Trade-offA choice where each option gains something and loses something. Say both sides.
TrapA common wrong turn that costs marks. Know it so you can step around it.
Deep diveThe one or two parts you explain in detail, usually the riskiest part of the design.
Follow-upThe extra question after your first answer. It tests whether you really understand.
BottleneckThe part that runs out first as load grows. Name it before you add a box.
Hot keyOne key that gets much more traffic than the others and overloads the machine holding it.
IdempotentDoing it twice has the same result as doing it once. This makes retries safe.
Fan-outSending one item to many receivers, such as one post to all followers.
Die, slow, lieFor each box ask: what if it stops, answers late, or answers wrong?
RubricA scoring table. You use it to grade your own practice runs.
Spaced repetitionRevisiting a prompt after a few days, so you remember it instead of cramming.

1. How to use this bank Ch 01Ch 61

One line: pick a prompt, set a timer, answer aloud without notes, then open the case sheet and mark what you missed.
  • Cover the hints. Read only the title and difficulty. Answer first. Then read the three hints and compare.
  • Hints have three jobs. The opening move shows how to start. The trade-off is the sentence an interviewer wants to hear. The trap is the mistake to avoid.
  • Difficulty tags are about the thinking needed, not about fame. Warm-up: one clear idea. Medium: two or three parts working together. Hard: correctness or scale problems that need a careful argument.
  • After each run, fix only two things. Pick the two lowest rubric scores and drill them. More than that and nothing sticks.

1Design a URL shortener
Warm-up		Open: Ask read:write ratio (about 100:1) and whether links expire. Estimate before drawing.
Trade-off: Random code versus counter-based code: counters give short codes with no collisions but need a coordinated range allocator.
Trap: Using a hash of the URL and ignoring collisions, or redirecting with 301 when you need click analytics.		Ch 32, Ch 13, Ch 02
Case sheet c01 · Handbook c01
2Design a Pastebin
Warm-up		Open: Separate small metadata from the big text blob. Ask max paste size and expiry rules.
Trade-off: Store text in object storage with metadata in a database, versus everything in one database row.
Trap: Forgetting expiry cleanup and private or unlisted pastes.		Ch 21, Ch 17, Ch 13
Case sheet c02 · Handbook c02
3Design a rate limiter
Warm-up		Open: Ask where it sits (gateway or inside each service) and what the key is (user, API key, IP).
Trade-off: Token bucket (allows bursts) versus sliding window (smooth but costs more memory). Local counters are fast but inexact; shared counters are exact but add a hop.
Trap: Race conditions on read-then-write counters. Use one atomic step.		Ch 16, Ch 15, Ch 11
Case sheet c03 · Handbook c03
4Design a key-value store
Medium		Open: Start from the single-node store, then say what you add: partitioning, replicas, then conflict handling.
Trade-off: Quorum settings: R + W > N gives fresher reads and costs latency. Leaderless gives availability and costs conflict resolution.
Trap: Saying "consistent hashing" without virtual nodes, or ignoring how a lost node is repaired.		Ch 19, Ch 24, Ch 25, Ch 28, Ch 33
Case sheet c04 · Handbook c04
5Design a distributed cache
Medium		Open: Ask what is cached and how stale it may be. Draw clients, cache nodes, and the database behind them.
Trade-off: Client-side hashing versus a proxy tier. Cache-aside versus write-through.
Trap: Treating the cache as the source of truth, and forgetting the stampede after a node dies.		Ch 13, Ch 14, Ch 15, Ch 25
Case sheet c05 · Handbook c05
6Design a news feed
Medium		Open: Estimate fan-out first: followers times posts per day. That number picks push or pull.
Trade-off: Fan-out on write (fast reads, heavy writes) versus fan-out on read (the reverse). Hybrid for celebrities.
Trap: Pushing a celebrity post to 50 million lists, and forgetting how deletes reach pre-built feeds.		Ch 60, Ch 13, Ch 36
Case sheet c06 · Handbook c06
7Design a chat system
Medium		Open: Ask 1:1 or groups, message history, and delivery receipts. Pick the connection type: WebSocket.
Trade-off: Store messages once and fetch (cheap writes) versus copy to each recipient (cheap reads). Per-chat ordering needs a sequence number.
Trap: Assuming the server always knows which gateway a user is on, and ignoring offline users.		Ch 06, Ch 34, Ch 03
Case sheet c07 · Handbook c07
8Design a notification system
Medium		Open: Ask channels (push, email, SMS), priorities, and user preferences. Draw producers, queues per channel, workers.
Trade-off: One shared queue (simple) versus a queue per channel and priority (isolation, more to run).
Trap: A marketing blast delaying a login code. Also no dedup, so a retry sends twice.		Ch 35, Ch 34, Ch 32
Case sheet c08 · Handbook c08
9Design Dropbox or Google Drive
Medium		Open: Split files into chunks, keep a metadata service, upload bytes straight to object storage.
Trade-off: Fixed-size chunks (simple) versus content-defined chunks (better dedup, more compute). Last-writer-wins versus keeping both copies on conflict.
Trap: Sending file bytes through your app servers, and ignoring conflict handling.		Ch 21, Ch 12, Ch 32
Case sheet c09 · Handbook c09
10Design YouTube or Netflix
Medium		Open: Separate upload and transcode (write side) from playback (read side, mostly CDN).
Trade-off: Pre-transcode every bitrate (storage cost) versus transcode on demand (latency).
Trap: Designing the player before the CDN. Bandwidth, not the database, is the bill.		Ch 12, Ch 21, Ch 37
Case sheet c10 · Handbook c10
11Design search autocomplete
Medium		Open: Latency budget first: under 100 ms per keystroke. Build the top-suggestions data offline.
Trade-off: Trie in memory versus a prefix table in a cache. Fresh trending terms versus stable precomputed lists.
Trap: Querying the database on every keystroke, and ignoring offensive or private suggestions.		Ch 58, Ch 13, Ch 57
Case sheet c11 · Handbook c11
12Design a web crawler
Medium		Open: Draw a frontier (queue of URLs), fetchers, a parser, and a seen-URL check. Ask scale and freshness.
Trade-off: Breadth-first (simple) versus priority by page value. Exact seen-set versus a Bloom filter.
Trap: Hammering one host (no politeness), and spider traps with endless links.		Ch 57, Ch 34, Ch 22
Case sheet c12 · Handbook c12
13Design Uber or nearby search
Hard		Open: Ask whether the points move. Static places and moving drivers need different stores.
Trade-off: Geohash or quadtree cells: fixed cells are simple, quadtrees adapt to density.
Trap: Searching only one cell and missing points just across a cell border.		Ch 58, Ch 15, Ch 25
Case sheet c13 · Handbook c13
14Design a payment system
Hard		Open: Name correctness first: no double charge, no lost money. Draw the ledger and the payment provider.
Trade-off: Synchronous charge (simple, can hang) versus async with status and reconciliation (robust, more states).
Trap: No idempotency key, or storing money as floating point numbers.		Ch 32, Ch 48, Ch 31
Case sheet c14 · Handbook c14
15Design ticket booking or a flash sale
Hard		Open: Say it is a contention problem: many buyers, few seats. Put a waiting room in front.
Trade-off: Pessimistic lock (safe, can block) versus optimistic version check (fast, retries) versus a hold with a timeout.
Trap: Holds that never expire, and a hold shorter than the payment timeout.		Ch 60, Ch 30, Ch 31
Case sheet c15 · Handbook c15
16Design a collaborative editor
Hard		Open: Ask how many people edit at once. State the core problem: two edits to the same spot.
Trade-off: Operational transform (needs a central server) versus CRDTs (merge anywhere, bigger data).
Trap: Locking the whole document. That defeats the point of collaboration.		Ch 59, Ch 06, Ch 27
Case sheet c16 · Handbook c16
17Design a message queue (Kafka-class)
Hard		Open: Draw an append-only log split into partitions, with replicas and consumer offsets.
Trade-off: Ordering only inside a partition (so it scales) versus global order (so it does not). acks=all (safe) versus acks=1 (fast).
Trap: Promising exactly-once without saying where duplicates are removed.		Ch 34, Ch 19, Ch 29

18Design a job scheduler
Medium		Open: Store jobs with a due time; workers claim due jobs with a lease.
Trade-off: Polling the database (simple) versus a time-bucketed queue (scales). Retry with backoff versus fail fast.
Trap: An in-memory timer that loses jobs on restart, and jobs that are not idempotent.		Ch 38, Ch 30, Ch 34
Case sheet c18 · Handbook c18
19Design metrics and log aggregation
Medium		Open: Separate collection, transport, storage, and query. Ask retention and alerting needs.
Trade-off: Push agents versus pull scraping. Full logs (costly) versus sampled or rolled-up metrics.
Trap: Unbounded label values (high cardinality) that blow up the metrics store.		Ch 54, Ch 37, Ch 19

20Design ad click aggregation
Hard		Open: State the numbers: clicks per second and how fresh the counts must be. Draw stream, window, store.
Trade-off: Stream processing (fresh, approximate) versus batch (exact, late). Often do both and reconcile.
Trap: Double counting from retries, and late events that arrive after the window closed.		Ch 37, Ch 34, Ch 32
Case sheet c20 · Handbook c20
21Design a distributed lock service
Hard		Open: Ask what the lock protects. Say a lock needs a lease, since clients crash.
Trade-off: Single Redis lock (simple) versus consensus-based lock (safe, slower). Fencing tokens protect against a slow old holder.
Trap: Believing a lease alone is safe. A paused client can wake up and still write.		Ch 30, Ch 29, Ch 27
Case sheet c21 · Handbook c21
22Decompose an e-commerce monolith
Hard		Open: Find business areas (catalog, cart, orders, payment) and who owns which data. Do not start with technology.
Trade-off: Split early (team independence) versus keep a modular monolith (simple). Strangler pattern to move piece by piece.
Trap: Services that still share one database, giving a distributed monolith.		Ch 44, Ch 45, Ch 46, Ch 48
Case sheet c22 · Handbook c22
23Design a ride-sharing service
Hard		Open: Split into location tracking, matching, trip state, and payment. Estimate location updates per second.
Trade-off: Match greedily per request (fast) versus batch matching every few seconds (better global result).
Trap: Writing every location ping to the main database.		Ch 58, Ch 06, Ch 48
Case sheet c23 · Handbook c23
24Design hotel booking
Medium		Open: Ask whether double booking is ever allowed (hotels sometimes overbook on purpose). Model room-night inventory.
Trade-off: Strong consistency on the booking write; eventual consistency is fine for search.
Trap: Locking by room instead of by room and date range.		Ch 17, Ch 31, Ch 30

25Design a digital wallet and ledger
Hard		Open: Start with a double-entry ledger: every transfer is two entries that sum to zero.
Trade-off: One database transaction (simple, limits scale) versus sagas across shards (scales, needs compensation).
Trap: Updating a balance field in place with no immutable history.		Ch 31, Ch 32, Ch 25

26Design a stock exchange
Hard		Open: State that order matching must be fast and in strict order. Think one matching engine per symbol.
Trade-off: Single-threaded in-memory matching with a log (fast, deterministic) versus distributed matching (harder).
Trap: Using a general database in the matching path.		Ch 29, Ch 34, Ch 08

27Design S3-style object storage
Hard		Open: Separate metadata from data. Ask durability target (eleven nines) and object size range.
Trade-off: Replication (simple, 3x cost) versus erasure coding (cheaper, slower repair).
Trap: Ignoring how metadata scales, and silent bit rot (needs checksums and scrubbing).		Ch 21, Ch 24, Ch 33

28Design a unique ID generator
Medium		Open: Ask: must IDs be sortable by time, and how many per second?
Trade-off: Snowflake style (time + machine + sequence) versus UUID (no coordination, not sortable) versus a ticket server.
Trap: Clock moving backward, and two machines with the same machine id.		Ch 32, Ch 27, Ch 57
Case sheet c28 · Handbook c28
29Design a maps and routing service
Hard		Open: Split into map tiles (served from CDN) and route search (graph algorithm). Ask scale of the road graph.
Trade-off: Plain Dijkstra (too slow) versus precomputed hierarchies (fast queries, costly updates).
Trap: Computing routes on the full world graph per request.		Ch 58, Ch 12, Ch 57
Case sheet c29 · Handbook c29
30Design a social graph
Medium		Open: Ask which queries: friends list, mutual friends, friend suggestions. Model edges.
Trade-off: Adjacency lists in a sharded store versus a graph database. Shard by user, but then mutual friends crosses shards.
Trap: Multi-hop queries at read time on a huge graph. Precompute or cap them.		Ch 17, Ch 25, Ch 23

31Design tweet search and trending topics
Hard		Open: Two problems: an inverted index for search and a sliding-window counter for trends.
Trade-off: Index in near real time (fresh, costly) versus in batches. Exact counts versus approximate (count-min sketch).
Trap: Treating trending as a plain counter, and ignoring spam and bots.		Ch 22, Ch 57, Ch 37

32Design a gaming leaderboard
Medium		Open: Ask size and update rate. A sorted set gives rank and top-N quickly.
Trade-off: One sorted set (simple, one node limit) versus sharded by score range (scales, rank gets harder).
Trap: Recomputing rank with a database sort on every request.		Ch 59, Ch 15, Ch 25

4. Concept deep-dive prompts (28)

One line: these are the questions asked after the design: why this store, what if it fails, how does it scale. Answer each in 60 to 90 seconds.
#Prompt and levelHintsStudy
33[Caching] Explain cache-aside versus write-through. When would you pick each?
Warm-up		Open: Say what each does on read and on write, in one sentence each.
Trade-off: Cache-aside is simple and tolerates cache loss; write-through keeps the cache fresh and costs write latency.
Trap: Claiming one is always better.		Ch 13
Quick note
34[Caching] The cache hit rate suddenly drops. What do you check?
Medium		Open: Ask what changed: a deploy, a key format, a flush, a node loss, a traffic shift.
Trade-off: Warming the cache slowly protects the database; refilling fast risks overload.
Trap: Jumping to "add more memory" before finding the cause.		Ch 14, Ch 15
Quick note
35[Caching] What is a cache stampede and how do you stop it?
Medium		Open: Define it: many requests miss together and all hit the database.
Trade-off: Request coalescing and jittered expiry versus locking (adds waiting).
Trap: Setting the same TTL on every key.		Ch 14
Quick note
36[Caching] How do you keep a cache and a database consistent?
Hard		Open: Say you cannot make both change at once, so pick delete-after-write plus a TTL safety net.
Trade-off: Short TTL (fresh, more misses) versus long TTL (stale risk).
Trap: Updating the cache before the database commit.		Ch 13, Ch 14, Ch 36
Quick note
37[Caching] One key gets a million reads per second. Now what?
Hard		Open: Name it a hot key. A single node cannot serve it.
Trade-off: Local in-process copy with a short TTL versus splitting the key into copies.
Trap: Adding nodes. One key still lives on one node.		Ch 60, Ch 14, Ch 15
Quick note
38[Sharding] How do you choose a shard key?
Medium		Open: List the queries first. The key should match the most common one and spread data evenly.
Trade-off: Hash (even, no range scans) versus range (scans, hot ends).
Trap: A key like the timestamp, which makes the newest shard hot.		Ch 25
Quick note
39[Sharding] How do you add shards without downtime?
Hard		Open: Use consistent hashing or many small logical shards mapped to few machines.
Trade-off: Move whole logical shards (simple) versus rehash everything (painful).
Trap: Mod-N hashing, which moves almost every key when N changes.		Ch 25, Ch 26
Quick note
40[Sharding] Replication versus sharding: when do you use each?
Warm-up		Open: Replication copies the same data for safety and read capacity; sharding splits data for size and write capacity.
Trade-off: Replication adds lag; sharding adds cross-shard queries.
Trap: Sharding when one primary with replicas would do.		Ch 24, Ch 25
Quick note
41[Sharding] A query needs data from several shards. How do you handle it?
Hard		Open: Scatter the query to the shards and gather results, or avoid it with a denormalised copy.
Trade-off: Scatter-gather latency is the slowest shard; a copy costs extra writes.
Trap: Cross-shard transactions as the default answer.		Ch 25, Ch 31
Quick note
42[Consistency] Explain CAP in plain words and give an example.
Medium		Open: During a network split you pick: answer possibly stale (available) or refuse (consistent).
Trade-off: A bank balance picks consistency; a like count picks availability.
Trap: Saying "pick two of three" as if it were always.		Ch 28
Quick note
43[Consistency] What is read-your-writes and how do you provide it?
Medium		Open: The user must see their own change right after making it.
Trade-off: Read from the leader for a short time, or pass a version token; costs leader load.
Trap: Reading from a lagging replica right after a write.		Ch 24, Ch 28
Quick note
44[Consistency] Why can a distributed lock be unsafe?
Hard		Open: A client may pause (garbage collection) and wake after its lease expired.
Trade-off: Fencing tokens make the storage reject old holders; costs a check on every write.
Trap: Trusting a timeout alone.		Ch 30, Ch 29, Ch 27
Quick note
45[Consistency] How do you order events across machines?
Hard		Open: Wall clocks drift, so use a logical order: sequence numbers per key or version vectors.
Trade-off: Single sequencer (simple, a bottleneck) versus logical clocks (scales, partial order).
Trap: Trusting timestamps from different servers.		Ch 27, Ch 32
Quick note
46[Messaging] Queue versus log (Kafka): how do you choose?
Medium		Open: Ask: does anyone need to replay, or are there many independent readers?
Trade-off: A queue is simpler for jobs; a log keeps history and fans out.
Trap: Using a log when you just need delayed retries.		Ch 34, Ch 35
Quick note
47[Messaging] How do you get exactly-once processing?
Hard		Open: Say delivery is at-least-once; you make processing idempotent.
Trade-off: Dedup table (cost: storage and lookup) versus naturally idempotent writes.
Trap: Promising exactly-once from the broker alone.		Ch 32, Ch 34, Ch 36
Quick note
48[Messaging] A consumer is falling behind. What do you do?
Medium		Open: Check lag, then the cause: slow handler, poison message, or too few partitions.
Trade-off: More consumers only help up to the partition count.
Trap: Adding consumers beyond the partitions.		Ch 34, Ch 35
Quick note
49[Messaging] How do you update a database and publish an event reliably?
Hard		Open: Name the dual-write problem. Use an outbox table written in the same transaction.
Trade-off: Outbox relay (a bit of delay) versus change data capture (more infrastructure).
Trap: Writing to the database, then publishing, with no recovery if the process dies between.		Ch 36, Ch 31, Ch 48
Quick note
50[Messaging] What do you do with messages that keep failing?
Medium		Open: Retry with backoff a few times, then move to a dead-letter queue.
Trade-off: Fast retry (quick, can overload) versus delayed (slow but gentle).
Trap: Retrying forever and blocking the partition.		Ch 34, Ch 35
Quick note
51[Microservices] When would you NOT use microservices?
Warm-up		Open: Small team, unclear boundaries, low scale: a modular monolith first.
Trade-off: Independence of teams versus network calls and distributed data.
Trap: Splitting by technical layer instead of business area.		Ch 39, Ch 44
Quick note
52[Microservices] How do you handle a transaction that spans services?
Hard		Open: Use a saga: local steps with undo steps.
Trade-off: Orchestration (clear flow, a central part) versus choreography (loose, hard to follow).
Trap: Using two-phase commit across many services.		Ch 48, Ch 46
Quick note
53[Microservices] How do services find and call each other safely?
Medium		Open: Discovery by name, with timeouts, retries and circuit breakers.
Trade-off: Sync calls (simple, coupled) versus events (loose, harder to debug).
Trap: Retries with no limit, causing a retry storm.		Ch 47, Ch 49, Ch 51
Quick note
54[Microservices] How do you migrate a monolith safely?
Hard		Open: Strangler pattern: route one capability at a time to the new service.
Trade-off: Shared database during transition (fast, couples) versus data copy via events (clean, slower).
Trap: A big-bang rewrite.		Ch 44, Ch 45, Ch 46
Quick note
55[Resilience] A downstream service is slow. How do you protect yourself?
Medium		Open: Timeouts first, then circuit breaker, bulkhead, and a fallback.
Trade-off: Fail fast (clean error) versus stale fallback (kinder, may mislead).
Trap: No timeout, so threads pile up.		Ch 40, Ch 51
Quick note
56[Resilience] Design for a whole region going down.
Hard		Open: Ask the RPO and RTO targets. They pick active-passive or active-active.
Trade-off: Active-active (fast recovery, conflicts) versus warm standby (cheaper, slower).
Trap: Never testing the failover.		Ch 41, Ch 26
Quick note
57[Resilience] How do you roll out a risky change?
Medium		Open: Canary a small share of traffic, watch the SLOs, and keep a quick rollback.
Trade-off: Slow rollout (safe) versus fast (less time at mixed versions).
Trap: A schema change that old code cannot read.		Ch 43, Ch 54
Quick note
58[Estimation] Estimate the storage for a photo app with 100M users.
Warm-up		Open: State assumptions aloud: uploads per user per day, size per photo, years kept.
Trade-off: Keep originals plus thumbnails versus originals only.
Trap: Mixing bits and bytes, or forgetting replication.		Ch 02
Quick note
59[Estimation] How many servers do you need for 50,000 requests per second?
Medium		Open: Peak QPS divided by per-server capacity, plus headroom.
Trade-off: Fewer big servers versus many small ones.
Trap: Using average instead of peak traffic.		Ch 02, Ch 08
Quick note
60[Estimation] How do you size a cache?
Medium		Open: Use the hot 20 percent of keys times object size.
Trade-off: Bigger cache (higher hit rate, more cost) versus tighter.
Trap: Forgetting memory overhead per key.		Ch 02, Ch 13
Quick note
Trap: practising only the famous cases. Interviewers often start from a case and then ask a concept question about it (for example, "your cache just died, what happens?"). Mix both lists in one session.

6. Practice plans Ch 61

One line: choose the 3-day crash plan or the 4-week plan. These are menus, not demands: skip what you already know.

3-day crash plan

  • Day 1, foundations: read the last-hour sheet. Do four warm-up cases aloud (URL shortener, rate limiter, pastebin, ID generator) and four caching and sharding concept prompts.
  • Day 2, depth: do four medium cases (news feed, chat, notification system, job scheduler) and the consistency and messaging concept prompts. Score each run with the rubric.
  • Day 3, hard and mock: do two hard cases (payment system, ticket booking) with a full 45 minute timer, ideally with a friend. Read your two lowest rubric rows again. Sleep early.

4-week plan

  • Week 1: framework and estimation. Do the estimation prompts and warm-up cases. Learn the 45 minute split by heart.
  • Week 2: data layer. Caching, sharding, replication, consistency prompts. Cases: key-value store, distributed cache, hotel booking, leaderboard.
  • Week 3: async and microservices. Messaging, microservices, resilience prompts. Cases: message queue, notification system, e-commerce decomposition, digital wallet.
  • Week 4: full mocks. Two or three mixed 45 minute runs, one hard case per run, plus a repeat of your weakest case from week 1.
Say (to yourself): "One prompt, one timer, two fixes." Short loops beat long study days.

7. Say-aloud script (60 seconds)

Say: "Let me restate the problem and ask a few questions: who are the users, how many, how fresh must reads be, and what can we leave out. I will assume 100 million daily users, mostly reads, and tell you what changes if that is wrong. Quick numbers: about 1,000 writes a second and ten times that for reads at peak. That fits one primary with replicas, so the first bottleneck is reads, and I will add a cache. Here is the simplest design, with numbered flows. The riskiest part is the hot key, so let me go deep there. The cache gives us fast reads, costs us staleness, and I pick it because the product allows a few seconds of lag. If the cache dies, requests fall through to the database, so I add request coalescing. To finish: here are the two risks I would watch and how I would monitor them."

8. Interview Q&A (the trickiest follow-ups)

A. Case follow-ups

You said "use Redis". Why not just the database?

Because I measured it. If reads are about 10,000 a second and the same few thousand items are asked for again and again, a cache answers from memory in under a millisecond and leaves the database nearly idle. I would still keep the database as the source of truth. The cost is staleness and one more thing to run. If the numbers were small, say 200 reads a second, I would skip the cache and keep the design simple. The point to say aloud is that every box has a number that justifies it.

Your design works for 1 million users. What breaks at 100 million?

I redo the estimate first, because the answer depends on which number grew. Usually storage grows past one node and write rates pass one primary, so sharding becomes needed. Reads move to caches and replicas. I would name the first thing to break, such as the single database primary at a few thousand writes a second, and fix that one thing. I would not redesign everything. Then I would say which monitoring number tells me it is time.

How do you avoid a double charge in a payment flow?

The client sends a unique idempotency key with the request. The server inserts that key first with a unique constraint. If the same key arrives again, the server returns the saved result instead of charging again. The provider call also carries the key, so a retry after a timeout is safe. I add a reconciliation job that compares my records with the provider settlement file to catch anything that slipped. Money is stored as whole cents, never as floating point.

Two people click the last seat at the same instant. What happens?

The seat has a state and a version. The first request moves it from free to held in one atomic step: a conditional update that only works if the seat is still free. The second request fails that condition and gets a clear "taken" answer. The hold has a timer longer than the payment timeout, so a slow payment does not lose the seat. If the hold expires, the confirm step must also check the hold itself, not rely only on a background sweeper.

A celebrity posts to 50 million followers. How does the feed work?

Pushing one post into 50 million lists would be a huge burst of writes. So for very large accounts I store the post once and do not push it. When a follower opens their feed, I take their pre-built list and merge in recent posts from the celebrities they follow. Normal users still use push, so reads stay one lookup. The cost is two code paths and a slightly slower read for people who follow many celebrities. Deletes need a filter at read time.

B. Design scenarios

The interviewer says "now make it multi-region". What do you do?

I restate what the change breaks: latency across regions and where writes go. I ask whether users need to write in every region or only read. The smallest step is read replicas and a CDN in each region with one write region. If writes must be local, I split users by home region so most writes stay local, and accept conflict rules for the rest. I say the cost: more lag, more cases to test. I would not rebuild from zero.

How would you handle one huge customer who slows everyone else?

This is the noisy neighbour problem. First, per-tenant rate limits and quotas so one customer cannot take all capacity. Second, separate queues or pools so their load cannot block others. For the biggest customers, give them their own cell, a full separate copy of the stack. I also put the tenant id in every log, metric and cache key, so I can see who is causing load. The cost is more units to operate and some wasted spare capacity.

How do you keep search results fresh without slowing writes?

The database stays the source of truth. A change-data-capture stream, or an outbox table, publishes each committed change as an event. An indexer reads the events and updates the search index. Writes stay fast because search is updated after the fact, usually within seconds. I accept that search can be a little behind. A periodic comparison job finds and repairs drift. I never write to both the database and the index from the app, because one write can fail and they would disagree.

Walk me through uploading a 4 GB file.

The client asks my service for an upload session and gets presigned URLs for parts of about 8 MB. It uploads parts straight to object storage, several at once, so my servers never carry the bytes. The server tracks which parts arrived, so after a failure the client resumes only the missing ones. A final call joins the parts and marks the file visible, so no reader sees half a file. A content hash lets me skip data I already hold. The trap is routing the bytes through app servers.

How do you rate limit across 20 gateway servers?

If each server counts alone, the user could get 20 times the limit. So counters live in a shared fast store such as Redis, and the check is one atomic script: read, add, compare. That is exact but adds a network hop. For less cost I can let each server take a slice of the budget and sync every second, which is approximate but very fast. I choose based on how strict the limit must be. I also return 429 with a retry-after header.

C. Failures and production incidents

The cache cluster restarts and the database falls over. Why, and what do you do now?

All keys vanished at once, so every request missed and went to the database, which was sized only for the miss rate of a warm cache. Right now I would shed load: rate limit the miss path, serve a stale or reduced page, and bring traffic back gradually. I would warm the hottest keys first. Afterwards I add request coalescing, jittered TTLs, and a limit on database concurrency. I also test cache-down as a drill, so this is no surprise next time.

Consumers are 2 hours behind on the queue. What do you check?

First I look at whether lag is growing or flat. If growing, the producer rate is higher than the consume rate. I look for a slow handler (a new dependency call), a poison message that is retrying forever, or fewer consumers after a deploy. Adding consumers only helps up to the number of partitions. Short term, I scale consumers, park the poison message in a dead-letter queue, and shed low-priority work. Long term I alert on lag growth, not only on lag size.

After a failover, some acknowledged writes are gone. How can that happen?

With asynchronous replication, the leader acknowledges a write before the follower has it. If the leader dies and that follower becomes leader, the last few writes are lost. The lost amount is the replication lag at the time. I could use semi-synchronous replication so at least one follower has the write before we acknowledge, which costs latency. Or I accept the loss for data where that is fine, and say so. The key is to state the recovery point target openly.

A downstream service is slow and now your whole API is slow. Why?

Threads or connections are stuck waiting on the slow service. New requests queue behind them, so everything slows, even routes that never use that service. The fix is layers: a timeout on every call, a circuit breaker that stops calling when failures pass a threshold, and a bulkhead, a separate pool for that dependency so it cannot use all threads. I also add a fallback, such as cached data. Retries must have a budget, or they make it worse.

Users report seeing deleted posts. Where would you look?

Deleted content shown means a copy that was not cleaned up. I would check caches, pre-built feed lists, search indexes, and replicas that lag. The common fix is a tombstone: the delete is recorded, and the read path filters out any id that is marked deleted. Then background cleanup removes the copies later. I would also check whether the delete event was lost on the way to the index, and add a reconciliation job.

D. Trade-offs and why-questions

Why not exactly-once delivery?

In a network that can lose or delay messages, the sender cannot know if a message arrived, so it must retry, and retries create duplicates. Systems that say exactly-once really mean at-least-once delivery plus a way to ignore duplicates. So I design the handler to be idempotent: use a message id and a dedup record, or make the write naturally repeatable, such as a set-status or upsert. That is simpler and more honest than trusting a broker promise.

Why would you pick eventual consistency on purpose?

Because it buys speed and availability, and many features do not need more. A like count that is a few seconds behind harms nobody. Eventual consistency lets replicas answer locally, lets writes succeed during a network split, and keeps latency low across regions. I would never use it for money, stock counts, or unique names. I decide per feature, not per system: checkout strong, recommendations eventual.

Why start with a modular monolith instead of microservices?

Microservices cost a lot: network calls that fail, data split across services, hard debugging, and a platform to run. They pay off with many teams, different scaling needs, and clear boundaries. With a small team and unclear boundaries, the wrong split is expensive to undo. A monolith with clean modules and separate data ownership inside gives most of the benefit and lets me split later along lines I now understand. I would split out the first piece that truly needs independent scaling.

Hash sharding or range sharding?

Hash sharding spreads keys evenly, so no machine is hot and load balances well. Its cost is that range scans touch all shards. Range sharding keeps neighbours together so scans are cheap, but a growing key like time puts all new writes on the last shard. I pick hash for key lookups and write-heavy workloads, and range when scans are the main query and the key is not always increasing. Consistent hashing or many small logical shards make later resizing painless.

What do you do when the interviewer disagrees with a trade-off?

I restate their concern in my own words to show I heard it. Then I answer with a number or a requirement: "At 50,000 writes a second one primary becomes the limit, so I shard." If they show me a requirement I missed, I thank them and change the design out loud, and say what changes downstream. I do not argue for the sake of it, and I do not give in without a reason. Showing that I can update is a strong signal.

You have 5 minutes left and the design has a gap. What do you do?

I say the gap plainly: "I have not covered failure handling." Then I use the die, slow, lie questions for the two most important boxes and give one-sentence answers with a recovery time. After that I give a three-sentence summary and list what I left out and the top risks. An honest, short close is better than rushing through everything or leaving the gap unmentioned.
