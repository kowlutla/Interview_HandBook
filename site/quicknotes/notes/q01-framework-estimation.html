Interview framework and estimation

Revision sheet for the day before the interview: how to run the hour, and how to do the numbers. Full explanations: Handbook Chapters 1 and 2.

0. Key definitions (know these cold)

TermDefinition
Functional requirementWhat the system does: a verb such as "upload a photo" or "follow a user". Keep the list to 3 to 5 core items.
Non-functional requirementHow well it must do it: scale, speed, uptime, consistency, durability, security, cost. This is what changes the architecture.
ScopeThe slice of the product you agree to design in the hour. Say what is in, nice to have, and out.
AssumptionA guess you say out loud so it becomes a shared fact (for example "100 million daily users"). It lets you justify later choices.
Deep diveGoing one level deeper on the 2 or 3 hardest parts of the design: the mechanism, the options, the cost, the choice.
TradeoffEvery choice gains something and loses something. State both, tied to the requirements.
Back-of-the-envelope estimateQuick rough arithmetic to find the order of magnitude (the power of ten) of load and data. Not exact on purpose.
DAUDaily active users: distinct people who use the product on one day. Better base than total registered users.
QPSQueries (requests) per second reaching a component. Also called RPS. The central sizing number.
Peak factorHow many times the busiest moment exceeds the daily average. Typically 2 to 3 for a consumer app, 10 to 100 for sale or news bursts.
Read:write ratioHow many reads per write. Reads scale by copying (cache, replicas, CDN); writes scale by splitting (sharding) or by a write-friendly store.
Blob vs metadataBlob: big file such as a photo or video (dominates bytes). Metadata: small record about it (dominates query traffic). Stored in different systems.
Replication factorHow many copies of the data we keep. 3 is common, so raw disk is 3 times the real data.
Erasure codingSplitting an object into data pieces plus extra parity pieces so some can be lost. About 1.3 to 1.5 times storage instead of 3 times.
LatencyHow long one operation takes from start to finish.
ThroughputHow much work finishes per second (requests or MB per second). Different from latency.
Percentile (p50, p95, p99)p99 is the time that 99 out of 100 requests beat. Shows how bad the slow tail is. Averages hide it.
Tail latencyThe slow end of the latency distribution (p99, p99.9). Gets worse when one request waits for many parallel calls (fan-out).
SLO / SLASLO: internal target ("99% of requests under 300 ms"). SLA: a promise to customers, often with penalties.
Availability and the ninesFraction of time (or of requests) the system works. 99.9% is "three nines", about 8.76 hours of downtime a year.
Error budgetThe unreliability the SLO allows. At 99.9% you may fail 1 request in 1,000 (about 43 minutes a month).
DurabilityChance that stored data is not lost over time. Different from availability ("can I read it now?").
HeadroomSpare capacity above the expected peak, for failures, deploys, and forecast error.
Little's LawL = arrival rate x time in system. Gives how many requests are in flight at once.
Hit ratioShare of requests answered by a cache. 90% to 99% cuts the database load ten times.

1. The interview framework Ch 01

One line: a design interview is a conversation that tests your process and judgement, not recall of one right architecture. Follow a fixed eight-step order so you never wonder what to do next.

What the interviewer scores

How you approach it

  • Problem exploration: do you ask questions before drawing?
  • Structured thinking: can they always tell where you are?
  • Communication: clear, takes hints, not defensive.

Quality of decisions

  • Technical depth: how it works inside, and how it fails.
  • Breadth and judgement: the right block, with a reason.
  • Tradeoffs: both the gain and the cost, tied to requirements.

Mindset

  • No single right answer: you are asked "how do you decide?".
  • The interviewer is usually on your side. A hard question is a door to show depth.
  • Deriving beats reciting a memorised design.

The eight steps in one table

StepWhat you doWhat to leave on the board
1. RequirementsAsk grouped questions: features, scale, read or write heavy, latency, consistency, durability, regions. Offer defaults so they can say "yes". Read the summary back.3 to 5 features, 2 or 3 quality targets, out-of-scope list, assumptions
2. EstimationDAU to QPS, peak, storage, bandwidth, cache. End with a sentence about what it means for the design.QPS (average and peak), storage, bandwidth, a conclusion
3. APIOne operation per core feature. Mention pagination (cursors), idempotency for retries, auth, protocol.3 to 5 calls with inputs and outputs
4. Data modelEntities, then access patterns (how data is looked up), then keys and store choice with a one-line reason.Entities, keys, store per dataset
5. High-level designDraw the smallest path first, then add parts for named reasons. Trace one read and one write.Diagram of 5 to 10 boxes with numbered flows
6. Deep divesPick where the non-functional needs bite hardest (feed fan-out, delivery guarantees, counting algorithm). Mechanism, options, cost, choice.2 or 3 detailed mechanisms
7. Bottlenecks and failureSingle points of failure, hot keys, cold cache, node or region loss, doubled traffic. Add monitoring.Risks with mitigations
8. Wrap-upDecisions with reasons, known limits, what you would do next.A clean last impression
Say: "I will first confirm requirements and scale, then do quick numbers, then API and data model, then the architecture, then I will go deep on the one or two hardest parts and finish with failures and a summary."

1a. Requirements, scope and assumptions Ch 01

One line: scale and quality needs, not the feature list, decide the architecture. A notes app for ten users and for ten million users do the same things and share almost no design.
DimensionPlain meaningQuestion to ask
ScaleHow many users, requests, bytes"How many daily users, and what is the read-to-write ratio?"
LatencyHow fast one request must finish, usually at p99"Do we need an answer in 200 ms or is a few seconds fine?"
AvailabilityHow much of the time it must work"If it is down for ten minutes, how bad is that?"
ConsistencyDo all users see the same data at once?"If I post and my friend refreshes, must they see it now?"
DurabilityMay we ever lose accepted data?"Can an acknowledged write ever be lost?"
Security, costWho sees what; budget and team size"Is the data regulated? Two engineers or a platform team?"
  • Ask questions whose answers change the design. "Photos and videos?" changes storage. "Logo colour?" does not.
  • Group them: features first, then scale, then quality. Listen for where the interviewer spends words: that is where they want depth.
  • "You decide" is a test. Pick a sensible number, say it, ask for a nod.
  • Three buckets: must have (design fully), nice to have (mention), out of scope (say so on purpose).
  • Classify the system aloud: read-heavy (cache, replicas, CDN), write-heavy (append-only, batching, queues), real-time (long-lived connections), data-processing (batch and stream), transactional (strong consistency, idempotency).
Trap: scoping away the hard part. If the prompt is "design a chat system" and you drop group chat, delivery guarantees and offline users, nothing is left to discuss. Ask: "Is group chat part of what you want to see?"
Say: "I will assume 50 million daily users, about 100 reads for every write, a page under 300 ms at p99, and that a feed a few seconds stale is fine. Does that sound reasonable?"

1b. Time budget Ch 01

One line: the two common failures are spending 20 minutes on requirements and numbers, or drawing with no foundation. A budget keeps both in check.

  • Watch for tarpits. Estimation and database choice eat time. Round and move on: "about 40 TB a year, close enough".
  • Signpost transitions ("I have the numbers, now the API and data model") so the interviewer can redirect you early.
  • Let them steer: after the high-level design ask "I can go deep on feed generation, storage, or failure handling. Which would you like?"
  • Protect the end. Even if a deep dive runs long, keep two minutes for the summary.

1c. Drawing and narrating Ch 01

One line: start with the smallest working path, then add each part only with a sentence that starts with "because".
  • Conventions: clients left, then entry points (DNS, CDN, load balancer, gateway), services, data stores right. Dashed arrows for asynchronous flows (queued, done later), solid for calls where the caller waits.
  • Narrate: say what you will draw, draw it, say what it does. Silent drawing leaves them waiting; silent thinking looks stuck.
  • Trace a real request end to end by pointing, one read and one write. This exposes gaps like data with nowhere to go.
  • Pause for contact: "Does this match what you had in mind? Shall I do the write path?"
  • If you find a mistake, say it plainly and fix only what it touches. Smooth self-correction scores well.

1d. Tradeoffs, mistakes, and seniority Ch 01

One line: state a tradeoff as options, criteria, choice with reason, and the cost plus the trigger to revisit.
Say: "There are two ways to build the feed: at write time or at read time. What matters is read latency and celebrity accounts. I precompute for ordinary users because reads become one lookup, and merge at read time for celebrities. The cost is extra storage and a harder read path; if most users turn out to be inactive I would revisit."

What senior and lead add

  • Raise the non-obvious: hot keys, thundering herd after cache flush, idempotency under retries, skewed partitions, clock drift, compatibility during deploys.

Mini example: page-view counter (the framework in 60 seconds)

Numbers: 1 billion events a day, about 10,000 per second average, peak about 30,000 per second. Raw event about 100 bytes, so 100 GB a day. Aggregates for 50 million pages at about 300 bytes are only about 15 GB. Conclusion: the hard part is writes and a hot page, not storage or reads. So: accept the event fast (return 202), put it in a durable partitioned log, aggregate in batches of a few seconds, write one combined increment per page, cache dashboard reads for 30 seconds. Make batches idempotent with a batch id so a replay is not double counted.

2. Estimation Ch 02

One line: estimation gets the order of magnitude right so the design fits the problem. A number that changes no decision was not worth computing.

Why it matters: at a thousand users one small server is enough; at a billion users a single database cannot even hold the data. Being off by 30% does not change the design; mistaking 50,000 for 50 or for 5,000,000 does.

Units and numbers to memorise

ItemValue
Size ladderKB, MB, GB, TB, PB each 1,000 times the last (really 1,024; the error is small)
Quick storage tricks1 million items x 1 KB = 1 GB. 1 billion x 1 KB = 1 TB. 1 billion x 1 MB = 1 PB. Add the exponents.
Seconds per day86,400 (round to 100,000, error about 14%)
Seconds per yearabout 31.5 million (round to 3 x 107)
Rule of thumb1 million per day is about 12 per second. 1 billion per day is about 11,600 per second.
Bits vs bytes1 Gbps is about 125 MB/s; 10 Gbps is about 1.25 GB/s. Multiply bytes by 8 to compare with links.
Trap: forgetting the factor of 8 between bytes and bits is the commonest bandwidth slip. Write units next to every number.

Latency numbers (ratios matter more than exact values)

OperationTypical timeLesson
CPU cache (L1)about 1 nsEffectively free
Main memory readabout 100 nsSpeed of an in-memory lookup
Send 1 KB on 1 Gbpsabout 10 microsecondsWire time for small data
SSD random read (4 KB)about 20 to 150 microsecondsAbout 1,000 times slower than memory

  • Network round trips dominate: 20 calls in a row inside one data centre is at least 10 ms before any real work. Batch and run in parallel.
  • Geography is not negotiable: Singapore to Virginia is well over 150 ms per round trip. Put data near users.
  • Compression is almost always worth it for network transfer: a few microseconds to compress 1 KB.
  • These are rules of thumb. Cloud networks vary, and a call through a busy load balancer can take several ms. Use them to compare options, not to promise a response time.

2a. From users to requests per second Ch 02

One line: total per day = DAU x actions per user; divide by 86,400 for the average; multiply by the peak factor to size.
Worked: 50 million DAU x 20 requests = 1 billion a day, about 11,600 QPS average. Peak factor 3 gives about 35,000 peak QPS.
  • Start from DAU, not registered users. DAU is roughly 10% to 60% of MAU depending on how habit-forming the product is. If given total users, state what fraction is active.
  • Split actions into reads and writes and estimate each. They have different costs, sizes, and scaling.
  • Peak factor: 2 to 3 for a consumer app in a few time zones; 1.5 to 2 for a worldwide product; 10 to 100 for ticket sales, flash sales, breaking news, game launches; business software has a 9-to-5 shape (3 to 5 over a week).
  • Size for the peak. Then add headroom on top. Peak factor (shape of demand) and headroom (failures, deploys, forecast error) are two separate multipliers.

Read:write ratio decides where to invest

SystemTypical ratioConsequence
Social feed, photo viewing100:1 to 1000:1Cache, CDN, read replicas, precomputed feeds
URL shortenerabout 100:1Cache hot mappings; the write path is tiny
Chatclose to 1:1Write path matters as much as read
Metrics and loggingwrite-heavy, 1:0.01 or lessAppend-only, batched ingestion
E-commerce catalogueabout 1000:1 browsing; checkout is write-criticalDifferent paths, different treatment

Reads scale by copying (caches, replicas, CDN: cheap, with some staleness). Writes scale by splitting (sharding or a write-optimised store): more copies make writes worse. Watch for hidden fan-out: one celebrity post can become millions of timeline writes.

2b. Storage, bandwidth and memory Ch 02

One line: storage = objects per day x size x 365 x years x copies. Bandwidth = peak QPS x payload. Cache = hot fraction x items x size.
WhatRecipeGotchas
StorageNew objects per day (writes only) x size incl. metadata x 365 x years x replicationAdd 30% to 100% for indexes, free space (do not run disks above 70% to 80%), backups. Reads create no storage.
BandwidthQPS x payload bytes, then x 8 for bits. Peak QPS sizes links; average QPS sizes monthly cost.Ingress (uploads) and egress (downloads) differ; egress is bigger and is what clouds charge for. Headers add 0.5 to 2 KB per request.
Cache memoryDistinct items per day x item size x fraction cached (start at 20%), plus 20% to 30% overheadHit ratio matters more than size. Also count per-connection memory (about 10 KB per socket).
Photo app (100M DAU): 10 million uploads a day at 2.5 MB (original plus resized copies) = 25 TB a day, about 9.1 PB a year logical, about 27 PB with 3 copies, about 13.7 PB with erasure coding (about 1.5x). Metadata 500 B each = 5 GB a day, about 1.8 TB a year. Views: 4 billion a day, about 46,300 per second average, about 139,000 at peak, read:write 400:1. Egress at peak: 139,000 x 200 KB is about 28 GB/s, about 222 Gbps; with a 90% CDN hit ratio the origin sees about 22 Gbps (at 50% it would be 111 Gbps).
  • Blobs vs metadata: blobs dominate bytes (object store, tiering, erasure coding); metadata dominates queries (sharded database plus cache).
  • Hot, warm, cold: recent data on SSD, older on cheaper disk, oldest on archive. Say how much is hot; the fast tier need not hold everything.
  • Hit ratio: 90% to 99% cuts database load 10 times (10% of traffic to 1%). At 35,000 peak QPS reads: 3,500 DB reads at 90%, 350 at 99%.
  • Cache sizing example: 500 million distinct profiles a day x 1 KB, cache 20% = 100 GB, about 3 to 6 nodes.
Say: "That is 222 Gbps at peak, far above one edge link, so images must come from a CDN with a high hit ratio. The CDN hit ratio is the most important performance number here."

Chat service in one box

Chat (50M DAU): 40 messages each = 2 billion a day, about 23,000 per second average, about 70,000 at peak; 2 recipients gives about 140,000 deliveries per second at peak. Stored message about 200 B, so 400 GB a day, about 146 TB a year, about 2.2 PB for 5 years with 3 copies. Peak 10 million connections at about 10 KB each = 100 GB; at 50,000 per gateway server that is 200, about 300 with headroom. Bandwidth about 340 Mbps. Conclusion: chat is limited by open connections and fan-out, not by bandwidth or CPU.

2c. Servers and headroom Ch 02

One line: servers = peak QPS / (per-server capacity x target utilisation), then inflate for zone failure.
ComponentRough capacity (say it as an assumption)
Stateless app server (calls DB and cache)about 500 to 5,000 requests per second (5 ms CPU on 16 cores is about 3,200 per second)
Small cached or static responses, event-driven10,000 to 100,000+ per second
Relational database nodea few thousand to 10,000+ simple queries per second; writes lower
In-memory cache node100,000+ simple operations per second
WebSocket gatewaylimited by connections: 50,000 to a few hundred thousand per server
Worked: peak 139,000 QPS, one server 2,000 per second, target 60% utilisation: usable 1,200 per second, so about 116 servers. To survive losing 1 of 3 zones multiply by 3/2: about 174. Round up for deploy slack: about 180, 60 per zone.
  • Why headroom: latency climbs steeply near 100% utilisation; failures remove capacity; deploys pull servers out; forecasts are wrong. Aim for 50% to 70% CPU at peak, or 1.5 to 2 times the raw peak need.
  • Say when unsure: "I will assume one server handles about 1,000 requests per second; a load test would confirm."

2d. Percentiles and tail latency Ch 02

One line: latency is a spread of values, not one number. Use p50 for the typical case and p95, p99, p99.9 for how bad it gets.
  • Average misleads. 99 requests at 100 ms plus one at 10 s average to 199 ms, which describes nobody. The median says 100 ms and hides the problem; p99 shows it.
  • Many requests per page. 50 requests, each 1% slow: 1 minus 0.9950 is about 39.5% of page loads hit a slow one.
  • Fan-out amplifies the tail. Wait for 100 parallel backends, each slow 1% of the time: about 63% of requests hit at least one slow one. The overall median approaches the single-server p99.
  • Tame the tail: timeouts and bounded retries; hedged requests (send a second copy after the p95 time, use the first answer, about 5% extra load); remove pauses (garbage collection, noisy neighbours, cold caches); keep utilisation moderate.
  • SLO vs SLA: SLO is the internal goal ("99% under 300 ms"), SLA the contract.
Trap: you cannot average percentiles. Two servers with p99 of 200 ms and 400 ms do not give a fleet p99 of 300 ms. Merge raw samples or mergeable histograms.

2e. Availability and durability Ch 02

One line: each extra nine is 10 times less downtime and roughly 10 times more effort. Series parts multiply availability; redundant parts multiply unavailability.
AvailabilityDowntime per yearPer monthPer day
99% (two nines)3.65 days7.2 hours14.4 min
99.9% (three)8.76 hours43.2 min1.44 min
99.95%4.38 hours21.6 min43 s
99.99% (four)52.6 min4.32 min8.6 s
99.999% (five)5.26 min25.9 s0.86 s
  • At four nines you have under an hour a year: shorter than a human page, log in, diagnose, fix cycle. So recovery must be automated (health checks, failover, automatic rollback).
  • Request-based availability: failing 0.1% of requests evenly is 99.9% even though you were never fully down. Error budget is what the SLO allows; when it is spent, slow down launches and fix reliability.
Trap: the parallel formula needs independent failures. Two replicas in one rack, on one power feed, running one buggy release fail together. Spread across zones and regions; shared parts (load balancer, DNS, config service) cap the whole system.

Durability (not the same as availability)

  • Availability asks "can I read it now?". Durability asks "will it still exist next year?". Down but durable is fine; up but losing data is worse.
  • Eleven nines (99.999999999% a year): store 10 million objects and expect to lose about one every 10,000 years. A design target, and it does not protect you from your own deletes or bad overwrites.
  • How: replication across disks, racks, zones; erasure coding (for example 10 data plus 4 parity, any 10 of 14 rebuild the object, 1.4x overhead, more CPU and network on repair, best for large cold objects); write acknowledged only after enough replicas hit stable storage (quorum), not memory on one machine; checksums and scrubbing against silent bit rot; backups and versioning (replication copies mistakes instantly; restore must be tested); another region for site loss.
  • Source of truth vs derived: spend the durability budget on the source of truth. Caches, search indexes and thumbnails can be rebuilt.

2f. Little's Law and throughput Ch 02

One line: L = arrival rate x time in system. Requests in flight equals requests per second times seconds each one stays.
UseCalculation
Threads per server5,000 per second x 0.2 s = 1,000 in flight; at 200 threads each, at least 5 servers just to hold the concurrency
DB connection pool2,000 queries per second x 5 ms = 10 busy connections; a pool of 20 to 30 is comfortable; 1,000 is wasteful and hurts the database
Chat connections50M users, 30-minute sessions: 580 sessions per second x 1,800 s is about 1.04 million average connections
Max throughputThroughput = concurrency / latency. 50 connections at 100 ms gives at most 500 per second
  • Throughput vs latency (highway): lanes raise throughput, not one car's trip time. Batching raises throughput but adds waiting; caching improves both; parallelism raises throughput until a shared resource saturates.
Say: "Arrival rate times time in the system gives the concurrency. If someone claims 10,000 per second at 500 ms latency, that means 5,000 requests held at once, so I check that we have the threads and memory for it."

Estimation habits that score

  1. State inputs first and invite correction.
  2. Round hard (100,000 seconds a day, 1,000 not 1,024).
  3. Separate reads from writes, average from peak, and say which you size for.
  4. Estimate the dominant resource: photos are storage and bandwidth, chat is connections, search is index memory.
  5. End every estimate with a design consequence.
  6. Sanity check against known machines: 64 to 512 GB RAM, tens of TB disk, 10 to 100 Gbps network card.
  7. Keep it to about five minutes.
Common slips: sizing for average; mixing bits and bytes or per day and per second; forgetting replication; quoting average latency when the goal is a percentile; computing precisely and never using the answer.

3. Say-aloud script (45 seconds)

  1. "Let me confirm the problem first. Core features are these three, and I will leave out these two. Does that match?"
  2. "I will assume 50 million daily users, mostly reads at about 100 to 1, a page under 300 ms at p99, and a few seconds of staleness is fine."
  3. "Quick numbers: 50 million users x 20 requests is a billion a day, about 12,000 a second on average, about 35,000 at peak. Storage is small, so the hard part is read load. That tells me cache and replicas, not sharding yet."
  4. "API has three calls; data is a user, a post, and a follow relation, keyed by what we look up."
  5. "I will start with client, load balancer, stateless app servers, cache, database, and add each part because a number or requirement asks for it."
  6. "The two hardest parts are fan-out and hot keys, so I will go deep there: options, cost, my choice."
  7. "Then failures: what if the cache is cold or a zone is lost. I will finish with a summary, the tradeoffs I accepted, and what I would do next."

4. Interview Q&A (detailed answers)

Click a question to open the answer. Grouped as: concepts, design scenarios, failure and real-time situations, and trade-off or "why" questions.

A. Concept questions

1. What is a system design interviewer actually evaluating?

They are asking whether they could trust you to lead a team from a vague business problem to a sound design. So they watch your process at least as much as your final diagram. The signals are problem exploration (do you ask questions first), structured thinking (can they follow where you are), technical depth (do you know how a part works and fails), breadth and judgement (right building block, with a reason), tradeoff reasoning, and communication. There is no single right answer: two candidates can pick different databases and both pass if each justifies the choice against the agreed requirements. A textbook design for the wrong problem fails, because you ignored the requirements you collected yourself.

2. What is the difference between functional and non-functional requirements, and which matters more for the architecture?

Functional requirements say what the system does: upload a photo, follow a user, request a ride. Non-functional requirements say how well: how many users, how fast, how available, how consistent, how durable, how secure, how expensive. The non-functional ones drive the architecture, because two products with identical features but different scale or consistency needs look completely different. A notes app for ten users and for ten million users do the same things and share almost no design. In practice I list three to five features, then pick the two or three quality targets that will actually shape the design, such as consistency for payments or latency for live video.

3. List the eight steps of your framework and say why you use a fixed order.

Requirements and scope, estimation, API, data model, high-level design, deep dives, bottlenecks and failure, and wrap-up. A fixed order matters because when I am nervous it frees me from deciding what to do next, so all attention goes to content. It also helps the interviewer, who can always see where I am and redirect me early. The order is a default, not a law: I may compress steps or go back when a deep dive shows an early assumption was wrong. What I must always know is which step I am in. I write the step names small at the side of the board and tick them off.

4. Why do we estimate, and how precise should it be?

We estimate to learn the order of magnitude of the problem, because designs change at powers of ten: 100 requests a second fits one machine, 100,000 does not, and 10 million needs a different structure. It also shows which resource runs out first (storage, bandwidth, memory, connections, or request rate) so I know where the design is hard. Precision beyond a factor of two or three is wasted, since my inputs are guesses anyway. I round hard, for example 100,000 seconds a day, and I always finish with a sentence about what the number means for the design. A number that changes no decision was not worth computing.

5. Walk me through turning daily active users into QPS.

I multiply daily active users by actions per user per day to get requests per day, and I do this separately for reads and writes. Then I divide by 86,400 seconds, rounding to about 100,000 for speed. For example 50 million users x 20 requests is 1 billion a day, which is about 11,600 per second on average. Averages are almost useless for sizing, so I multiply by a peak factor of 2 to 3 for a consumer app, about 35,000 per second here, and much more for bursty events such as sales. A handy rule is that 1 million a day is about 12 per second.

6. What is the difference between latency and throughput?

Latency is how long one operation takes from start to finish. Throughput is how many operations finish per second. On a highway, latency is one car's trip time and throughput is cars per hour. More lanes raise throughput without making one trip faster. Batching raises throughput because the fixed cost per operation is shared, but each item waits for the batch, so latency goes up. Caching can improve both. I use the distinction in interviews to say which one a requirement is about: an interactive user cares about p99 latency, a background pipeline cares about throughput.

7. Why are percentiles better than averages for latency?

The average is dominated by the many fast requests and hides slow outliers. If 99 requests take 100 ms and one takes 10 seconds, the average is 199 ms, which matches nobody, while p99 shows the problem. The p50 tells me the typical experience and p95, p99, p99.9 tell me how bad it gets, which is what SLOs are written against. The tail matters more than it seems because a page makes many requests (with 50 requests and a 1% slow rate, about 40% of page loads hit a slow one) and because fan-out makes the slowest backend set the total time. Also, percentiles cannot be averaged across servers; I merge raw samples or histograms.

8. Explain availability nines and what they mean in minutes.

Availability is the share of time, or of requests, that the system works. 99.9% allows about 8.76 hours of downtime a year, 99.99% about 52.6 minutes, and 99.999% about 5.26 minutes. Each extra nine means ten times less downtime and about ten times more effort and cost. At four nines the budget is smaller than a human page, diagnose and fix cycle, so recovery must be automatic: health checks, failover, automated rollback, redundancy in every layer. I also pick request-based availability for services, since failing 0.1% of requests evenly is still 99.9% even if we were never fully down.

9. Availability versus durability: what is the difference?

Availability asks whether I can read or write now. Durability asks whether data I stored will still exist later without loss or corruption. A system can be down but durable (data is safe on disks and returns after the outage), or up but not durable (a cache that always answers but loses everything on restart), and the second is worse for a source of truth. Durability is reached by writing to several independent devices before acknowledging, replication or erasure coding across racks and zones, checksums and scrubbing against silent corruption, and backups or versioning against human error. Eleven nines means about one object lost per 10,000 years for 10 million objects.

10. State Little's Law and what it is good for.

For a stable system, the average number of items inside equals the arrival rate times the average time each stays: L = lambda x W. If 5,000 requests arrive per second and each takes 200 ms, about 1,000 are in flight at any moment. That sizes anything that holds a resource per request: threads, sockets, database connections, sessions, queue depth. Turned around, throughput equals concurrency divided by latency, so 50 connections at 100 ms cannot exceed 500 requests per second. It also explains cascading failure: if latency grows five times, concurrency grows five times, which can exhaust threads and make latency worse.

B. Design scenarios

11. You get the prompt "Design a ride-hailing service". What do you do in the first five minutes?

I treat the prompt as incomplete and ask questions that change the design. First, users and features: request a ride, match with a nearby driver, live tracking, payment; I narrow to three or four and say what I leave out. Then scale: riders, drivers, requests per second, number of cities, and latency expected for matching. Then what must be consistent, for example a driver must never be assigned to two riders. I offer defaults ("I will assume 10 million rides a day") so the interviewer only needs to nod. I finish by writing the summary on the board and reading it back, so any correction comes before I invest time in the wrong direction.

12. Estimate storage and bandwidth for a photo-sharing app with 100 million daily users.

I state assumptions: 10% upload one photo a day (10 million uploads), each stored at about 2.5 MB including resized versions, and each user views 40 photos a day at 200 KB. Storage is 10 million x 2.5 MB = 25 TB a day, about 9 PB a year, and about 27 PB with three copies, or about 13.7 PB with erasure coding. Metadata at 500 bytes each is only about 1.8 TB a year. Views are 4 billion a day, about 46,000 per second average and about 139,000 at peak, so egress is about 222 Gbps at peak. The consequence: a distributed object store with tiering, and a CDN that must hit about 90%, leaving the origin about 22 Gbps. The app tier needs only about 180 servers, so it is not the hard part.

13. Estimate the load for a chat service and say what the bottleneck is.

With 50 million daily users sending 40 messages each, that is 2 billion messages a day, about 23,000 per second on average and about 70,000 at peak. With two recipients on average, deliveries are about 140,000 per second at peak. Stored at about 200 bytes, that is 400 GB a day, about 146 TB a year, and about 2.2 PB for five years with three copies. If 10 million users are connected at peak at about 10 KB each, that is 100 GB of connection memory and about 200 to 300 gateway servers at 50,000 connections each. Bandwidth is only about 340 Mbps. So the bottlenecks are open connections and fan-out, not bandwidth or CPU; I would partition messages by conversation and keep a routing service that knows which gateway holds each user.

14. How do you decide how many application servers you need?

First compute peak QPS from users, actions, and a peak factor. Then estimate what one server can do from CPU time per request: 5 ms of CPU on 16 cores is about 3,200 per second at full load, and I say I would confirm with a load test. I divide peak by capacity times a target utilisation of about 60%, then inflate for failure domains: with three zones, multiply by 3/2 so losing a zone is survivable. For 139,000 peak with 2,000 per second per server that gives about 116 servers, about 180 after zone headroom. I also check that memory or connection limits are not hit first.

15. How would you size a cache for a product or profile service?

I estimate distinct items read per day, the size of one cached item, and the fraction to hold, starting at 20% because access is skewed (the 80/20 rule). For 500 million distinct profiles of 1 KB, caching 20% is 100 million x 1 KB = 100 GB, plus 20% to 30% overhead. That is about 3 to 6 nodes once I shard for capacity and throughput and keep replicas for availability. Then I judge by hit ratio, not only size: at 90% the database sees 10% of reads, at 99% only 1%. So with 35,000 peak reads, the database goes from 3,500 to 350 reads per second.

16. Pick a system and show how you decide where the numbers say the design is hard.

Take the page-view counter: 1 billion events a day is about 10,000 per second, about 30,000 at peak. Storage is small: 100 GB a day of raw events kept 7 days, and about 15 GB of aggregates. Reads are tiny. So the hard part is not storage or bandwidth; it is 30,000 writes per second and a hot page that can get thousands of views a second, which makes one database row a bottleneck. Therefore I design for it: a durable partitioned log to absorb bursts, an aggregator that groups events per page every few seconds and writes one increment per page, and spreading a hot page over several partitions since addition is order-free. I then trace each component back to a number.

C. Failures and real-time situations

17. You are 15 minutes in and still on requirements. What do you do?

I say it plainly and compress. I stop gathering and summarise: "I have enough; here are the features, the numbers I will assume, and what I leave out." Then I move to estimation with rounded numbers, finishing it in a couple of minutes, and go straight to the API and data model in a few lines each. The diagram and deep dives are where most marks are, so I protect that time. I also ask the interviewer whether the pace works, which lets them steer. The mistake would be pretending nothing happened and running out of time before reaching the interesting parts.

18. Halfway through you realise an earlier decision was wrong. How do you handle it?

I say so directly and fix it, because real designs evolve and spotting a flaw early is a strength. I state what I assumed, what is wrong (for example, polling the database for every feed request will not hold at 100 reads per write), and the change (add a cache), then check what else depended on it. I update only what the change affects instead of redrawing everything, to keep to the time budget. Interviewers generally reward smooth self-correction, while hiding the mistake or defending it against the evidence scores badly.

19. The interviewer interrupts and suggests a different approach. What do you do?

I treat it as information, not a verdict. I restate the suggestion to be sure I understood, then compare it with mine against the requirements we agreed, naming what each gains and loses. If theirs fits better I adopt it and say why. If I still prefer mine, I name the specific requirement that tips it and admit the cost of my approach. Either way I show collaboration and reasoning. I avoid instant surrender without analysis and I avoid defensiveness, because the interviewer is partly checking how I would behave when a teammate disagrees.

20. Your service latency spiked and threads are exhausted although traffic is unchanged. Explain with numbers.

This is Little's Law at work. At 5,000 requests per second with 200 ms latency, 1,000 requests are in flight. If a slow downstream dependency pushes latency to 1 second, in-flight requests become 5,000 with the same arrivals. Thread pools fill, new requests queue, and the waiting makes latency even worse, which is a cascading failure. My immediate steps: shed load or rate limit, cut timeouts so slow calls free their threads, trip a circuit breaker on the slow dependency, and degrade to cached or partial data. Longer term I size pools with L = lambda x W, add timeouts everywhere, and keep utilisation moderate.

21. One zone out of three fails and the other two collapse. What went wrong in capacity planning?

Headroom for failure was not included. If three zones each run at 80% of capacity, losing one pushes the other two to 120% of capacity, so they overload. To survive the loss of one of three zones, each should run at no more than about two thirds at peak, because the survivors carry 1.5 times the load. In sizing terms I divide by a target utilisation of about 60% and multiply by N/(N-1) for N zones. Queues also blow up near full load: response time is about service time divided by (1 minus utilisation), so 90% busy is 10 times slower than idle. In the incident I shed load and add capacity; afterwards I fix the plan and test zone loss.

22. A request that calls 100 shards in parallel has terrible p50 even though each shard looks fine. Why?

The request waits for the slowest shard. If each shard is slow 1% of the time, then 1 minus 0.99100, about 63%, of requests hit at least one slow shard, so the overall median approaches the per-shard p99. This is tail latency amplification. Fixes: reduce per-shard tail (find pauses such as garbage collection, noisy neighbours, cold caches), use hedged requests (send a duplicate to another replica after the p95 time and take the first answer, at about 5% extra load), set timeouts with bounded retries, reduce fan-out by better partitioning, and keep utilisation moderate so queues do not form.

D. Trade-offs and why-questions

23. Why size for peak and not average, and how much headroom on top?

Users do not arrive evenly. Provision for the average and the busy hours overload the system exactly when most users are present. So I size for peak, which is average times a peak factor (2 to 3 for a typical consumer app, much more for events). Headroom is a separate multiplier on top for failures, deploys, and forecast error. In practice I run at 50% to 70% CPU at peak, which is about 1.5 to 2 times the raw peak need, and I inflate for zone loss. Average QPS is still useful for cost, for example monthly transfer. Autoscaling cuts idle cost but does not remove the reserve, since scaling takes minutes.

24. Why separate blobs from metadata?

They have opposite shapes. Blobs (photos, video) are big, rarely changed, and dominate bytes, so they belong in an object store with erasure coding and cold tiers. Metadata is small and structured, dominates query traffic, and needs lookups, indexes and caching, so it fits a sharded database plus a cache. In the photo example, blobs are about 9 PB a year while metadata is about 1.8 TB. Putting both in one database would make it huge and slow, and would waste expensive database storage on bytes that never need queries. The metadata row simply holds the link to the blob.

25. Replication or erasure coding for durability?

Replication keeps full copies, usually three, which is simple, fast to read, and fast to repair, but costs 3 times the storage. Erasure coding splits an object into data and parity pieces (for example 10 plus 4, any 10 of 14 rebuild it), giving similar durability at about 1.4 times the storage. The cost is more CPU and network on reads and repairs. So I use replication for hot, small, latency-sensitive data and erasure coding for large, cold objects such as old photos, which halves raw disk in the photo example. Neither replaces backups or versioning, since both copy a bad delete.

26. How do you talk about tradeoffs without sounding indecisive?

I use four parts: name the options, name the criteria from the requirements, choose with a reason, and state the cost and what would make me revisit. For example: "Feed at write time or read time? Reads dominate and celebrities exist, so I precompute for ordinary users and merge at read time for celebrities. The cost is extra storage and a harder read path; if most users are inactive I would revisit." That sounds decisive because there is a decision, and thoughtful because the cost and trigger are stated. The failure mode is saying "it depends" and stopping, without saying what it depends on or which way I lean here.

27. When is estimation a waste of time?

When it computes numbers that influence no decision, or chases precision such as 86,400 instead of rounding to 100,000. The right level is rounded, order of magnitude, ending in a conclusion: "about 10,000 events a second, peak 30,000, so per-event database updates are out." I stop as soon as I know which resource is the bottleneck, whether writes, storage, bandwidth, memory, or connections, and return only if a later decision needs another number. Estimation should take about four to five minutes of a 45 minute round. If I notice I have spent three minutes on one calculation, I round it and move on.

28. What distinguishes a senior answer from a mid-level one?

A mid-level answer produces a reasonable architecture with standard components, mostly when prompted. A senior answer drives the conversation, quantifies each claim, and explains why each component is there and what it costs. Seniors volunteer failure modes (node loss, cache flush, hot key), discuss operability such as metrics, alerts, rollback, and describe how the design grows from a monolith with one database to sharded services. Leads add organisational judgement: what to build or buy, how to split work across teams, safe rollout with feature flags and canaries, cost per user, and the biggest uncertainty with a plan to retire it early.

29. What do you do if time is running out and the design is unfinished?

I prioritise a coherent end-to-end design over completeness of any one piece. I say I am behind, ask which area the interviewer most wants, give a concise treatment of it, and list known risks and next steps. I would rather leave a sound but incomplete deep dive and a clear summary than finish one component and leave the rest of the system undefined. I never skip the wrap-up: two minutes covering decisions, tradeoffs and limits leave a stronger last impression than one more detail.

30. Why prefer redundancy across zones if each replica is 99.9% available?

Because two independent 99.9% replicas fail together only with probability 0.001 x 0.001, so the pair is 99.9999% available, while three 99.9% services in series fall to 99.7%. Redundancy multiplies the failure chances; dependencies in series multiply the success chances downward. But the math only holds when failures are independent. Replicas in one rack, one power feed, or running the same buggy release fail together, so I spread across zones and regions and watch shared parts such as the load balancer, DNS, and config service, which cap the whole system. I also make non-essential dependencies optional so their failure does not fail the request.
