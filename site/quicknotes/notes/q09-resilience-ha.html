0. Key definitions (know these cold)

TermDefinition
Architecture styleA named, well-known way to cut a system into parts and decide which parts may talk to which.
MonolithThe whole app is built and deployed as one unit. Simple to run, but every change ships everything.
MicroservicesMany small services, each owning its own data and deployed on its own. Teams move independently, but you now run a distributed system.
Bounded contextA part of the business where words have one clear meaning (an "order" in billing is not an "order" in shipping). A good service boundary.

1. Architecture styles and DDD Ch 39

One line: pick the simplest style that fits the team and the load; split into services only when the pain of one big deploy is bigger than the pain of a distributed system.

Styles live at three scales and you mix them. Inside one program: layered, hexagonal (ports and adapters), clean/onion. Between deployable units: monolith, modular monolith, SOA, microservices, serverless. How data and control flow: event-driven, pipes and filters, CQRS, BFF. A real system picks one from each, for example a modular monolith, each module built hexagonally, publishing events, with one BFF per client.

StyleUse whenAvoid when
Layered (UI, logic, data)Simple apps, small teams.Logic leaks into every layer; the database shapes everything.
Hexagonal / cleanYou want business logic testable without the DB or web framework. The core defines "ports"; adapters (DB, HTTP, queue) plug in.Tiny CRUD apps; the extra layers are just cost.
MonolithEarly product, small team, unclear boundaries. Start here.Many teams blocking each other on one release.
Modular monolithMedium teams; want clean boundaries without network cost. Often the best default.Parts need very different scaling or tech.
MicroservicesMany teams, parts with different scale or release speed, clear bounded contexts.Small team; boundaries unknown (you get a "distributed monolith": all the pain, none of the gain).
Event-drivenFan-out, decoupling, audit trail, async work.You need an immediate answer and simple debugging.
Serverless (FaaS)Spiky or rare workloads, glue code, scale to zero.Steady heavy traffic, strict latency (cold starts), long jobs.
BFF (backend for frontend)Web and mobile need different shaped responses.One client only; BFFs that grow business logic.
CQRSReads and writes have very different shapes or scale.Simple CRUD; extra sync complexity.

Domain-driven design in simple words

  • Ubiquitous language: business people and engineers use the same words, in code too.
  • Bounded context: the area where a word means one thing. This is the natural microservice boundary. Draw the boundary where the language changes.
  • Subdomains: core (what makes you different, build it and put your best people on it), supporting (needed but not special), generic (auth, billing: buy it).
  • Context map: how contexts relate, and an anti-corruption layer (a translator) so another system's model does not leak into yours.
  • Aggregate: a group of objects changed together in one transaction, with one root. Keep transactions inside one aggregate; use events between them.
Rules of thumb: one team can own a few services, not dozens. Each service owns its data (no shared database tables). A cross-service call adds roughly 1 ms in the same zone and its own failure chance. Serverless cold starts range from tens of milliseconds to a few seconds.
Traps: splitting into microservices on day one; services that share a database (a hidden monolith); chatty services that need five calls per request; no clear owner for a boundary; "event-driven" with no plan for duplicates and ordering.
Say: "I would start as a modular monolith, with bounded contexts as modules and a database schema per module. We split a module out into a service only when it needs its own scaling, its own release speed, or its own team. That keeps the early cost low and the split cheap."

2. Resilience patterns Ch 40

One line: assume every call can be slow or fail; bound the waiting, retry carefully, stop calling what is broken, isolate resources, and shed load before you fall over.

Failures are transient (gone in a moment: a dropped packet), intermittent (comes and goes: a bad node), or persistent (stays broken until fixed). The cruel one is the slow dependency, because it is worse than a dead one: callers wait, threads fill up, and the slowness spreads upward. That is a cascading failure.

The toolbox

ToolWhat it doesKey rule
TimeoutStop waiting after a set time.Set a connect timeout and a read timeout. Pick from the dependency's p99 (a bit above it), never "none" or "infinite". Pass a deadline down the chain so deeper calls know how much time is left.
RetryTry again on transient failure.Only for idempotent calls (same effect if done twice) or with an idempotency key. Use exponential backoff plus jitter, a max of 2 to 3 tries, and a retry budget (for example retries are at most 10% of traffic).
Circuit breakerStop calling a failing dependency.States: closed (normal), open (fail fast), half-open (let a few test calls through). Scope it per dependency or endpoint.
BulkheadSeparate pools per dependency or per tenant.One slow dependency can only exhaust its own pool.
FallbackA cheaper answer when the real one fails.Cached copy, default value, hide the feature. Decide it in advance.
Load sheddingReject excess work early.Return 429 or 503 fast; drop low-priority first; protect the core path.
BackpressureMake the producer slow down.Bounded queues, rate-limited intake, block or reject when full.
Queue load levellingAbsorb a burst in a queue and process at a steady rate.Needs async-friendly work and a queue-depth alarm.
Hedged requestSend a second copy to another replica if the first is slow.Only for idempotent reads; only after about p95 so extra load stays near 5%.

Timeouts, queues and Little's law

Little's law: in-flight requests = arrival rate x time each takes. At 1,000 requests/s and 50 ms each, about 50 are in flight. If the dependency slows to 2 s, that is 2,000 in flight: your thread pool of 200 is full in a moment. That is why a timeout (cap the time) and a bounded queue (cap the waiting) matter more than a big thread pool.

Timeout budget rule: the outer timeout must be larger than the sum of inner timeouts and retries, or the caller gives up while inner work is still running and wasted. A user-facing 2 s budget cannot hold three serial calls of 1 s each.

Tail latency

If a page calls 100 services in parallel and each has a 1% chance of being slow, then 1 - 0.99^100, about 63%, of pages are slow. So we care about p99, not the average. Remedies: hedged requests, request coalescing (many identical requests share one backend call), smaller fan-out, caching, and keeping queues short.

Numbers: backoff 100 ms, 200 ms, 400 ms up to a cap; 2 to 3 retries; breaker opens at about 50% errors over a window with a minimum request count; open for about 10 to 60 s; retry budget about 10% extra load; hedge after about p95.
Traps: retrying non-idempotent calls (double charge); retries without jitter (all clients retry together: a retry storm); retries at every layer; timeouts longer than the caller's own timeout; a fallback that calls the same broken dependency; infinite queues (they hide overload until memory runs out and latency is minutes).
Say: "Every remote call gets a deadline. We retry only idempotent calls, once or twice, with backoff, jitter and a budget. A circuit breaker per dependency fails fast, bulkheads keep one slow dependency from eating all threads, and at the edge we shed low-priority work with a 429 before we fall over."

3. High availability and disaster recovery Ch 41

One line: HA is about surviving small failures with no human; DR is about recovering from big ones, and it is defined by two numbers, RPO and RTO.

Availability maths

AvailabilityDowntime a yearDowntime a month
99% (two nines)about 3.65 daysabout 7.3 hours
99.9% (three nines)about 8.8 hoursabout 43 minutes
99.99% (four nines)about 53 minutesabout 4.3 minutes
99.999% (five nines)about 5.3 minutesabout 26 seconds
  • In series (a request needs A and B and C): multiply. Three parts at 99.9% each give 0.999^3, about 99.7%. More hard dependencies means lower availability.
  • In parallel (any one of two copies is enough): unavailability multiplies. Two copies at 99% each: 1 - 0.01 x 0.01 = 99.99%. This assumes the copies fail independently, so spread them across zones.
  • Real limits: shared dependencies (one DNS, one config system, one bad deploy) break the independence assumption.

Remove single points of failure, layer by layer

LayerHow to remove the single point
DNSMultiple providers or a managed global DNS with health checks; short TTL for failover records.
Load balancerManaged LB across zones, or pairs with a floating IP.
App tierStateless instances across at least 2 to 3 zones; state in shared stores, not in memory.
CacheReplicas and failover; the DB must survive a cold cache.
DatabasePrimary with synchronous or asynchronous replicas in other zones; automated failover.
Queue / streamReplicated brokers (for example replication factor 3, in-sync replicas).
Zone / regionSpread across zones; second region for DR.

Failover details interviewers poke at

  • Replication: synchronous means a write is confirmed only after the replica has it (no data loss, slower, blocks if the replica is down). Asynchronous confirms first (fast, but the last few writes can be lost on failover). Many systems use "semi-sync": one sync replica plus async others.
  • Health detection: use several missed checks, not one, to avoid false alarms. A false failover can be worse than the outage.
  • Split brain: the old leader comes back and still thinks it leads. Fix with a majority quorum for elections, and fencing: a rising token so storage rejects writes from the old leader (see the lock service case).
  • Static stability: design so the system keeps working when a control plane is down (do not need to call a "scale up" API during the outage). Keep spare capacity ready: if you lose 1 of 3 zones, the other two must carry the load, so run each at under about 66%.

Disaster recovery

DR strategyRTO / RPO (typical)CostWhat it is
Backup and restorehours / hourslowestCopies in another region; rebuild when needed.
Pilot lighttens of minutes / minuteslowData replicated; the core (DB) runs, app servers are off until needed.
Warm standbyminutes / seconds to minutesmediumSmall full copy running; scale up on failover.
Active-active (multi-site)near zero / near zerohighestFull capacity in two or more regions.

Backups

  • Full, incremental, differential: full is everything; incremental is changes since the last backup of any kind; differential is changes since the last full.
  • Point-in-time recovery (PITR): a base backup plus the write-ahead log, so you can rewind to "just before the bad query" (for example 10:41:59).
  • A backup you never restored is not a backup. Run restore drills and time them against the RTO.
  • Immutable, offsite copies protect against ransomware and an admin deleting everything.
  • Corruption versus loss: replication copies bad data to every replica instantly. Only backups and PITR rescue you from a bad delete or a bug.
Numbers: 99.9% about 43 min a month; 99.99% about 4.3 min a month; AZ-to-AZ latency about 1 to 2 ms; region-to-region about 50 to 150 ms (so synchronous cross-region writes are costly); DNS failover is limited by TTL and by clients that cache longer than they should.
Traps: "we have replicas so we have backups" (replicas copy deletes); a DR plan never tested; failover capacity not reserved; stating five nines with several single-zone dependencies; ignoring that the failover tool itself lives in the failed region; forgetting DNS TTL.
Say: "I would ask for the RPO and RTO first, then choose the cheapest strategy that meets them. Stateless tiers go across three zones; the database has a synchronous replica in another zone and an async copy in a second region. We keep point-in-time backups, restore them on a schedule, and run a game day to prove the failover works."

4. Multi-tenancy and cells Ch 42

One line: many customers share the same system to keep cost low, so you must keep their data apart, stop one from hurting others, and limit how many a single failure can touch.

Isolation models

ModelWhat it meansGoodBad
SiloOwn stack or database per tenant.Strongest isolation; easy per-tenant compliance and restore; no noisy neighbour.Expensive; hard to run thousands; many upgrades.
PoolEveryone shares tables, rows tagged with a tenant id.Cheapest; one upgrade; scales to huge tenant counts.A bug can leak data; noisy neighbours; hard per-tenant restore.
Bridge (hybrid)Shared app tier, separate schema or database per tenant, or big tenants siloed and small ones pooled.Balance of cost and isolation.More moving parts; routing needed.

Typical answer: pool for the long tail of small tenants, silo for the few large or regulated ones, with a directory that says where each tenant lives.

Keeping tenants apart (defence in depth)

  1. Tenant context: work out the tenant id once at the edge (from the verified token, not from a user-supplied field) and carry it everywhere (headers, logs, messages, async jobs).
  2. Query scoping: the data layer always adds WHERE tenant_id = ?; do not rely on each developer remembering.
  3. Row-level security in the database as a second wall.
  4. Tenant-aware caches, keys and queues: put the tenant id in every cache key (t42:user:7).
  5. Encryption per tenant (separate keys) so deleting a key can erase a tenant, and a leak is not readable.
  6. Leak tests: automated tests that act as tenant A and try to read tenant B.

Noisy neighbour controls

ControlHow
Per-tenant rate limitsEach tenant gets its own request budget; the 429 goes to the heavy one only.
QuotasCaps on storage, jobs, API calls per period, tied to the plan.
Fair schedulingWeighted or per-tenant queues so one tenant's 1 million jobs do not block everybody; round-robin between tenants.
Resource limitsPer-tenant concurrency, connection and memory caps (a bulkhead per tenant).
Move the whaleGive a very large tenant its own shard, pool or cell.

Routing, lifecycle, and data rules

  • Tenant directory: a small, highly available, cached map from tenant id to shard or cell. The router reads it on each request (cache it, and expect it to change when tenants move).
  • Onboarding: automated provisioning (create records, keys, config). Offboarding: export, then delete on a schedule, including backups and caches.
  • Residency: some tenants require data to stay in a country or region; route them to a cell in that place.
  • Metering and billing: count usage per tenant (events with tenant id), aggregate, and attribute cost.
  • Moving a tenant: copy data, sync changes, briefly pause writes, flip the directory entry, verify, clean up.

Cell-based architecture

  • Cells vs plain sharding: a shard splits only the data; a cell splits the whole stack (app, cache, queue, db), so the blast radius covers everything.
  • Good cell design: cells do not call each other; fixed maximum size (so each is tested at its limit); the router is as simple and stable as possible; deploy to one cell first, then the next (a built-in canary).
  • Blast radius maths: with N equal cells a cell failure touches about 1/N of tenants. 20 cells means about 5%. A cell deploy bug only hits its share too.
Numbers: N cells gives blast radius about 1/N; keep a tenant in one cell so a request never fans across cells; the tenant directory may be read on every request, so cache it with a short TTL and have a fallback if it is down.
Traps: tenant id taken from a request parameter instead of the verified token; a cache key without the tenant id (cross-tenant leak); background jobs that lose the tenant context; one shared global queue where one tenant starves others; a router that has become a big, fragile single point of failure; no plan to move a whale.
Say: "I would pool small tenants in cells and give big or regulated tenants their own cell. The tenant id comes from the verified token and is enforced at the data layer, in row-level security, and in every cache key. Per-tenant rate limits and fair queues handle noisy neighbours, and cells keep any failure to a slice of customers."

5. Deployment, CI/CD and autoscaling Ch 43

One line: ship small changes often through an automated pipeline, expose them gradually, watch the numbers, and be able to undo quickly; let the platform add or remove capacity by itself.

CI/CD: every change is built once into one artifact (an image), tested (many fast unit tests, fewer integration tests, few end-to-end tests), and the same artifact is promoted from test to staging to production. Do not rebuild per environment. Flaky tests are fixed or removed because they teach people to ignore red builds.

Deployment strategies

StrategyHowRollbackCost / risk
RecreateStop old, start new.Redeploy old.Downtime. Only for non-critical or batch.
RollingReplace a few instances at a time.Roll back the same way, slowly.No extra capacity; old and new run together, so versions must be compatible.
Blue/greenFull new stack ready; flip traffic.Flip back in seconds.Double capacity during the switch; database changes still need care.
Canary1%, 5%, 25%, 100% of traffic with automatic checks on errors and latency.Send traffic back; automatic abort.Needs good metrics. Best safety for the effort.
ShadowCopy real traffic to the new version, ignore its replies.Nothing to undo.Double load; side effects must be disabled.

Feature flags, database changes, compatibility

  • Feature flags separate deploy (code is live) from release (users see it). They give instant off switches, gradual rollout and dark launches. Dangers: flags nobody deletes, the flag service becoming a dependency (cache values locally), untested flag combinations.
  • Expand and contract for the database: (1) add the new column, (2) write both old and new, (3) backfill, (4) switch reads to new, (5) stop writing old, (6) drop old later. Each step is safe on its own, so you never need a "big bang" migration.
  • Compatibility: during a rollout, old and new code run together and both talk to the same database and queues. New code must read old data and old code must tolerate new data (add fields, do not rename or remove them in one go).
  • Rollback vs roll-forward: rolling back is fast for stateless code but not after an irreversible data change; then roll forward with a fix. Keep the previous version deployable.

Graceful shutdown and health probes

  • Graceful shutdown: on a stop signal, mark not ready (so the load balancer stops sending new requests), finish in-flight work, close connections, then exit within the grace period. Without it, every deploy drops requests.
  • Readiness probe: "can I take traffic now?" (fails during warm-up). Liveness probe: "am I stuck? restart me." Startup probe: gives slow starters time. Do not tie liveness to a database check, or a database blip restarts every pod.

Containers, Kubernetes and autoscaling

ThingSimple meaning
ContainerA packaged app plus its libraries, run in an isolated slice of the host (namespaces for isolation, cgroups for CPU and memory limits). Lighter than a virtual machine.
Pod / DeploymentA pod is one or more containers that run together. A Deployment keeps N pods running and does rolling updates.
ServiceA stable name and address in front of changing pods.
Requests and limitsRequest = what the scheduler reserves for the pod; limit = the cap. Memory over the limit gets the pod killed; CPU over the limit is slowed.
HPAChanges the number of pods from a metric (CPU, requests per second, queue depth). Reacts in tens of seconds to minutes.
VPAChanges the size (requests) of each pod.
Cluster autoscalerAdds or removes nodes (machines) when pods cannot be scheduled or nodes are empty. Takes minutes.
  • Scale on a metric that matches the bottleneck: CPU for compute work, queue depth or lag for workers, requests in flight for request/response.
  • Scale up fast, scale down slowly (a cool-down window), to avoid flapping.
  • Keep spare headroom: new nodes take minutes, a spike can arrive in seconds. Use a minimum replica count, and pre-scale for known events.
  • Limits: autoscaling does not help if the database is the bottleneck, and each new pod opens more database connections.
Numbers: rolling update with maxSurge 25% and maxUnavailable 0 keeps full capacity; canary steps like 1, 5, 25, 50, 100%; HPA target CPU about 60 to 70%; pod start 10 to 60 s; new node 1 to 5 min; grace period about 30 s; rollback by traffic flip: seconds.
Traps: rebuilding the artifact per environment; a migration that breaks the old version during the rollout; no readiness probe so traffic hits a cold pod; a liveness probe that checks a dependency; CPU limit too tight so the app is throttled; scaling on CPU for an I/O-bound or queue-driven worker; a flag service outage that breaks the app.
Say: "One artifact goes through the pipeline. Release is a canary with automatic abort on error rate and p99, behind a feature flag, with expand-and-contract database changes so old and new versions can run together. Pods drain gracefully, readiness gates traffic, and HPA scales on the real bottleneck with a cluster autoscaler behind it."

6. Say-aloud script (45 seconds)

"I would start with the simplest structure that fits: a modular monolith with clear bounded contexts, and split out a service only when scaling, release speed, or team ownership demands it. For resilience, every remote call has a deadline, retries are limited to idempotent calls with backoff, jitter and a budget, and circuit breakers and bulkheads stop one slow dependency from spreading. When overloaded, we shed low-priority work early. For availability, I would first ask for the RPO and RTO, then spread stateless services across three zones, replicate the database across zones with automated, fenced failover, and keep tested point-in-time backups and a second region matched to the DR budget. For a multi-tenant product, I would pool small tenants and silo big ones, enforce the tenant id at every layer, limit noisy neighbours, and group tenants into cells so a failure touches only a slice. Finally, releases go through one artifact, canary with automatic rollback, feature flags, safe database changes, graceful shutdown, and autoscaling on the metric that is the real bottleneck."

7. Interview Q&A (30)

A. Concepts

1. Monolith, modular monolith, microservices: what is the difference and how do you choose?

A monolith is one deployable unit. A modular monolith is still one deployable unit, but the code is split into modules with strict rules about who may call whom, and often a schema per module. Microservices are separate deployables, each with its own data, talking over the network. I choose by team size and pain. A small team with unclear boundaries should use a monolith or a modular monolith, because the cost of a network call, a distributed transaction and a separate pipeline is high. I split to microservices when many teams block each other on one release, or a part needs very different scaling or technology. The main danger is a distributed monolith: services that must be deployed together. The modular monolith makes a later split cheap.

2. What is a bounded context and why does it matter for service boundaries?

A bounded context is an area of the business in which each word has one agreed meaning. "Customer" in sales (a lead with a budget) is not "customer" in billing (a payer with invoices). If one model tries to serve both, it becomes huge and every team fights over it. So we draw boundaries where the language changes and give each context its own model and data. That boundary is usually a good place for a service or module because the things inside change together and talk a lot, while calls across the boundary are fewer. I also use an anti-corruption layer, a translator, where another context's model would otherwise leak in.

3. Explain timeouts, retries, circuit breakers and bulkheads and how they work together.

The timeout limits how long one call may take, so slow does not become stuck. The retry gives a second chance for transient failures, but only for idempotent calls, with backoff, jitter and a budget. The circuit breaker watches the error rate; when it is too high it opens and fails calls instantly, so we stop hammering a sick dependency and free our threads, and it later probes with a few calls (half-open). The bulkhead gives each dependency its own pool of threads or connections so one slow dependency cannot take them all. Together: timeout bounds waiting, retry masks small blips, breaker stops sustained failure, bulkhead contains the damage, and a fallback gives the user something useful.

4. What is idempotency and why does it matter for retries?

An operation is idempotent if doing it twice has the same effect as doing it once. Reading is naturally so; "set the balance to 50" is, "add 10 to the balance" is not. A retry can duplicate work because a timeout does not tell you whether the first call ran. For non-idempotent actions like payments or order creation, the client sends a unique idempotency key and the server stores the result under that key; a repeat returns the stored result instead of acting again. Without this, retries are dangerous and I would not enable them.

5. What is the difference between HA and DR?

HA keeps the system up through small, common failures, like one server or one zone, using redundancy and automatic failover, with no human needed and usually little or no data loss. DR is the plan for large, rare events, such as losing a region, a destructive bug, or ransomware, where we recover from a second site or from backups. DR is measured by RPO (how much data we may lose) and RTO (how long we may be down). HA reduces how often we need DR, but it does not replace backups, because replicas faithfully copy mistakes.

6. Explain RPO and RTO and how they drive design.

RPO is the amount of recent data you can lose, measured as time. RTO is how long you can be down. They are business decisions with prices. An RPO of zero needs synchronous copies in another location, which costs write latency. An RTO of seconds needs a warm or active-active second site. An RPO of one day and RTO of one day is just nightly backups. So I ask for both numbers per system, then choose from backup and restore, pilot light, warm standby, or active-active, taking the cheapest that meets them. Different parts can have different targets: payments strict, analytics relaxed.

7. How do you calculate the availability of a system with several parts?

Parts that are all required are in series, so I multiply their availabilities: three 99.9% parts give about 99.7%. Redundant copies are in parallel, so I multiply the failure chances: two independent 99% copies give 99.99%. Then I add the caveat that failures are rarely independent: a shared deploy, a shared DNS or config system, or one zone can take both copies down. So the calculation gives an upper bound, and the real number depends on removing shared dependencies and spreading across failure domains.

8. What is a cell-based architecture and what problem does it solve?

A cell is a complete, independent copy of the service stack serving a fixed share of tenants. A thin router sends each tenant to its cell. It solves blast radius: if a cell has an outage, a bad deploy or a poison tenant, only that cell's tenants suffer. With 20 cells, a failure hits about 5% of customers. It also gives a natural place to canary: deploy to one cell first. It differs from plain sharding, which splits only data; cells split the app, cache, queue and database together. Costs are more total infrastructure, a router and directory to run, and more work to move tenants.

B. Design scenarios

9. Design the resilience of a service that calls three downstream services to build one page.

First, set a deadline for the whole request (say 800 ms) and pass it down. Call independent services in parallel with each having its own timeout under that budget. Add a circuit breaker and a separate bulkhead pool per downstream. Decide a fallback per part: the recommendations box can disappear or use a cached list, but the core product data is required. Retry only idempotent reads, once, with jitter, and not if the budget is nearly spent. Cache what is safe. Add load shedding at the edge. Watch p99 per dependency, breaker state, and retry rate. Note that with three parallel calls the page is as slow as the slowest, so tail latency matters.

10. Design HA for a service in one region that needs 99.99%.

Four nines is about 4 minutes a month, so there is no room for manual steps. I use three zones. A multi-zone load balancer sits in front, with stateless app instances in every zone and enough capacity that losing one zone leaves the other two under about two-thirds load. The database has a primary and a synchronous standby in another zone with automatic, fenced failover, plus read replicas. Cache and queue are replicated across zones. Deploys are canary so a bad release is not a long outage. Health checks need several failures before acting. Then I tell the interviewer that a region-wide event still takes us down, and if that is not acceptable I add a warm standby region.

11. Design DR for a database with RPO 5 minutes and RTO 30 minutes.

An RPO of 5 minutes means we need copies that are at most 5 minutes behind: asynchronous cross-region replication with lag alarms (typically seconds), plus continuous log shipping for point-in-time recovery. An RTO of 30 minutes means a warm standby: a replica in the second region already running, and a rehearsed runbook for promotion, repointing apps, and moving traffic. Backups go to another account or region and are immutable. I run restore drills and game days and measure the real time against 30 minutes. I also note that the DNS TTL and client caching add to RTO, so I keep TTLs short on the failover records.

12. Design tenant isolation for a SaaS product with 10,000 small tenants and 20 huge ones.

A hybrid. The 10,000 small tenants share pooled cells with a tenant id on every row, row-level security, tenant-keyed caches and per-tenant rate limits. The 20 huge tenants each get a silo, a dedicated database or their own cell, which removes noisy-neighbour risk, allows custom limits, and makes compliance or residency simple. A tenant directory maps each tenant to its cell, so the router is the same for both. I build tools to move a tenant between pool and silo, because a small tenant can grow. Everything carries tenant context from the verified token, including async jobs, and automated tests try cross-tenant reads.

13. A team wants a zero-downtime release of a change that renames a database column. How?

Use expand and contract. Release 1: add the new column, write both columns, keep reading the old. Backfill existing rows in batches. Release 2: read from the new column (still writing both). Release 3: stop writing the old column. Later, drop the old column. At each step old and new versions of the app can run together, so rolling or canary deploys are safe and every step can be rolled back. A direct rename would break the old version during the rollout.

14. Design a safe release process for a high-traffic service.

A pipeline that builds one artifact, runs tests, and promotes the same artifact. Deploy to one cell or a small slice first as a canary, with automated comparison of error rate, p99, and key business metrics against the old version, and automatic rollback. Use feature flags so risky behaviour is turned on separately, gradually, per tenant. Database changes are backward compatible. Pods drain gracefully. Keep rollback to the previous version one click away, and have a freeze rule for peak times. After release, watch dashboards and alarms for a set bake time before widening.

15. How would you autoscale a worker that processes a queue?

CPU is a poor signal for queue workers, so I scale on queue depth or on the age of the oldest message. Target a backlog that can be drained in an acceptable time: desired workers is about backlog divided by (rate per worker times allowed drain time). Set a minimum for baseline, a maximum so we do not overload the database or downstream, and a slow scale-down. Workers must be idempotent and handle shutdown gracefully by finishing or returning the job. Also use per-tenant fairness so one tenant's flood does not take all workers.

C. Failures and real-time production scenarios

16. A downstream service gets slow and your whole service starts timing out. What happened and what do you do now?

Likely a cascading failure: calls to the slow service wait, in-flight work grows (Little's law), the thread pool fills, and now even endpoints that do not use that service are blocked. Right now: check which dependency's latency rose, then trip or force-open its breaker, or turn off the feature with a flag so we fail fast, and shed low-priority traffic. Scale out only if the bottleneck is on our side. After: add or tighten timeouts, a bulkhead pool per dependency, and a fallback, and check whether our retries amplified the problem. I would also do a post-incident review of timeout budgets.

17. Error rates spike right after a deploy. What do you do?

Stabilise first, investigate second. If it is a canary the pipeline should already have rolled back; if not, I roll back or flip traffic to the previous version immediately, or turn off the feature flag, because the quickest safe action is to undo the change. If a database change makes rollback unsafe, I roll forward with a quick fix instead. Then I compare logs and traces for the new version, check for compatibility problems (new code with old data, config), and write up why the canary checks did not catch it. Communicate status early to stakeholders.

18. Retries made an outage worse (a retry storm). How do you stop it and prevent it?

To stop it: reduce the load on the struggling service by opening breakers, shedding traffic, or temporarily setting retries to zero with a config flag; let the service recover and then bring traffic back gradually, not all at once. To prevent it: backoff with jitter, a small maximum number of tries, a retry budget (retries capped at about 10% of the base traffic), retry in only one layer (otherwise 3 x 3 x 3 = 27 calls), honour a "retry-after" header, and have the server shed load early with 429 or 503 so clients know to slow down.

19. The primary database fails. Walk through what happens and what can go wrong.

Health checks miss several beats, the failover system (or a person) picks the most up-to-date replica, promotes it, and moves the writer address. Apps reconnect, and writes continue. What can go wrong: with asynchronous replication, the last few writes are lost (that is the RPO); a false alarm causes an unneeded failover; the old primary comes back and accepts writes (split brain), which is why we fence it using quorum and fencing tokens; connection pools hold dead connections; and the new primary may not have enough capacity. We practise this with game days and alarm on replica lag.

20. An entire zone goes down. What should happen?

The load balancer's health checks stop sending traffic to that zone; the other zones take the load. That only works if we reserved capacity: with three zones each running above 66% load, the loss of one overloads the others. The database fails over if its primary was in that zone. Autoscaling adds capacity, but nodes take minutes, so the pre-reserved headroom is what holds the line. Static stability means we do not depend on a control-plane call to survive. Afterwards, check data consistency and rebalance.

21. Someone ran a bad delete on production data. Replicas have it too. Now what?

This is corruption, not infrastructure loss, so failover is useless: replication has copied the delete everywhere. I stop the damage (block writes if needed), then use point-in-time recovery: restore the last base backup into a separate instance and replay the write-ahead log to just before the bad statement. Then extract the lost rows and merge them into production, or switch over if the whole database is affected. This is why we keep PITR and test restores, and why admin access, change review, and soft deletes matter.

22. One tenant's heavy export job slows the platform for everyone. How do you handle it live and long term?

Live: identify the tenant from per-tenant metrics, throttle or pause its jobs, and move it to a lower-priority queue; if needed, cap its concurrency. Long term: per-tenant quotas and rate limits, fair scheduling with weighted per-tenant queues, resource limits as bulkheads, running heavy jobs on separate workers or read replicas, and moving whales to their own cell. Add per-tenant dashboards and alerts so we see the problem before the other tenants do, and communicate limits in the plan.

23. After a failover, DNS still sends some users to the dead site. Why?

DNS answers are cached by resolvers, browsers and sometimes by apps for longer than the TTL says. So some clients keep the old address for minutes or more. Mitigations: short TTLs on failover records (set before the incident, not during), a global load balancer or anycast that does not depend on DNS caching, health-checked routing, clients that retry another address, and keeping the old site able to return a clear error or redirect. And I would include DNS caching in the measured RTO.

24. HPA keeps scaling up, but latency stays bad. What do you check?

The bottleneck is probably not the pods. Check the database (CPU, locks, connection limits: more pods means more connections), downstream dependencies, a shared cache, or a lock. Check whether the metric is wrong (CPU is low because the app is waiting on I/O). Check that pods are actually ready and not throttled by too-low CPU limits, that nodes were added in time, and that pod start-up (cold caches, warm-up) is not the delay. Look at p99 per dependency in traces. Adding replicas to an app whose bottleneck is the database only makes the database worse.

D. Trade-offs and why-questions

25. Why not start with microservices?

Because at the start you do not know the right boundaries, and wrong boundaries are very expensive to fix across a network and across separate databases. Microservices add network failures, distributed transactions or eventual consistency, harder debugging, many pipelines, and more on-call work. A small team spends more time running the platform than building the product. A modular monolith gives most of the structure for little of the cost, and good module boundaries become service boundaries when real pressure appears.

26. Active-active versus active-passive: how do you choose?

Active-passive is simpler and cheaper: one site writes, the other waits, and failover takes minutes and may lose the last writes. But the standby is often untested and may not work when needed. Active-active gives near-zero RTO and uses capacity from both sites, but data must be written in two places, which brings conflicts, higher cost (about 2x with headroom), and complex consistency. I choose active-active only when the RTO and the revenue loss justify it and the data model can handle conflicts, for instance by giving each user a home region. Otherwise warm standby.

27. Synchronous versus asynchronous replication: what do you pick?

Synchronous gives zero data loss (RPO zero) but every write waits for the replica, so latency goes up and a replica failure can block writes. It is fine across zones in one region (1 to 2 ms) and painful across regions (50 to 150 ms). Asynchronous keeps writes fast and the primary independent, but the last moments can be lost on failover. A common compromise is synchronous to one nearby replica in another zone and asynchronous to a remote region. The choice follows the RPO, and I say it out loud.

28. Silo, pool or bridge for multi-tenancy?

Pool is cheapest and simplest to operate for many small tenants, but needs careful isolation code and gives noisy-neighbour risk. Silo gives strong isolation, per-tenant restore and compliance, but costs more and is harder to operate at scale. Bridge shares the app tier and splits the data layer, or pools the tail and silos the head. I pick by tenant size distribution, compliance needs and price tiers. Most real SaaS ends up hybrid with a directory and tools to move tenants between models.

29. Canary versus blue/green versus rolling?

Rolling is the cheapest: no extra capacity, but slow rollback and mixed versions. Blue/green gives instant rollback and clean testing of the whole new stack, but needs double capacity and the database must work with both. Canary gives the best risk control because real traffic hits the new version in small steps with automatic checks, but needs strong metrics and routing. For critical services I use canary on top of rolling, and blue/green when I need an atomic switch, such as an infrastructure change.

30. Why scale horizontally rather than vertically, and when is vertical fine?

Horizontal scaling (more instances) has no hard ceiling, tolerates the loss of a node, and fits stateless services behind a load balancer. Vertical scaling (a bigger machine) is simple and needs no code change, but has a ceiling, gets very expensive at the top, and is a single point of failure. Vertical is fine for a stateful primary database for a long time, small workloads, or when it buys simplicity. The usual pattern: scale the stateless tier horizontally, scale the database vertically first, then add replicas, caching, and finally sharding.
