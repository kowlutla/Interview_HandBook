Cheat sheet

Failure Handling Checklist

What to say about failures in any design: which component breaks how, which standard mitigation answers it, what to ask about data and consistency, and a ten-point script you can say aloud in about three minutes. Every row links to the chapter that owns the idea.

1. How to use this page

Interviewers almost always end with "what happens when X fails?". A weak answer names one component and says "we add a replica". A strong answer walks the same fixed grid: component, failure type, detection, mitigation, what the user sees, what data is at risk. This page is that grid. Foundations: Chapter 27 (partial failure, why you cannot tell slow from dead), Chapter 40 (patterns), Chapter 41 (redundancy and DR).

Key idea In a distributed system you cannot distinguish a dead peer from a slow one; you only have timeouts. So every failure answer reduces to: bound the wait (timeout), decide whether to try again (retry, only if safe), stop hammering (breaker, shedding), isolate (bulkhead), and degrade on purpose (fallback).

5. Data loss and consistency: questions to ask yourself

QuestionA good answer sounds likeChapter
Which writes can we never lose?Payments, orders and account changes are acknowledged only after durable commit (WAL fsync plus a replica in another AZ). Metrics and view counts may lose a few seconds.19, 24
When do we acknowledge a write: before or after replication?Synchronous to one standby for zero RPO on critical data; asynchronous for the rest and accept a small, stated RPO (seconds).24
Can the same message be delivered twice, or never?Assume at-least-once. Make consumers idempotent by dedup key; use the outbox so a DB commit and a publish cannot diverge.32, 31
What if the client times out but the server succeeded?The client retries with the same idempotency key and gets the original result; no double charge.32
Does a failure across services leave partial state?Use a saga with compensations, and a reconciliation job that finds and fixes stuck states.31, 48
What do readers see during failover or a partition?Name the choice per feature: consistent and unavailable (balances) versus available and possibly stale (feeds). Say read-your-writes is kept via primary reads or tokens.28
How do we avoid two leaders?Leader election through consensus or a lease, with fencing tokens so a paused old leader cannot write.29, 30
How do we find silent corruption or replica drift?Checksums on read, scrubbing, Merkle-tree anti-entropy, periodic reconciliation against the source of truth.33
What if a human or a bug deletes data?Replication copies the delete. Need point-in-time recovery, soft deletes, versioned and immutable backups, and tested restores.41, 21

6. Disaster recovery: RPO, RTO and the four strategies

RPO (recovery point objective) is how much recent data you may lose, measured in time. RTO (recovery time objective) is how long you may be down. Lower values cost much more. Ask the interviewer for them, or state assumptions. Detail is in Chapter 41; availability arithmetic is in Chapter 2 (99.9 percent is about 8.8 hours of downtime a year, 99.99 percent about 53 minutes).

7. The 10-point failure walkthrough script

Say these aloud, in order, after your main design is on the board. Each point is one or two sentences; skip those not relevant, but keep the order so you never freeze.

  1. Single points of failure. "I walk the request path and check each box has redundancy across AZs: LB, gateway, services, cache, DB, queue." (41)
  2. Failure model. "For each dependency I consider crash, slow, partial, wrong data and overload; slow is the dangerous one." (27)
  3. Timeouts and deadlines. "Every call has a timeout under the caller's deadline, so no thread waits forever." (40)
  4. Retries. "Retries are exponential with full jitter, at one layer only, under a retry budget, and only for idempotent calls." (40, 51)
  5. Idempotency. "Mutating endpoints take an idempotency key, and consumers dedupe, since delivery is at-least-once." (32)
  6. Isolation. "Bulkheads per dependency and tenant, circuit breakers on remote calls, cells to cap blast radius." (40, 42)
  7. Overload. "Rate limits at the edge, load shedding by priority, bounded queues with backpressure, autoscaling with headroom." (16)
  8. Async failures. "Failed messages retry with backoff, then go to a DLQ with alerts and a replay tool; the outbox keeps DB and broker consistent." (35, 31)
  9. Data durability and consistency. "State the sync or async choice per data class, the RPO, and what users see during failover; reconcile for cross-service flows." (24, 28, 48)
  10. Recovery and detection. "SLO-based alerts, runbooks, tested backups, DR strategy with stated RPO and RTO, canary deploys with automatic rollback, regular game days." (54, 41, 43)
Interview tip Anchor on the user-visible outcome. Instead of "the cache fails", say "the cache fails, the hit rate drops from 95 to 0 percent, the database sees 20x the reads, so we coalesce requests, shed low-priority reads and keep the checkout path alive." Numbers from Chapter 2 make that credible.
Watch out Common mistakes: adding retries without idempotency, "just add a replica" without naming the replication lag, claiming exactly-once delivery, forgetting that a failover target needs spare capacity, and never mentioning how you would detect the failure.

9. Recap

  • Walk the request path component by component, and for each name the failure type, detection, mitigation and user-visible result.
  • Slow and overloaded are worse than dead; cap waits, budget retries, isolate, shed and degrade on purpose.
  • Assume at-least-once delivery and make every mutation idempotent.
  • State RPO and RTO, choose a DR strategy to match, and say you will test it.
  • Finish with detection: SLO alerts, runbooks, canaries, game days.
