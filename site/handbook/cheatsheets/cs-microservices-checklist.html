Cheat sheet

Microservices Checklist

A review checklist for any microservice design, in question form. Each item has the question an experienced reviewer asks, what a good answer sounds like, and the chapter that teaches it. Use it to critique your own whiteboard design before the interviewer does.

1. How to use this page

Microservices trade simple in-process calls for independent deployment and scaling, and pay for it with network failure, data duplication and operational load (Chapter 44). A review therefore checks twelve areas in order: boundaries, data, communication, contracts, consistency, resilience, discovery and configuration, security, observability, deployment and testing, ownership, and smells. The first question is always whether to split at all.

Key idea A good microservice is independently deployable, owns its data, and is owned by one team. If a change usually needs two services released together, or two services read the same tables, you have a distributed monolith: all the costs and none of the benefits.

2. Boundaries

QuestionGood answerChapter
Why is this a separate service?A concrete reason: different scaling profile, different release cadence, a team boundary, different availability or compliance need, or a different technology need. "Because microservices" is not a reason.44
Does the boundary follow a business capability or bounded context?Yes: Orders, Payments, Catalog, not "UserDAO service". Names come from the domain language, and one context has one consistent model.45, 39
Can the common change be made inside one service?Walk three likely features; each touches one service. If most touch three, the seams are wrong (high coupling, low cohesion).45
Is the granularity right?Sized to what one team (about 5 to 9 people) can own and rebuild; not a nano-service per function and not a mini-monolith.45
Would a modular monolith do for now?For a small team or an unclear domain, start with a modular monolith with enforced module boundaries, and extract later along the proven seams (strangler fig).44, 39

3. Data ownership

QuestionGood answerChapter
Who is the single owner of each piece of data?Exactly one service writes it and is the source of truth; others hold read-only copies, or ask via API.46
Does any other service touch this database directly?No. Access only through the owner's API or events; a shared schema is hidden coupling and blocks independent change.46
How do we answer queries that span services?API composition for small cases; a CQRS read model built from events for heavy or frequent joins; never a distributed join at request time over many services.46, 36
How do copies of data stay in sync?The owner publishes change events (through an outbox or change data capture); consumers update local copies, accept eventual consistency and handle reordering and duplicates.36, 31
Does each service use the right store?Per-service choice justified by access patterns (relational for transactions, document or key-value for scale), without adding stores nobody can operate.20, 17
How are reports and analytics served?Events or CDC feed a warehouse; analytics never queries operational databases directly.36, 23

4. Communication

QuestionGood answerChapter
Which calls must be synchronous?Only where the caller needs the answer now (reads for a user request). Everything that can complete later (notifications, projections, fulfilment) is async by event or queue.47, 36
How long is the synchronous call chain?Short: ideally depth 2 or 3. Availability multiplies: five services at 99.9 percent each give about 99.5 percent combined.51, 2
REST, gRPC or messaging, and why?REST at the public edge and for simple resources; gRPC for internal low-latency typed calls; messaging for decoupling, buffering and fan-out.47, 5, 7
Do clients call dozens of services?No: a gateway or BFF aggregates, handles auth and rate limits, and shields internal topology.11
Event or command?Events state facts that happened (OrderPlaced) and the publisher does not care who listens; commands target one handler. Do not disguise commands as events.36, 35
Is delivery assumed at-least-once?Yes, with idempotent consumers and dedup keys; ordering only per key or partition.32, 34

5. Contracts and versioning

QuestionGood answerChapter
Is the contract explicit and machine-readable?OpenAPI, protobuf or Avro schemas in a repository, generated clients, reviewed like code.47, 7
How do we change it without breaking consumers?Additive changes only (new optional fields); never reuse or retype a field; tolerant readers; deprecate, measure usage, then remove. Expand then contract for breaking changes.47, 53
How do we know a change is compatible?Consumer-driven contract tests in CI; schema registry compatibility checks for events (backward, forward or full).47
How are API versions handled?Version in path or header only for truly breaking changes, with an announced sunset; support at most two live versions.5
Do events leak internal models?No; publish a deliberate public event schema, not the database row.36

6. Sagas and consistency

QuestionGood answerChapter
Which business operations span services?List them (place order, refund, onboarding). Each gets an explicit saga, with a defined start, steps, compensations and end states.48, 31
Choreography or orchestration?Choreography (events) for 2 to 3 simple steps; an orchestrator (workflow engine) for longer flows, timeouts and visibility.48, 38
What is the compensation for each step?A semantic undo (refund, release stock), itself idempotent and retryable; steps that cannot be undone (email sent) go last.48
How do we publish an event and update the DB atomically?Transactional outbox plus relay (or CDC); consumers use an inbox or dedup table.48, 31
What happens when a saga step times out or stalls?Timeouts per step, a stuck-saga detector and a reconciliation job; humans get an alert and a tool.48
Which data must be strongly consistent?Keep it inside one service and one transaction (for example a balance). Everything else accepts eventual consistency, and the UI is designed for it (pending states).28

7. Resilience

QuestionGood answerChapter
What if a dependency is slow, not down?Timeout on every call, within an end-to-end deadline budget; circuit breaker; bulkhead; fallback.40, 51
Could retries cause a storm?Retry at one layer with backoff, full jitter and a budget; others pass the error through.51
Can one noisy caller or tenant take us down?Per-client quotas, rate limits, priority classes and shedding; cells for large tenants.16, 42
Is there a hard dependency cycle?No cycles; startup does not need dependencies to be up; the service degrades instead of crashing.51
What is the degraded mode?Named per feature (stale data, hide recommendations, queue writes) and tested with fault injection.40, 41

8. Discovery, configuration and mesh

QuestionGood answerChapter
How do services find each other?DNS or platform service discovery (for example Kubernetes services), client-side or server-side load balancing; health-checked registry; no hard-coded IPs.49
Where does configuration live?Externalized, versioned, per environment, changeable without rebuild; secrets in a secret manager, never in images or git.49, 55
Do we need a service mesh?Only with many services and a need for uniform mTLS, retries, traffic shifting and telemetry; accept sidecar cost and complexity. Otherwise use libraries and the gateway.50
How does a bad config push get contained?Staged rollout, validation, instant rollback, and config changes treated like code deploys.43

9. Security

QuestionGood answerChapter
Who is the caller, end to end?Authenticate at the edge (OAuth2/OIDC token); pass identity inward as a signed token; services verify, not just trust the network.55
Is service-to-service traffic trusted?Zero trust: mTLS with short-lived certificates and per-service authorization policies (least privilege).53, 50
Where is authorization decided?Coarse checks at the gateway, fine-grained ones in the owning service next to the data.55, 11
How are secrets and PII handled?Secret manager with rotation, encryption in transit and at rest, PII minimized and stored in one service where possible, with residency rules.55, 56

10. Observability

QuestionGood answerChapter
Can we follow one request across all services?A correlation or trace id generated at the edge and propagated through HTTP headers and message metadata; distributed tracing with sampling.52
What are the SLIs and SLOs per service?Availability and latency (p99) measured at the user-facing boundary; error budgets decide whether to ship or fix reliability.54
Are logs usable?Structured, centralized, with trace id, tenant and version, and a retention policy.52
Do alerts page on symptoms?Alert on SLO burn rate and golden signals (latency, traffic, errors, saturation), with runbooks, not on every CPU spike.54
Can we see dependencies?Service map from traces, with per-dependency latency and error panels.52

11. Deployment, testing, ownership

QuestionGood answerChapter
Can this ship alone, any day?Yes: own pipeline, backward-compatible changes, no lockstep releases or shared release trains.53
How is a release made safe?Canary or blue/green, feature flags, automated rollback on SLO regression; database changes via expand and contract.43, 26
What is the testing strategy?Many unit tests, component tests with fakes, contract tests between services, few end-to-end tests; production checks through synthetic probes and canaries.53
Does it scale on its own?Stateless, autoscaled on load or queue lag, with resource limits and a tested cold-start time.43, 8
Who owns it and who is paged?One named team owns code, data, dashboards and on-call ("you build it, you run it"), with a service catalog entry, runbook and SLO.53, 44
Does the team structure match the architecture?Yes (Conway's Law): a service that needs three teams to change is a boundary mistake or an organization mistake.44
What is the platform cost?Each service adds pipeline, monitoring, on-call and infrastructure; a paved road (templates, shared tooling) keeps it affordable.53

13. Ten-second review script

  1. "Why split?" Name the scaling, team or release reason (44).
  2. "Seams follow capabilities, and typical changes touch one service" (45).
  3. "Each service owns its data; cross-service reads use APIs or read models" (46).
  4. "Sync on the user path, events for the rest; chains are short" (47).
  5. "Contracts are versioned, additive, and contract-tested" (47).
  6. "Cross-service operations are sagas with outbox and idempotency" (48).
  7. "Timeouts, budgeted retries, breakers, bulkheads, degraded modes" (51).
  8. "Discovery, config and secrets are externalized; mTLS between services" (49, 50).
  9. "Traces, structured logs and SLOs per service" (52).
  10. "Independent canary deploys, one owning team on call" (53).
Interview tip Volunteer the counterargument: say what the design costs (network failure modes, eventual consistency, operational overhead) and when you would keep it a modular monolith. Interviewers reward the candidate who does not treat microservices as free.
Watch out Do not draw twelve services for a problem a team of five could solve with one. Count the services against the number of teams and the real scaling or release differences before you commit.

14. Recap

  • Check shape first: boundaries from business capabilities, data owned by exactly one service, short synchronous chains.
  • Contracts are explicit, additive and tested; cross-service work is a saga with outbox, idempotency and reconciliation.
  • Assume dependencies get slow: timeouts, budgeted retries, breakers, bulkheads, degraded modes.
  • Externalize discovery, config and secrets; authenticate every hop; trace every request.
  • Ship independently with canaries; one team owns, runs and is paged for each service.
  • Watch for distributed-monolith smells: shared database, lockstep deploys, chatty calls, orphaned services.
