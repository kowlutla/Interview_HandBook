Chapter 47 · Module 7 · Microservices

Service Communication and Contracts

Once a system is split into services (Chapter 44, Chapter 45, Chapter 46), everything those services do together happens over a network, and the agreement about what they say to each other becomes the most important thing you own. This chapter covers how to choose between synchronous calls and asynchronous messages, what chains of calls do to availability and latency, how to write contracts and evolve them without breaking anyone, and how to handle errors, retries, and deadlines across service boundaries.

1. Why communication is the hard part of microservices

In a monolith, one part of the code calls another by calling a function. That call takes nanoseconds, cannot be lost, cannot time out, and is checked by the compiler: if you rename a parameter, the build fails until every caller is fixed. When you split the monolith into services, each of those function calls may become a network call, and every one of those guarantees disappears. The call can be slow, can fail halfway, can be delivered twice, and the "compiler" that used to check both sides is now a separate team's deployment pipeline that you cannot see.

Two questions therefore decide whether a microservice architecture works in practice:

  1. How do services talk? Do they call each other and wait (synchronous), or hand over a message and move on (asynchronous)? This choice determines how failures spread and how fast the system feels.
  2. What exactly do they promise each other? The shape of requests, responses, and events, plus the rules for changing that shape over time, is the contract. Teams deploy independently only if the contract is explicit and changes to it are safe.
Key idea A service boundary is a public API even if it is "internal". The moment a second team depends on it, you have customers, and the rules of public API design (stability, compatibility, deprecation, documentation) apply. Most painful microservice incidents are not caused by a service being down. They are caused by one service changing something another service silently relied on.

This chapter owns the cross-service communication view. The details of REST, HTTP semantics, pagination and GraphQL syntax live in Chapter 5; serialization formats and their encoding costs live in Chapter 7; the internals of brokers and logs live in Chapter 34; and coordinating multi-service business transactions is the subject of Chapter 48. Here we focus on the decisions and the contract discipline that sit between them.

2. Synchronous versus asynchronous communication

2.1 Synchronous request/response

In synchronous communication the caller sends a request and waits for the reply before continuing. The caller is blocked (or holds a connection and a thread or coroutine) for the duration. The two protocols you will meet most are:

  • REST over HTTP/JSON. Resources identified by URLs, standard verbs (GET, POST, PUT, DELETE), standard status codes, human-readable bodies. It is universal, easy to debug with a browser or curl, cacheable by HTTP infrastructure, and works across organisation boundaries. The costs are verbose text payloads and no built-in schema enforcement unless you add one (OpenAPI, section 6). See Chapter 5.
  • gRPC over HTTP/2 with Protocol Buffers. You define services and messages in a .proto file and generate client and server code. Payloads are compact binary, one connection carries many concurrent calls, and the framework gives you deadlines, streaming, and typed stubs. The costs are poor browser support without a proxy, harder manual debugging, and some load-balancing traps (section 15).

The defining property of both is that the answer comes back on the same connection while the caller waits. That makes the programming model simple (you write total = pricing.quote(cart) and use the result on the next line), but it also welds the caller's fate to the callee's, as the next sections show.

2.2 Asynchronous messaging and events

In asynchronous communication the sender hands a message to an intermediary (a queue or a log such as Kafka, Chapter 34) and continues without waiting for the receiver to process it. The receiver consumes the message whenever it is ready. There are two flavours worth separating:

  • A command (or task message) says "please do this": ChargeCard(order 42, 30.00). There is one intended handler, and the sender cares that the work happens.
  • An event says "this happened": OrderPlaced(order 42). The sender does not know or care who listens; zero, one, or twenty consumers can react. Events are facts in the past tense and are not requests.

The sender and receiver are decoupled in time (the receiver may be down or slow and the message waits), and often in knowledge (the publisher does not know the consumers). The price is that the programming model is harder: there is no immediate answer, results arrive later (or via another message), delivery is typically at-least-once so consumers must tolerate duplicates (Chapter 32), and tracing "what happened to order 42" means following messages through several systems.

2.3 A decision framework

Neither style is better in general. Use the questions below, in order, for each interaction between two services rather than choosing one style for the whole system.

  1. Does the caller need the answer to continue? A price quote while rendering a cart: yes, so synchronous. Sending a receipt email: no, so asynchronous.
  2. Must the work succeed before you can tell the user "done"? If the user-visible promise depends on it (the card was actually authorised), you need the result. If you can honestly say "we received your order and will confirm", asynchronous is fine.
  3. Is it a query or a change? Reads that need fresh data from the owner are naturally synchronous. State changes that other services want to react to are naturally events.
  4. How many consumers, and do you know them? One known callee suggests a call or a command. An open-ended set of reactors suggests publishing an event.
  5. What if the callee is down for ten minutes? If the business can tolerate delay, put a queue in between and the outage becomes a backlog. If it cannot, you need redundancy and a fallback instead (Chapter 40).
  6. What are the latency and load shapes? Spiky load that the callee cannot absorb is a strong reason for a queue as a shock absorber. Strict end-to-end latency budgets argue for direct calls.

3. Temporal coupling and the availability of call chains

Temporal coupling means two services must both be healthy at the same instant for a request to succeed. Every synchronous call adds one such requirement. The consequences can be calculated.

3.1 The multiplication rule

If a request needs services A, B, C in series, and each one is independently available with probability p, the request succeeds with probability p times p times p. Services that are each 99.9 percent available ("three nines") sound excellent, but:

Services in the synchronous chainEach at 99.9%Downtime per 30 daysEach at 99.99%
199.900%about 43 minutes99.990%
399.700%about 2.2 hours99.970%
599.501%about 3.6 hours99.950%
1099.005%about 7.2 hours99.900%
2098.019%about 14.3 hours99.800%

The numbers are computed as 0.999 raised to the number of services; a month of 43,200 minutes at 99.005 percent leaves about 430 minutes (7.2 hours) of failed requests. The lesson is that the availability of a request path is below the availability of its weakest member, and falls with every extra hop. A checkout that depends synchronously on ten services each at 99.9 percent cannot promise even "two nines and a half".

3.2 Latency compounds too

Latency adds along a chain, but tail latency is worse than it looks. If each of five services has a 99th percentile of 100 ms, the chance that at least one of five calls lands in its slow 1 percent tail is about 1 minus 0.99 to the 5th, close to 5 percent. So roughly one in twenty end-to-end requests sees a "p99-slow" hop. With fan-out of 50 parallel calls the probability is about 40 percent. This is the tail-at-scale effect covered in Chapter 40, and it is why call depth and fan-out are design variables, not details.

3.3 Ways to shorten and soften the chain

  • Remove the dependency from the critical path by turning "call B" into "publish an event B reacts to" when the user does not need B's answer.
  • Own the data you need through a local read model or cache fed by events (Chapter 46), so a query does not need the owner to be up.
  • Degrade gracefully: if recommendations are down, render the page without them. Mark dependencies as essential or optional and handle optional ones with fallbacks.
  • Add redundancy and limits: timeouts, circuit breakers and bulkheads so a slow callee does not exhaust the caller (Chapter 40).

4. Fan-out, call depth, and cascading load

Fan-out is how many downstream calls one incoming request triggers. Call depth is how many hops deep the longest chain goes. Both grow quietly as teams add features, and both hurt in specific ways.

4.1 What goes wrong

  • Latency of the slowest. A page that fans out to 20 services in parallel is as slow as the slowest of the 20, so tail latency dominates (section 3.2).
  • Amplification. One user request at the edge may produce 30 internal calls. A 1,000 requests per second front door becomes 30,000 internal calls per second, and a small slowdown in one deep service multiplies into queued threads upstream.
  • Retry storms. If each layer retries three times, a failing leaf at depth 4 sees up to 3 to the 4th, which is 81 times the original load, exactly when it is least able to cope. Retries belong at one layer, not all of them, plus retry budgets (Chapter 40).
  • Distributed deadlock-like cycles. Service A calls B which calls A back. Under load both pools are full of requests waiting for each other. Cycles in the call graph are a design bug; break them with events or by merging the services.
  • Unclear ownership of failures. With depth 7, the on-call engineer for the front service is paged for a bug in a service they have never heard of.

4.2 Rules of thumb

  • Keep synchronous call depth small, often cited as 2 to 3 hops for user-facing paths. Beyond that, ask whether service boundaries are drawn badly (Chapter 45) or whether some of the calls can be asynchronous.
  • Parallelise independent calls and set a per-call timeout and an overall deadline (section 13), returning partial results where possible (section 12.3).
  • Budget fan-out: a front-door request should not trigger an unbounded number of internal calls. Paginate, batch, and cache.
  • Draw the call graph. If you cannot draw it on one page, nobody can reason about its failure modes. Distributed tracing (Chapter 40 and the observability chapters) makes the real graph visible.

5. API contracts: what they are and how to write them down

A contract is the full set of promises a provider makes to its consumers: the operations or topics it offers, the shape and meaning of the data, the error behaviour, the performance and availability expectations, and the rules for how all of that may change. Part of the contract can be machine-checked (the shapes), and part is only written down (semantics such as "amount is in minor units, so 1250 means 12.50").

5.1 Interface description languages

An IDL (interface definition language) describes an API in a neutral, machine-readable form, so that documentation, client code, server stubs, mocks, and compatibility checks can all be generated from one source of truth. The common ones:

ToolDescribesNotes

Protocol Buffers (.proto)gRPC services and messages; also a common message encoding for KafkaFields are identified by numbers, not names, which is the key to safe evolution (section 6).

5.2 Schema registries

For event streams the contract has to be available at runtime, not only at build time. A schema registry is a service that stores every version of every schema, assigns each an id, and enforces a compatibility mode when a producer tries to register a new version. A typical flow with Kafka:

  1. The producer registers the schema for topic orders. The registry checks it against previous versions under the configured rule (for example "backward compatible") and rejects it if it would break consumers.
  2. The registry returns a small numeric schema id. The producer writes each message as a few bytes of id plus the compact binary payload, instead of repeating field names.
  3. The consumer reads the id, fetches (and caches) the schema from the registry, and decodes the payload, resolving it against the schema version it was built with.

The registry turns "someone changed the message format and broke three consumers at 2 a.m." into a failed build for the person making the change. Pair it with a CI step so incompatible schemas never reach production. Details of the encodings and their evolution rules are in Chapter 7.

Watch out A schema describes shape, not meaning. If a producer starts sending amount in dollars instead of cents, every schema check still passes. Semantics need documentation, examples, and consumer-driven tests (section 8).

6. Compatibility: the rules that make independent deployment possible

Services deploy on different schedules, so at any moment old and new versions of producers and consumers coexist. Compatibility rules define which combinations still work.

6.1 Backward and forward compatibility

  • Backward compatible: a new consumer can read data written by an old producer. Think "new code, old data".
  • Forward compatible: an old consumer can read data written by a new producer. Think "old code, new data".
  • Full compatibility: both hold. This is the target for anything with long-lived data (event logs, mobile clients you cannot force to upgrade).

For request/response APIs, the usual framing is from the consumer's side: can an existing client keep working after the server changes (server changes must be backward compatible for old clients) and can a newer client talk to an older server during a rollout (forward compatibility)? For event logs, both directions matter because old events are replayed to new consumers and new events reach old consumers.

6.2 The practical rules

6.3 Tolerant reader and the robustness principle

The tolerant reader pattern says a consumer should take only what it needs from a message and ignore everything else. It must not fail because of unknown extra fields, and must not depend on field order or on the absence of fields it does not use. Combined with a producer that only makes additive changes, this lets the producer evolve freely. Practical consequences:

  • Configure JSON deserializers to ignore unknown properties (many default to failing, which silently turns every additive change into a breaking one).
  • Do not validate a response against a strict full-schema in production code paths, only against the parts you use. (Strict validation in tests of your own provider is a different matter.)
  • Use sensible defaults when optional fields are absent.
  • The other half, sometimes called "be conservative in what you send", means providers should not emit surprising values: no new status strings in a field consumers switch on without warning.

6.4 Never repurpose fields; evolving enums

In Protocol Buffers each field has a permanent field number used on the wire. If you delete field 4 and later add a different field with number 4, old messages stored on disk or in a log will be decoded as the new field, producing garbage with no error. The rule: never reuse a field number or name; mark removed ones as reserved so the compiler stops anyone from reusing them. In JSON the same applies to names: do not turn status from "order status" into "payment status".

Enums are the classic trap. A consumer written as switch(status) with cases PLACED, SHIPPED, DELIVERED works until the producer adds CANCELLED and the consumer hits an unhandled case, crashes, or worse, treats it as DELIVERED. Mitigations:

  • Consumers must have a defined default branch for unknown values ("show as Unknown", "skip", "route to dead-letter") and never assume the set is closed.
  • In Protocol Buffers, the first enum value should be a zero default such as STATUS_UNSPECIFIED; unknown numeric values decoded by older code are preserved as numbers in proto3 rather than failing.
  • Treat adding an enum value as a documented, announced change, and for enums consumers switch on, consider it as risky as adding a field consumers must handle.
  • Prefer representing open-ended categories as strings plus documentation, or as an enum plus an explicit "other" escape, when the set is likely to grow.

7. Versioning strategies and deprecation

Additive evolution handles most changes. When a change is genuinely breaking (a field's meaning changes, a resource is restructured), you need a way to run old and new behaviours side by side. That is versioning.

Key idea The best version strategy is to need versions rarely. Make additive, backward compatible changes by default (field-level evolution). Reserve a new major version for changes that cannot be made additively, and treat each major version as a product you must operate in parallel and eventually retire.

7.1 A deprecation process that actually works

  1. Announce with the replacement and a date. Put it in the OpenAPI/proto (a deprecated flag), the changelog, and a direct message to known consumers.
  2. Signal in-band: return a Deprecation and Sunset header (these are standard HTTP header names) and log a warning-level metric each time a deprecated element is used, tagged by calling client.
  3. Measure: build a dashboard of which consumers still use the old element. You cannot remove what you cannot see; the gateway or service mesh (Chapter 50) can attribute calls to callers.
  4. Chase the stragglers: contact owners, offer migration help, escalate near the date.
  5. Brown-out: shortly before removal, deliberately fail the old path for short windows to flush out forgotten callers.
  6. Remove only when usage is zero (or an agreed small residue is accepted by the business), then delete the code and the tests.

Internal APIs deserve the same process on a shorter clock. "We own all the consumers" is rarely true for long.

8. Consumer-driven contract testing (Pact)

Schemas guarantee shape; they do not tell you which parts consumers actually use. End-to-end tests across many services are slow, flaky, and cannot run in one team's pipeline. Consumer-driven contract testing fills the gap: each consumer writes down exactly what it expects from the provider, and the provider verifies those expectations in its own build.

8.1 How Pact works

Pact is the best-known framework (the idea is available for HTTP and for messages). The workflow:

  1. Consumer test. In the consumer's unit tests, the developer describes an interaction: "when I send GET /orders/42 with this header, I expect status 200 and a JSON body containing id (a string) and total (a number)". The Pact library runs the consumer code against a mock provider that replies as specified, so the consumer is tested without a real provider.
  2. Pact file. A successful run writes a pact, a JSON document listing the interactions the consumer relied upon. Only the fields the consumer actually reads are included, which keeps the contract minimal and honours the tolerant reader idea.
  3. Publish. The consumer's CI publishes the pact to a broker (the Pact Broker or a hosted equivalent), tagged with the consumer version and branch.
  4. Provider verification. The provider's CI fetches all pacts from consumers that depend on it, replays each interaction against the real provider code (with test data prepared by "provider states", for example "an order 42 exists"), and checks the responses match.
  5. Publish results and gate deploys. Verification results go back to the broker. A "can I deploy?" check answers whether this version of this service is compatible with the versions of its partners currently in each environment, blocking an unsafe release.

8.2 What it gives you, and its limits

  • Fast, isolated, per-service tests that catch breaking changes at build time instead of in a shared staging environment.
  • A map of real usage: the pacts show which consumers use which fields, so a provider knows what it can safely remove.
  • Not a substitute for functional testing: pacts check that the provider can satisfy the consumer's expectations, not that business logic is right. They also do not cover performance or full end-to-end flows. Keep a thin layer of end-to-end smoke tests.
  • Needs a culture: consumers must write and maintain pacts, and the provider must treat a failing pact as a stop sign, not a nuisance. Without that, the broker becomes shelfware.

The same idea works for events: a consumer pact for a message states "I need an OrderPlaced event with orderId and items[]", and the producer verifies that its serialization can satisfy it. Schema registry compatibility checks (section 5.2) and message pacts complement each other: the registry stops structural breaks, pacts catch "this field you thought was optional is one I rely on".

9. Contract-first design, ownership, and governance

9.1 Contract-first versus code-first

Code-first means you write the service and generate the API description from the code. It is fast to start, but the contract becomes whatever the code happens to do, and it changes accidentally when someone refactors a class. Contract-first means you write the OpenAPI, proto, or AsyncAPI document first, review it with consumers, then generate server stubs and client SDKs from it and implement behind them.

Contract-first has real advantages: consumers can start building against a mock server before the provider exists (so teams work in parallel), the contract is reviewed as a deliberate artefact in a pull request, and breaking changes show up as diffs in the contract file, where a linter or compatibility checker (for example a tool that diffs two OpenAPI versions, or a proto breaking-change detector) can fail the build. Code-first remains reasonable for small internal APIs with one consumer; the important thing is that the published contract is checked in, versioned, and diffed in CI either way.

9.2 Ownership

  • Every API and every event topic has exactly one owning team that is listed in a catalog (for example a developer portal) with an on-call contact, a status (experimental, stable, deprecated), and its schemas.
  • The producer owns the schema and compatibility; consumers own how they interpret it. A consumer does not get to demand a change by threatening to break, but it does get to register its expectations through a pact.
  • Data ownership follows service ownership (Chapter 46): the service that owns the data owns the contract for it. Nobody reaches into another service's database as an informal API, which is the most common source of hidden coupling.

9.3 Governance without a bottleneck

Governance means keeping hundreds of APIs consistent and safe without making a central committee approve every change. The scalable approach is automation plus lightweight review:

  • A shared style guide (naming, pagination, error shape, idempotency headers, date formats) enforced by an API linter in CI.
  • Automatic breaking-change detection in the pipeline: a pull request that makes an incompatible change to a published contract fails unless it carries an explicit, reviewed exception and a migration plan.
  • A registry or catalog as the single place to discover APIs, with ownership and deprecation status.
  • A small API review group consulted for new public or cross-domain APIs, not for every field.
  • Metrics: per-API usage by consumer, error rates, and deprecated-field usage, which feed the deprecation process (section 7.1).

10. Handling breaking changes: expand and contract

Sometimes a breaking change is unavoidable (splitting a field, changing a type, moving to a new resource shape). The way to ship it with zero downtime and no coordinated big-bang deploy is the expand and contract pattern (also called parallel change). It breaks a breaking change into a sequence of individually safe, backward compatible steps:

  1. Expand. The provider adds the new representation alongside the old one. Both exist and carry the same information. Nothing breaks, because old consumers still read the old field.
  2. Migrate. Consumers move, one at a time and on their own schedule, to the new representation. The provider tracks which consumers still read the old one.
  3. Contract. When no consumer uses the old representation, the provider removes it.

The same three phases apply to database columns, event schemas, and API versions. For writes, you may also need the provider to accept both forms and to write both forms during the migration so that old and new readers see consistent data.

When even that is not enough (for instance, a new resource model), run two versions in parallel behind the gateway (Chapter 11) and translate in one place: either the new version is implemented on top of the old, or an adapter layer maps between them. The monolith migration technique in Chapter 44 (the strangler fig) uses the same idea.

11. Data formats in messages

The wire format affects size, speed, evolvability, and debuggability. A fuller comparison is in Chapter 7; here is how to choose for service-to-service traffic:

Choices that matter more than the encoding itself:

  • Money: send integer minor units plus an ISO 4217 currency code ({"amount": 1250, "currency": "USD"}), never floating point.
  • Time: use UTC timestamps in ISO 8601 or epoch milliseconds, never local time without a zone. Make clear whether a field is an instant or a calendar date.
  • Identifiers: opaque strings, not integers that consumers might do arithmetic on, and not exposing internal sequence numbers. See Chapter 32.
  • Envelope: events should carry metadata separate from the business payload: event id, type, schema version, timestamp, producer, correlation and causation ids for tracing.
  • Size: keep messages small. For large payloads send a reference (object store URL) plus a checksum (the "claim check" pattern) rather than megabytes through the broker.

12. Error contracts, status mapping, and partial failure

The error behaviour of an API is part of its contract, and it is the part most often left vague. Callers need to decide, from the error alone, whether to retry, to give up, or to show the user a message.

12.1 A consistent error shape

Use one error body across all services. A widely used standard for HTTP APIs is Problem Details (RFC 9457, formerly RFC 7807):

HTTP/1.1 409 Conflict
Content-Type: application/problem+json

{
  "type": "https://api.example.com/errors/insufficient-stock",
  "title": "Insufficient stock",
  "status": 409,
  "detail": "Only 2 units of SKU-123 remain.",
  "instance": "/orders/42",
  "traceId": "4bf92f3577b34da6",
  "retryable": false
}

The pieces: a stable machine-readable type or error code consumers can branch on (not the English message), a human detail, the trace id for support, and an explicit signal about retrying.

First, do not flatten every downstream failure to a 500: a validation error in a downstream service that surfaces as 500 invites retries that can never succeed. Second, do not leak internals: map a downstream's raw error to your own contract (a payments 402 does not become your 402 unless you mean it) and never return stack traces or internal hostnames.

12.3 Partial failure semantics

A call that spans several services, or a batch of items, can partly succeed. The contract must say what the caller can conclude:

  • Timeouts are ambiguous. A DEADLINE_EXCEEDED does not mean "did not happen"; the callee may have completed the work and the reply was lost. This is why retried writes need idempotency (section 13).
  • Batch calls should return per-item results (for example HTTP 207 style bodies or a list of statuses) so the caller can retry only the failed items, instead of an all-or-nothing response that hides what happened.
  • Aggregation calls (an aggregator calling five services) must decide per dependency whether failure is fatal. A profile page can return 200 with "recommendations": null and a "partial": ["recommendations"] marker while a missing account record is a hard failure.
  • Multi-service writes cannot rely on a distributed transaction. If payment succeeded and inventory then failed, you need compensation, which is the subject of Chapter 48 and of Chapter 31.

13. Idempotency, retries, timeouts, and deadline propagation

13.1 Idempotency across services

Because networks lose replies, a client that times out does not know whether the operation ran. The safe response is to retry, which only works if the operation is idempotent: doing it twice has the same effect as doing it once. Reads and PUT-style "set to this value" operations are naturally idempotent. Creating an order or charging a card is not, so the contract adds an idempotency key:

  1. The caller generates a unique key per logical operation (for example a UUID) and sends it as Idempotency-Key: 7f3c..., reusing the same key on every retry.
  2. The provider stores the key with the result (or an "in progress" marker) in the same transaction as the work itself.
  3. If the same key arrives again, the provider returns the stored result without redoing the work. If the key arrives with a different request body, it rejects it as a misuse.

The same principle applies to message consumers: with at-least-once delivery, a consumer must deduplicate by message id or make its handler idempotent (the inbox pattern, Chapter 48). Full treatment of keys, delivery semantics, and id generation is in Chapter 32.

13.2 Retry policy

  • Retry only errors that are plausibly transient (503, 504, connection reset, UNAVAILABLE), and only for idempotent operations.
  • Use exponential backoff with jitter, a cap on attempts, and a retry budget (for example, retries may add at most 10 percent to normal traffic) so retries cannot amplify an outage.
  • Retry at one layer. If the gateway, the service, and the client library all retry, the multiplication from section 4 appears.
  • Honour Retry-After and stop on non-retryable codes.

13.3 Timeouts and deadline propagation

Every network call needs a timeout, because the default in many libraries is "wait forever", and a thread stuck forever is how one slow service takes down its callers (Chapter 40). But a fixed per-hop timeout is not enough. If the user's request has a 2-second budget and service A spends 1.5 seconds before calling B with B's own 2-second timeout, B may keep working for 1.5 seconds after the user has already given up, wasting capacity on an answer nobody will read.

Deadline propagation fixes this: the entry point sets an absolute deadline (or remaining budget), and every service passes it downstream, so each hop uses the time that is left, not a fresh timeout. If the remaining budget is smaller than the minimum useful time, the service fails fast instead of starting hopeless work. gRPC does this natively; over HTTP you carry a header such as X-Request-Deadline or a remaining-milliseconds value.

Related rule: the timeout of a caller should be longer than the callee's own timeouts plus its retries only if you want the callee to finish; otherwise make caller timeouts shorter than the sum, and rely on the propagated deadline. Include p99 observed latencies when choosing numbers, not averages.

14. Service-to-service authentication (preview)

Inside the network, "trusted because it is internal" does not hold: one compromised service must not be able to impersonate all the others. Two ideas, previewed here and developed elsewhere:

  • Mutual TLS (mTLS): both sides present certificates, so each service proves its identity to the other and the traffic is encrypted. A service mesh sidecar can do this transparently for every call and rotate certificates automatically (Chapter 50).
  • Token propagation: the end user's identity and permissions must reach deep services, usually by passing a signed token (a JWT) issued at the edge, or by exchanging it for a narrowly scoped token per hop, so that downstream services can authorise on behalf of the user and not only on the identity of the caller (Chapter 55).

Contract implications: the API description should state required scopes per operation, and authorisation must be enforced by the owning service, not assumed from the gateway alone.

15. gRPC in depth

15.1 Call types

  • Unary: one request, one response. The default and the closest to a REST call.
  • Server streaming: one request, a stream of responses (a price feed, or paged results sent as they are ready).
  • Client streaming: a stream of requests, one response (uploading chunks, then a summary).
  • Bidirectional streaming: both sides send independent streams over the same call (chat, live sync).

Streams ride on a single HTTP/2 connection with multiplexing: many concurrent calls share one TCP connection without head-of-line blocking at the HTTP level. Flow control prevents a fast sender from overwhelming a slow receiver. Streaming contracts need extra rules: what happens on mid-stream failure, how a stream is resumed (include a cursor or sequence number), and when each side half-closes.

15.2 Deadlines and cancellation

Every gRPC client call should set a deadline. The deadline travels in the call metadata, the server can read the time remaining, and when it passes both sides see the call end with DEADLINE_EXCEEDED. If a server makes further gRPC calls while handling a request, passing the incoming context carries the deadline and the cancellation to them automatically. Cancellation also propagates when the client disconnects, so downstream work stops.

15.3 The load-balancing pitfall with long-lived connections

A traditional layer-4 load balancer distributes connections. With HTTP/1.1 and short connections that spreads requests well. gRPC opens one long-lived HTTP/2 connection and multiplexes thousands of calls on it, so a layer-4 balancer sees only one connection and pins all the traffic from that client to one backend. Symptoms: one backend pod at 90 percent CPU, others idle; new pods added during scaling receive no traffic from existing clients; rolling deployments make load lopsided.

Remedies, in rough order of operational simplicity: use an L7 proxy or service mesh (Chapter 50) that balances per request; use client-side load balancing with a headless service or name resolver that returns all backend addresses; or set a maximum connection age on servers so connections are recycled and re-balanced (a cheap mitigation, with brief reconnect churn). Also remember health checking and graceful shutdown (send GOAWAY, drain in-flight calls) so deployments do not drop requests.

15.4 Other gRPC contract points

  • Maximum message size defaults are small (commonly 4 MB); large payloads should be streamed or chunked.
  • Browsers cannot speak native gRPC; use gRPC-Web through a proxy, or expose REST at the edge and gRPC inside.
  • Use the status codes in section 12.2 and rich error details (structured error messages in the response trailer), and keep the proto package versioned (shop.order.v1) so a breaking redesign can be a new package.
  • Use a linter and breaking-change detector on .proto files in CI.

16. GraphQL federation, BFF, and the internal gateway

16.1 Backend for Frontend

A mobile app, a web app, and a partner integration need different data shapes and different payload sizes. A Backend for Frontend (BFF) is a thin service owned by the front-end team that composes calls to the underlying domain services into exactly what that client needs, so the domain services are not distorted by presentation concerns, and a client makes one request instead of ten. It is a place where the fan-out of section 4 is deliberately managed: parallel calls, timeouts, partial results, caching. The edge-facing gateway and BFF patterns are covered in Chapter 11.

16.2 GraphQL and federation

With GraphQL, the client sends a query naming precisely the fields it wants, and the server returns that shape. This removes over-fetching and many round trips from clients. In a microservice world there is still the question of who answers: a single monolithic GraphQL server would reintroduce a central bottleneck. Federation splits the graph: each domain service publishes its own subgraph schema (the Order service defines Order, the Customer service defines Customer, and the Order subgraph can add a field customer that refers to a Customer by key). A gateway or router composes the subgraph schemas into one supergraph, plans each incoming query across subgraphs, calls them in parallel where it can, and stitches the results.

  • Good: clients see one coherent schema; domain teams own their part; the router handles the join logic.
  • Costs: the router is critical infrastructure; query planning and N+1 fetch patterns need batching (data loaders) to avoid chatty calls between router and subgraphs; expensive queries need depth, complexity, and cost limits; caching is harder than with plain HTTP GET; and the composed schema is itself a contract needing compatibility checks, since a subgraph change can break the supergraph.

16.3 Internal API gateway

An internal gateway (or the mesh's ingress/egress) in front of groups of internal services gives a uniform place for authentication, rate limits, routing between versions, request logging, and traffic shaping for east-west traffic, much as the external gateway does for north-south traffic. Use it where it adds clear value, such as exposing a domain's API to many internal teams, shifting traffic between versions (canary and blue/green), or enforcing quotas on a noisy consumer. Do not funnel every service-to-service call through one central gateway: it becomes a latency tax, a single point of failure, and a team bottleneck. Mesh sidecars give many of the same controls without a central hop.

17. Event contracts, choreography, and synchronous-over-asynchronous

17.1 Designing event contracts

An event is a long-lived public contract because consumers appear over time, some replay history, and the producer often never learns who they are. Guidelines:

  • Name events as past-tense domain facts: OrderPlaced, PaymentCaptured, not UpdateInventory (which is a command in disguise that couples the publisher to a specific consumer's needs).
  • Choose the amount of data deliberately. An event notification carries just the id and type ("order 42 changed"), so consumers call back to fetch details; it is small but couples consumers back to the producer's API and load. An event-carried state transfer includes the data consumers typically need (items, total, address), so they act without calling back, at the cost of a larger and more binding payload. Many teams choose the second for core business events and keep sensitive fields out.
  • Include identity and ordering metadata: a unique event id (for deduplication), aggregate id and version or sequence number (for ordering and gap detection), timestamps, and a correlation id. Partition by aggregate id so events for one order stay in order (Chapter 34).
  • Publish reliably. Writing to your database and then publishing to the broker are two operations; a crash between them loses or duplicates events. The transactional outbox writes the event to a table in the same transaction and a relay publishes it (see Chapter 48 and Chapter 36).
  • Document it in AsyncAPI with the schema in the registry and compatibility enforced (section 5).

17.2 Choreography

In choreography each service reacts to events from others and publishes its own, with no central coordinator: Order publishes OrderPlaced; Inventory reserves stock and publishes StockReserved; Payment charges and publishes PaymentCaptured; Shipping reacts. It is loosely coupled and easy to extend (add a consumer without touching the producer), but the business flow is spread across services, so it is hard to see, to monitor ("where is order 42?"), and to change; cyclic event dependencies creep in. For complex multi-step business flows with compensation, an orchestrator is often clearer. That trade-off, with compensation and timeouts, is the topic of Chapter 48.

17.3 Synchronous-over-asynchronous

Sometimes the user-facing API must look synchronous while the work is asynchronous (slow, retried, or on a queue). Patterns:

  • Accept and poll. The API returns 202 Accepted with a Location header for a status resource. The client polls it (PENDING, COMPLETED, FAILED) with a suggested Retry-After. Simple and robust.
  • Accept and call back. The client supplies a webhook URL; the service posts the result when done (needs signing, retries, and idempotency on the receiving side; see Chapter 5).
  • Accept and push. Results are delivered via server-sent events or WebSocket (Chapter 6).
  • Request/reply over a queue. The caller publishes a request with a replyTo queue and a correlation id, then blocks waiting for the matching reply with a timeout. It keeps a synchronous feel for the caller while still buffering through the broker, but reintroduces temporal coupling for the waiting caller and needs care for timeouts, orphaned replies, and duplicates, so use it sparingly.

18. Fixing chatty APIs

An API is chatty when a client must make many small calls to accomplish one logical task: fetching an order, then each line item, then each product, then each price. Over a network each call pays latency, serialization, authentication, and queueing costs, and the failures multiply (section 3). Fixes, in order of how often they are the right answer:

  • Coarse-grained operations. Design endpoints around the client's use case, not around database tables: GET /orders/42?include=items,shipping or a dedicated GET /order-summary/42 returns what the screen needs in one call. Beware of overdoing it into giant responses; allow field selection or expansion so clients take only what they need.
  • Batch endpoints. POST /products:batchGet with 100 ids replaces 100 calls (and return per-item results; section 12.3). Cap batch sizes and define behaviour for partial failure.
  • Aggregation in a BFF or GraphQL router (section 16), which calls the services in parallel close to them, where latency is a millisecond rather than a mobile network's 100 ms.
  • Data loader pattern. Inside a service, collect the ids requested within one request and fetch them together, avoiding the N+1 query pattern.
  • Caching at the client, gateway, or service (Chapter 13), with HTTP validators (ETag) for cheap revalidation.
  • Local read model. If service A constantly asks service B for the same reference data, replicate it into A through events and read it locally (Chapter 46). This also removes the runtime dependency.
  • Revisit boundaries. If two services talk to each other constantly about the same data, they may belong together (Chapter 45).
  • Binary protocol and connection reuse (gRPC, HTTP/2 keep-alive) reduce per-call overhead, but do not fix a fundamentally chatty design.

19. Worked example: an Order API across three releases

We design the contract for an Order service used by a web checkout, a mobile app, a warehouse service, and an analytics pipeline, and evolve it through three releases without ever forcing a consumer to deploy in lockstep.

19.1 Release 1: the first contract

Contract-first, in OpenAPI for the synchronous API and AsyncAPI for events. The first version, published as v1 in the URL, because it is a public product API that mobile apps hardcode:

POST /v1/orders                 (header: Idempotency-Key)
{
  "customerId": "c_81",
  "items": [ {"sku": "SKU-123", "qty": 2} ],
  "total": 25.00                  // decimal dollars, USD
}
201 Created
{
  "id": "o_42", "status": "PLACED",
  "customerId": "c_81",
  "items": [ {"sku": "SKU-123", "qty": 2} ],
  "total": 25.00
}
GET /v1/orders/o_42
Event on topic orders: OrderPlaced {eventId, orderId, customerId, items[], total}

Decisions and reasons: the idempotency key makes retried POSTs safe (section 13); ids are opaque strings; status is a documented string enum with a statement "clients must tolerate unknown values"; errors use Problem Details (section 12); the OpenAPI file is linted and checked in; the warehouse and analytics register pacts; the OrderPlaced schema is in the registry with backward compatibility enforced. Synchronous calls on the critical path are limited to inventory reserve and payment authorise; email, loyalty, and analytics hang off the event.

Three mistakes are already visible in hindsight, and they are exactly the sort found in real systems: total is a floating-point decimal, there is no currency, and there is no way to express a discount. The rest of the example fixes them through compatible evolution.

19.2 Release 2: additive changes, no version bump

Business needs: multi-currency, line-level prices, and a discount. Using the rules in section 6:

  • Add currency (optional, default USD when absent from requests, always present in responses).
  • Add totalMinor (integer, minor units) beside total; mark total deprecated in the OpenAPI document. This is the expand step of section 10.
  • Add items[].unitPriceMinor and an optional discount object.
  • Add status value CANCELLED. Because the contract has always said "tolerate unknown statuses" and the pacts of the web and warehouse consumers show a default branch, this is acceptable. The analytics job's pact does not, so the team contacts them first; this is the governance process catching an enum risk (section 6.4).
  • Update the event with the same additive fields. The registry accepts it under backward compatibility, and consumers that ignore unknown fields keep working.

Rollout: no consumer has to change in the same deploy. Pact verification runs in the Order service's pipeline against the three consumers' pacts and passes. A dashboard records which clients still read total (the migrate step). Old mobile app versions will take months to disappear, so the field stays.

19.3 Release 3: the breaking change, and the contract step

Two further needs arrive. The checkout flow must become asynchronous because payment authorisation with an external provider is slow, and the items structure must change to support bundles (an item that contains other items), which cannot be added compatibly to the old array.

  • Asynchronous placement: a new v2 endpoint, POST /v2/orders, returns 202 Accepted with a Location header and a status resource (section 17.3), and the final outcome is delivered as OrderConfirmed or OrderRejected events and via the polled status. v1 stays, implemented as an adapter that calls the same core and waits up to a few seconds, preserving its synchronous behaviour for old clients.
  • Bundles: v2 introduces lines[] with nested components[]. The event moves to a new topic orders.v2 with a new schema; for a transition period the service dual-publishes to both topics (sections 7 and 10), with a translator for orders. Consumers move on their own schedules, tracked in the catalog.
  • Contract step for earlier debts: usage of total has dropped to zero in v2 clients, but v1 still exposes it, because v1 is a frozen contract. The sunset of v1 is announced with a date six months out, Deprecation and Sunset headers are added, brown-out windows run two weeks before the date, and v1 and the old topic are removed once the usage dashboard reads zero.
  • Reliability pieces added: timeouts with a 1.5 second end-to-end deadline on the synchronous quote path; a retry budget at the gateway only; the outbox for event publishing; idempotent consumers with an inbox table; the multi-step order process moves to a saga (Chapter 48).

21. Interview Q&A

When would you choose asynchronous messaging over a synchronous call between two services?

I choose asynchronous when the caller does not need the result to continue, when the work can honestly be acknowledged and completed later, when many independent consumers may react to the same fact, or when the callee cannot absorb the caller's peaks and needs a buffer. Sending a receipt, updating search indexes, and awarding loyalty points are examples. I choose synchronous when the answer is needed to proceed, such as a price quote or stock check feeding a page the user is looking at. The deciding questions are: does the caller need the answer now, what should happen if the callee is down for ten minutes, and how many consumers exist. I also mention the cost of async: eventual consistency, duplicate delivery, and harder tracing.

Five services in a synchronous chain are each 99.9 percent available. What is the end-to-end availability and what can you do about it?

Availability multiplies for a serial chain, so it is about 0.999 to the fifth power, roughly 99.5 percent, which is around 3.6 hours of failure per month instead of 43 minutes. The options are to shorten the chain by moving non-essential work behind events, to give services local read models so queries do not require the owner to be up, to mark dependencies as optional and degrade gracefully, and to add redundancy plus timeouts, circuit breakers, and bulkheads so one failure does not spread. The point of the calculation is that call depth is a reliability decision, not only a latency one.

What makes a change backward compatible, and what are the rules for evolving a schema safely?

A change is backward compatible if existing consumers keep working without modification. Additive changes are safe: new optional response fields, new optional request fields with defaults, new endpoints. Removing or renaming fields, changing types or units, adding required request fields, and tightening validation are breaking. Fields must never be repurposed, and in protobuf their numbers are reserved after removal. Consumers must follow the tolerant reader pattern, ignoring unknown fields and handling unknown enum values, otherwise even additive changes break them. For event logs I also want forward compatibility because old consumers will see new events, and I enforce the rules mechanically with a schema registry and a breaking-change check in CI.

Explain consumer-driven contract testing and why it beats end-to-end tests for service boundaries.

Each consumer records, in its own tests against a mock provider, exactly which requests it makes and which parts of the response it needs, producing a pact file. The provider's pipeline replays all of its consumers' pacts against the real implementation, so a change that would break any consumer fails the provider's build before release, and a broker's can-I-deploy check blocks unsafe combinations. It beats end-to-end tests at boundaries because it runs fast in each team's pipeline, needs no shared environment, is not flaky, and tells the provider exactly which consumer depends on which field. It does not replace testing business logic or a few end-to-end smoke tests.

How do you ship a breaking change with zero downtime and without coordinating consumer deploys?

I use expand and contract. First expand: the provider adds the new field or version alongside the old, both populated, so nothing breaks. Then migrate: consumers move at their own pace while I track who still uses the old element through metrics, deprecation headers, and contact with owners. Finally contract: when usage is zero I remove the old form. For bigger changes I run a new major version in parallel behind the gateway with an adapter, and for events I dual-publish to the old and new topic. Each step is independently deployable and reversible, so no big-bang release is needed.

Why can a gRPC service get uneven load behind a standard load balancer, and how do you fix it?

gRPC runs over HTTP/2 and keeps one long-lived connection that multiplexes many calls. A layer-4 balancer distributes connections, so a client with one connection sends everything to one backend, while others stay idle and newly scaled pods get nothing. Fixes are to use a layer-7 proxy or service mesh that balances each call, to do client-side load balancing over the full list of backend addresses from a headless service or resolver, or to cap connection age so connections are recycled. I would also ensure health checks and graceful draining so deployments do not drop calls.

What is deadline propagation and why is a fixed per-hop timeout not enough?

With deadline propagation the entry point sets an absolute time by which the user-facing request must finish, and each service passes the remaining budget downstream. With only fixed per-hop timeouts, a downstream service can keep working long after the original caller gave up, because its own timeout is measured from when it received the call, and chains of such hops can exceed the user's patience many times over. Propagation lets each hop fail fast when little budget remains and cancel work when the budget is gone, saving capacity during incidents. gRPC supports it natively and over HTTP it is carried in a header.

How do you make a retried write across services safe?

A timeout means the outcome is unknown, so a retry may repeat a side effect. The caller sends a unique idempotency key with each logical operation and reuses it on every retry. The provider stores the key and result atomically with the work, and replays the stored result for duplicates, rejecting the same key with a different body. For message consumers, which usually see at-least-once delivery, I deduplicate on the message id with an inbox table or make the handler naturally idempotent. I also retry at only one layer, with backoff, jitter, and a retry budget, so retries cannot amplify an outage.

Compare choreography with orchestration for a multi-service business flow, and where do contracts matter?

In choreography services react to each other's events with no coordinator, which is loosely coupled and easy to extend but makes the overall flow implicit and hard to monitor or change. In orchestration a coordinator calls or commands each step and tracks state, giving a visible flow and clear compensation logic at the cost of a more central component. In both, event and command contracts are the interface: past-tense facts for events, explicit commands with idempotency for orchestrated steps, schemas in a registry, and compatibility rules enforced. Complex flows with compensations usually justify orchestration, covered in the sagas chapter.

How would you reduce chattiness between a mobile client and many backend services?

I would put a BFF or a GraphQL router between the client and the domain services so the client makes one request per screen and the aggregator fans out in parallel inside the data center, with timeouts and partial results for optional parts. I would add batch endpoints for lookups by many ids, design coarse-grained operations around use cases with field selection, and cache with validators. Internally I would use data loaders to avoid N+1 patterns and, if one service repeatedly needs another's reference data, replicate it through events into a local read model. If two services remain extremely chatty I would question the boundary itself.

What does good governance of internal APIs look like without creating a bottleneck?

Every API and topic has one owning team recorded in a catalog with status and contacts. A style guide is enforced by linters in CI, breaking-change detection fails any incompatible change to a published contract unless an explicit migration plan is attached, and the schema registry enforces compatibility for events. A small review group handles new cross-domain or public APIs, not every field. Usage metrics by consumer and by deprecated element feed a deprecation process with sunset dates. The principle is to automate the checks and reserve human review for design decisions.

22. Recap

  • Splitting into services turns function calls into network calls; the contract between services becomes the central engineering artefact.
  • Choose sync versus async per interaction: sync when the caller needs the answer now, async when work can be deferred, fan out to many consumers, or needs buffering.
  • Synchronous chains multiply unavailability and amplify tail latency; limit call depth, fan-out, and retries, and keep non-essential work off the critical path.
  • Write contracts first in OpenAPI, protobuf, and AsyncAPI, store event schemas in a registry with enforced compatibility, and diff contracts in CI.
  • Evolve additively: add optional fields, never repurpose or reuse, be a tolerant reader, treat enums as open, and prefer field-level evolution to new versions.
  • For real breaking changes use expand and contract, parallel versions, dual publishing, and a measured deprecation process ending in removal.
  • Consumer-driven contract tests (Pact) let providers verify real consumer expectations in their own pipeline.
  • Make errors part of the contract: stable codes, a consistent shape, correct status mapping, explicit retryability, and defined partial-failure behaviour.
  • Retries need idempotency keys, one retry layer, backoff, and budgets; timeouts need propagated deadlines.
  • gRPC needs per-call load balancing, deadlines, and size limits; BFFs, federation, and batching tame chatty clients; event contracts need identity, ordering, and reliable publication through an outbox.
