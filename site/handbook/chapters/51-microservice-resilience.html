Chapter 51 · Module 7 Microservices in Depth

Resilience Between Services

Splitting a system into services turns every function call into a network call that can be slow, lost or overloaded, and it lets one sick service make many healthy ones sick. This chapter applies the resilience tools of Chapter 40 to whole call graphs: how failures spread, how retries multiply, how to budget time across a chain, and how to design so that a non-critical dependency can never take down a critical path.

1. Why service-to-service resilience is its own problem

Chapter 40 introduced the individual tools: timeouts, retries with backoff and jitter, circuit breakers, bulkheads, hedged requests and graceful degradation. Each of those is explained there and this chapter does not repeat the mechanics. Here is a brief recap so the rest reads smoothly. A timeout bounds how long you wait. A retry repeats a failed call hoping the failure was transient. A circuit breaker stops calling a dependency that is failing so it can recover. A bulkhead limits the resources one dependency or caller may consume. A fallback returns a degraded answer instead of an error.

The new difficulty in a microservice architecture is that these tools are applied by many independent teams, in many layers, to a graph of services. Each choice is reasonable locally and dangerous globally. A team that adds "retry 3 times" to its client is being careful. Three teams in a chain, each being careful, create 27 calls to the bottom service for one user click. A team that sets a 30 second timeout "to be safe" holds a thread for 30 seconds while the user left after 3. A team that makes a health check verify its database makes all its instances leave the load balancer at once when the database blips.

So the subject of this chapter is emergent behaviour of the call graph. We will look at the mathematics of chains, then the anatomy of a cascading failure, then each defence in the context of a graph, and end with testing, contracts and a realistic incident.

Key idea In a monolith a failure is usually contained in a process. In a microservice graph a failure is a load-bearing signal: slowness propagates upstream (callers wait), and extra load propagates downstream (callers retry). Resilience between services means controlling both directions: bound how much a slow dependency can hurt you, and bound how much you can add to a struggling dependency.

2. The mathematics of call chains

2.1 Availability multiplies

Availability is the fraction of requests (or time) that succeed. If a request needs service A and B and C to all work, and their failures are independent, the availability of the whole is the product of the parts. Five services each at 99.9% (three nines, about 43 minutes of downtime per month each) give 0.9995 = 0.995, or 99.5%. That is about 3.6 hours of downtime per month, five times worse than any single component. Ten such services in series give 99.0%, roughly 7.3 hours per month.

Two lessons follow. First, depth is expensive: each extra synchronous hop on the critical path lowers the ceiling. Second, the way to raise the ceiling is not only to make each service better; it is to remove services from the critical path, by making them optional (fallbacks, Section 11), asynchronous (Section 12) or cached (Section 13).

2.2 Fan-out and the tail

Fan-out makes this worse in a second way. Suppose a page calls 30 backends in parallel and needs all of them. If each succeeds 99.9% of the time, all 30 succeed 0.99930 = 97%, so 3% of pages are broken. For latency the effect is dramatic. If each call is under 200 ms 99% of the time, a request that waits for 100 parallel calls is under 200 ms only 0.99100 = 37% of the time. The caller's median latency is therefore roughly the callee's 99th percentile. This is the "tail at scale" effect covered in Chapter 40; the implication here is architectural: keep fan-out narrow on the critical path, and make most of the fan-out optional with a deadline after which you answer with what you have.

2.3 Redundancy and its limit

Redundancy goes the other way: two independent replicas each at 99.9% give 1 - 0.0012 = 99.9999%. The catch is the word independent. Replicas of the same service share the same code (one bug kills all), the same configuration push, the same database, the same dependency and often the same availability zone. Real failures are overwhelmingly correlated, which is why the observed availability of redundant systems falls far short of the textbook figure. Chapter 41 covers redundancy across zones and regions; the point for this chapter is that calling a dependency "redundant" does not make a path to it independent of the thing that is actually failing.

3. Anatomy of a cascading failure

A cascading failure is one where a failure in one component increases the chance of failure in others, spreading until much of the system is down. Almost every large microservice outage follows the same script. Understanding the script step by step is the best protection against it, because each step is where one of the later defences cuts the chain.

3.1 Step by step

  1. A dependency becomes slow, not dead. A database fails over, a garbage collection storm hits, a noisy neighbour steals CPU, or a bad query appears. Calls that took 40 ms now take 3 seconds. Slow is more dangerous than dead: a dead dependency produces fast errors (connection refused), but a slow one produces nothing for seconds.
  2. Callers hold resources while waiting. Each in-flight call occupies a thread (or a connection, or a memory buffer, or an event-loop callback) until it finishes. By Little's law, the number of requests in flight equals arrival rate times time in system. At 200 requests per second and 50 ms per call, only 10 are in flight. When latency becomes 5 seconds, 1,000 are in flight. A pool of 200 threads is exhausted in about one second.
  3. The caller's pool is exhausted, so the caller becomes slow for everything. New requests, including ones that do not even need the slow dependency, queue for a thread. The healthy parts of the caller are now unreachable. The failure has jumped from one service to its caller.
  4. Upstream timeouts fire. The caller's own callers (and the gateway) wait, hit their timeouts and return errors. But the work in the sick service is usually not cancelled, so it keeps consuming resources for requests whose answer nobody wants.
  5. Retries multiply the load. Each timed-out caller retries (client library, mesh sidecar and gateway may each do it). The sick dependency, already struggling, now receives two, three or ten times the normal traffic. It cannot recover while it is being hammered.
  6. Health checks and autoscalers make it worse. Instances that are too busy to answer health checks are removed from the load balancer, concentrating load on the survivors. They fail too. Autoscalers add cold instances that start slowly and demand connections and caches. Orchestrators restart crashing instances, which then restart cold.
  7. Collapse. Now a large fraction of the graph is failing. Even after the original trigger is fixed, the system stays down because of retry backlog, empty caches, cold pools and thundering herds (Section 19). This is a metastable failure: a state that sustains itself without the original cause.

4. Retry storms and multi-layer amplification

Retries are the most dangerous reliability feature because they work perfectly in the case they were designed for (a rare, isolated transient failure) and catastrophically in the case that matters most (a dependency that is overloaded). When a dependency is overloaded, a retry does not recover a lost request; it adds another request to an already saturated system, and the failure probability of each attempt is high, so most retries fail too.

4.1 Where multiplication comes from

Amplification happens in two dimensions.

  • Depth: in a chain A calls B calls C calls D, if each service retries a failed call up to 3 attempts, then a failure at D makes C try D three times, and each of those is a full attempt of B's call to C, which B may also repeat three times. D sees up to 3 x 3 x 3 = 27 attempts for one request at A. In general, with n retrying hops and k attempts each, the bottom service sees up to kn requests.
  • Layers: within one hop, retries can be configured at several layers without anyone realising. The application HTTP client retries; the sidecar proxy of a service mesh retries; the API gateway or ingress retries; the cloud load balancer retries on connection errors. Three layers with 3 attempts each give 27 attempts on a single hop.

4.2 Why retries sustain an outage

Suppose a service handles 1,000 requests per second at full capacity and normally receives 700. A brief blip makes 30% of requests fail. With a single retry layer and 3 attempts, the offered load becomes 700 x (1 + 0.3 + 0.09) = 973 per second, close to the limit. Add a second retry layer and the offered load exceeds capacity, error rates rise to 50%, and amplification rises further. The system crosses a threshold and stays over it even though the trigger has passed. This is a retry storm, a classic metastable failure.

5. Retry budgets and where to retry

Two disciplines defuse retry storms: retry in only one layer, and cap retries with a budget.

5.1 Retry at one layer only

Decide, per call path, which single layer owns retries, and turn retries off everywhere else. Where should it be? A good rule: retry as close to the failing call as possible, and as far from the user as possible. Concretely, the layer immediately above the dependency call (the client library in the calling service, or the sidecar for that hop) knows exactly which call failed and whether it is safe to repeat. Retrying at the gateway means repeating the entire user request including every successful hop, which multiplies work and is rarely idempotent. Services in the middle should generally not retry at all when they were themselves called with a deadline, instead returning a clear error quickly so the top-level policy decides.

Equally important is retrying only the right errors. Connection refused, connect timeout and explicit "unavailable" responses are good candidates. A request timeout where the server may have processed the request is only safe for idempotent operations (see the idempotency-key technique in Chapter 47). A 4xx client error, a validation failure or "resource exhausted / 429" should not be retried blindly; 429 and 503 with a Retry-After header mean "slow down", and the correct response is to obey it. Never retry a failure caused by overload with the same urgency.

5.2 Retry budgets

A retry budget limits retries as a fraction of recent traffic rather than a fixed count per request. For example: "retries may be at most 10-20% of the number of first-attempt requests over the last 10 seconds." When the dependency is healthy, a few percent of requests fail transiently and all of them are retried, which is the intended benefit. When the dependency is down, 100% of requests fail, but only 10-20% extra traffic is generated, so the retry load is bounded at about 1.1 to 1.2 times normal rather than 3 times or 27 times. Envoy has a retry budget setting of this kind, Finagle and Linkerd use the same idea, and gRPC's retry throttling uses a token bucket where successes add tokens and failures remove them, so a run of failures stops retries.

Audit every layer (client library, sidecar, ingress, cloud load balancer, SDKs for managed services) and write down which one is the retry owner.

6. Timeout budgets

A timeout is a statement: "after this long, the answer is useless to me." The most common mistake in service graphs is choosing timeouts independently for each hop, producing an inverted pyramid in which the callee waits longer than its caller. Consider a gateway with a 2 second timeout calling a service with a 5 second timeout to its database. After 2 seconds the user has been told "error", but the service keeps its thread and database connection for three more seconds working on an answer that no one will read. Multiplied by thousands of requests this is how thread pools drain.

6.1 The rule: inner timeouts must be smaller than outer

Start from the outermost number, which comes from the user or the SLO (for example the page must respond within 2 seconds, or the mobile app gives up at 10). Then allocate a budget down the chain: each hop must finish with enough time left for its caller to return the answer, and the sum of sequential work plus the longest parallel branch must fit inside the parent's deadline. If service A makes two sequential calls, B (typical 80 ms, p99 300 ms) and C (typical 40 ms, p99 200 ms), A needs about 500 ms for the p99 of both plus its own work, so A's timeout to its caller must be larger than that, and the individual timeouts to B and C must be smaller than A's total.

Choose each timeout from the dependency's observed latency distribution, typically a little above its p99 or p99.9 under normal load, not from guesses like "30 seconds to be safe". A timeout that is too long hides failure and exhausts resources; one that is too short converts ordinary tail latency into errors and retries. Set separate connect timeouts (short, often 100-500 ms, since a TCP connect is either quick or failing) and request timeouts, and make sure there is always a timeout on every network call; the default in many HTTP clients is "none", which means "forever".

7. Deadline propagation across a chain

Per-hop timeouts have a flaw: they do not know how much time has already been spent. Suppose the gateway allows 2 seconds in total. A spends 1.5 seconds on its own work and then calls B with a fresh 1 second timeout. The user is gone after 0.5 more seconds, but B and everything below it still work for a full second. Deadline propagation fixes this. Instead of each hop applying a relative timeout, the request carries an absolute deadline (or the remaining time), and every hop uses what is left.

gRPC has this built in. A client sets a deadline on a call; the library sends the remaining time to the server in the grpc-timeout header; the server can read the deadline from its context; and when the server makes downstream gRPC calls using that context, the deadline is propagated automatically in Go and Java when the context is passed along (a common bug is to create a new, unrelated context or executor and so lose it). When time runs out, the call is cancelled and the cancellation propagates down so that servers can stop work. HTTP has no standard header, so companies define one (for example an X-Request-Deadline or "remaining millis" header) and enforce it in shared client libraries or the mesh.

Deadlines give three further benefits. Fail fast: if a service receives a request whose deadline has already passed, or with less time than the minimum it needs, it rejects immediately rather than wasting work. Cancel work: when the deadline expires or the client disconnects, the server aborts queries, closes connections and frees threads. Adaptive degradation: a service with a nearly exhausted budget can choose a cheaper path (skip the recommendation call, return a cached answer) instead of failing outright.

A practical note on clocks: absolute timestamps depend on synchronised clocks across hosts, which is rarely exact, so most implementations propagate remaining duration and each hop converts it to a local deadline, subtracting a small margin per hop for network and serialization time.

8. Circuit breakers in practice

The mechanics of the three breaker states (closed, open, half-open) are in Chapter 40. Applied to service graphs, the decisions that matter are the scope of each breaker, and how breakers coexist with load balancers.

8.1 Scope: per dependency, per endpoint, per instance

A breaker that wraps "all calls to the Orders service" is too coarse if only one slow endpoint (a heavy report query) is failing, because it will cut off healthy endpoints (create order). Too fine, such as one breaker per URL with query parameters, never collects enough samples to open. A reasonable default is one breaker per dependency and per logical operation class (reads vs writes, or per endpoint for important ones). Each needs a minimum number of requests in the window before it can trip, so that two failures in a quiet period do not open it.

Breakers can also be applied at two levels at once. A client-side breaker in each calling process makes a decision from that caller's own experience. A host-level outlier detector in a sidecar or load balancer judges individual instances of the callee: if one pod returns five consecutive 5xx errors, eject that pod for 30 seconds, doubling the ejection time on repeat offences, but never eject more than a maximum percentage (for example 50%) of the pool, so a systemic failure does not remove everything. The two are complementary: the outlier detector handles "one bad pod", the breaker handles "the whole dependency is bad".

8.2 What the caller does when the breaker is open

An open breaker fails instantly, which is useful only if the caller has a plan: return a fallback (Section 11), a cached value (Section 13), a queued write (Section 12) or a clear error that tells the upstream not to retry. It is a common bug to treat "breaker open" as a retryable error, which then retries into the breaker and creates log noise. Mark it as non-retryable, and expose breaker state as a metric and alert, because an open breaker is information about a dependency that the dependency's owner may not yet have.

8.3 Interaction with load balancing

Load balancing and breakers interact in three ways worth knowing. First, least-request or "power of two choices" balancing naturally steers away from slow instances because they have more outstanding requests, while plain round-robin keeps sending equal traffic to a sick pod; prefer the former for services with variable latency. Second, health checks and outlier detection overlap: active health checks catch a pod that is down, passive outlier detection catches a pod that answers health checks but fails real requests; use both. Third, when an instance is ejected the others receive its load, so capacity headroom matters: if every pod runs at 80%, ejecting one pod of five pushes the rest to 100%. This is why the maximum ejection percentage and sensible capacity planning (the N+1 or N+2 rule) are part of resilience, not just performance.

9. Bulkhead isolation in practice

A bulkhead is a boundary that keeps a failure in one compartment from sinking the whole ship. In services it means: give each dependency, tenant or priority class its own limited share of a resource, so that exhaustion of one share leaves the others intact. The resource may be threads, connection pool slots, concurrency permits, queue slots, memory, or even whole processes and clusters.

9.1 Forms of bulkhead

  • Thread-pool bulkhead: each dependency call runs on a dedicated executor with a bounded queue. Strong isolation (the caller thread is freed on timeout) but costs context switches and memory. Common in Java with Hystrix-style or Resilience4j libraries.
  • Semaphore (concurrency-limit) bulkhead: a counter of in-flight calls per dependency; the call runs on the caller's thread and is rejected if the counter is at its limit. Cheap, and the usual choice with async or event-loop runtimes (Node, Netty, Go), where the limited resource is concurrency, not threads.
  • Connection pool per dependency: separate HTTP/gRPC/database connection pools with maximum sizes, so a slow database cannot consume all outbound sockets.
  • Per-tenant isolation: in multi-tenant systems, a noisy tenant must not use more than its share. Apply per-tenant concurrency or rate limits (see Section 20), sharded queues, or even dedicated cells for the largest tenants. The same idea underlies cell-based architecture: a failure or poison request affects one cell, not the fleet.
  • Per-priority isolation: reserve capacity for high-priority traffic (checkout) that low-priority traffic (batch export, crawlers) cannot touch. Separate queues or reserved permits implement this.
  • Process or cluster isolation: the strongest bulkhead. Run the risky workload (a report generator, a plug-in runner) in its own deployment, on its own nodes, with its own database, so that even a memory leak or CPU spike cannot reach critical services.

9.2 Sizing a bulkhead

Use Little's law again: permits needed = peak request rate x p99 latency of the dependency (plus a little headroom). If Payments takes 120 ms at p99 and the peak is 300 requests per second, then 300 x 0.12 = 36 concurrent calls, so a limit of about 50 is generous; a limit of 500 provides no protection. Limits that are too tight reject legitimate load, so base them on measurements and review them after traffic growth. Adaptive concurrency limits (as in Netflix's concurrency-limits library and Envoy's adaptive concurrency filter) tune the limit automatically from observed latency, much like TCP congestion control.

Interview tip If asked "how do you stop one slow downstream from taking everything down?" answer with the sequence: a timeout so waits are bounded, a bulkhead so the waits cannot use more than their share, a breaker so you stop calling, and a fallback so users still get something. Naming all four and in that order shows you know they are layers, not alternatives.

10. Isolating critical from non-critical paths

Not every feature of a page is equally valuable. A product page without recommendations is still a product page; a checkout without payments is not a checkout. A clear separation of critical path (the minimum for the core user journey) and non-critical path (enhancements) is the most powerful structural resilience decision, because it removes whole services from the availability product of Section 2.

The separation can be enforced at several levels. In code, the non-critical calls are made in parallel with short timeouts and their failure is swallowed (the section of the page is simply omitted). In the request, a critical request and a low-priority request carry different criticality labels (Section 14). In deployment, critical services run in dedicated clusters, with their own databases and their own deployment pipeline, and are not allowed to depend on anything less critical than themselves. In the dependency graph, the rule "a service may only synchronously depend on services at the same or higher criticality tier" prevents a tier-1 service from being held hostage by a tier-3 one.

11. Dependency tiering and fallbacks

Classify each dependency of each service by what happens to the user if it is unavailable, then decide what the caller does. This is a design document you write once per service and review whenever the dependency list changes.

12. Breaking coupling: sync versus async

Every synchronous call is a coupling in time: the caller cannot finish until the callee does, so their availabilities multiply. Replacing a synchronous call with a message through a durable broker (see Chapter 47) breaks that coupling: the caller records its intent and moves on, and the consumer processes it when it can. If the consumer is down, messages accumulate in the queue and are processed after it returns, which converts an outage of the consumer into delay rather than failure.

That trade is excellent for work that does not need an immediate answer: sending email, updating a search index, awarding loyalty points, generating a report, propagating a change to read models. It is wrong for work where the user needs the answer now (is the card valid?). A useful hybrid is to do the minimum synchronously (reserve inventory, authorize payment) and publish an event for everything else. The transactional outbox pattern (write the event to a table in the same database transaction as the state change, and have a relay publish it) ensures the event is not lost if the broker is briefly unavailable.

Async is not free resilience. The queue is itself a dependency that can fill up, and consumers can fall behind; a poisonous message that always fails can block a partition unless there is a dead-letter policy; and handlers must be idempotent because delivery is at least once. Monitor queue depth and age of oldest message as first-class signals, and apply backpressure when the queue grows (Section 15).

13. Caching as resilience: stale-if-error

Caches are usually introduced for speed, but they are also a resilience layer, because a cached answer is available even when the source is not. Two concepts make this explicit. Stale-while-revalidate serves a slightly expired entry immediately while refreshing it in the background, so users never wait on a refresh. Stale-if-error serves an expired entry when the refresh fails, so a dependency outage produces old data instead of an error. Both are standard HTTP cache directives (Cache-Control: max-age=60, stale-while-revalidate=30, stale-if-error=86400) and are easy to implement in in-process caches too: keep the expired value, try the refresh with a short timeout, and on failure return the old value and mark the response as stale.

Choose which data may be stale: product descriptions, feature flags, configuration, exchange rates, permissions (with care), recommendations all tolerate minutes or hours of staleness; account balances and inventory counts generally do not. Where staleness is dangerous, keep a last known good copy and clearly flag it to the user, or refuse the operation. Also protect the dependency from the cache itself: when a hot entry expires, many callers refresh at once (a cache stampede). Request coalescing (single flight), jittered expiry and background refresh avoid that (Chapter 13 covers these patterns).

A good pattern For configuration and reference data, have each service keep a local copy refreshed periodically and persisted to disk. At boot, if the config service is unreachable, the service starts from the last saved copy. The config service then stops being a startup dependency of the fleet (see Section 17).

14. Load shedding and priority headers

When demand exceeds capacity, a service has two choices: try to serve everyone (and serve everyone badly, with queues growing and latency exploding) or shed load, deliberately rejecting some requests so the rest are served well. Load shedding is covered in Chapter 16; here the question is how it works across services.

The rule of thumb: reject early and cheaply. A request rejected at the front door costs microseconds. A request that is accepted, queued for 10 seconds, partially processed and then timed out costs a full request's worth of work and still fails. Good shedding signals include the number of in-flight requests above a limit, queue length or queue wait time (reject requests that have already waited longer than their useful lifetime), CPU above a threshold, and the remaining deadline of the incoming request (reject if less time remains than the typical processing time). The response should be fast and explicit: HTTP 503 or 429 with a Retry-After value, or gRPC RESOURCE_EXHAUSTED or UNAVAILABLE.

14.1 Criticality and priority

Shedding should discard the least valuable work first. To do that, services need to know which requests are valuable, and that information must travel with the request, because the receiving service cannot guess. Systems such as Google's describe request criticality levels (for example critical-plus, critical, sheddable-plus, sheddable). A calling service sets the label from the user's journey and propagates it to every downstream call, and each service sheds the lowest class first as its load rises. In HTTP systems this is typically a header such as X-Request-Priority: critical | normal | batch, enforced by the mesh or a shared library, and honoured in queues (separate queues or weighted fair queueing) and in bulkhead permits (Section 9).

Watch out Priority labels are only trustworthy if they are validated. If any client may claim "critical", every client will. Assign criticality at the edge (gateway) based on the authenticated journey, and strip or cap labels coming from untrusted callers. Also make sure that shed responses do not trigger retries: clients must treat 429/503 with Retry-After as a signal to back off.

15. Backpressure across services

Backpressure means a slow consumer signals a fast producer to slow down, so queues do not grow without bound. Without it, each stage in a pipeline buffers work until memory runs out. Unbounded queues are a hidden form of latency: a queue of 10,000 items with 10 ms service time means the last item waits 100 seconds, long after its caller gave up. Prefer bounded queues with fast rejection to deep buffers.

Backpressure mechanisms differ by protocol. TCP has window-based flow control; HTTP/2 and gRPC have per-stream flow-control windows; Reactive Streams and Kafka consumers use pull ("request n items") so the consumer decides the rate; message brokers expose queue depth that producers can read and respond to by throttling. At the service level, the simplest and most robust form is rejecting with a retry hint at the boundary (Section 14) combined with clients that obey it, and a concurrency limit on outgoing calls so a service cannot flood its dependency. The signals must propagate all the way to the user-facing edge, which may then show "try again shortly" or switch to a degraded experience, rather than silently piling work onto the system.

16. Avoiding cycles and fan-out amplification

16.1 Circular dependencies

If service A calls B, B calls C and C calls A, any slowness in the loop is self-reinforcing: threads in A wait for B, which waits for C, which waits for A, which has no free threads, so the cycle deadlocks across the network. Even without a strict cycle, a lower-tier service calling a higher-tier one creates the same effect. Avoid cycles by design: keep the dependency graph a directed acyclic graph with layers (edge, aggregation, domain, platform). Break unavoidable cycles with events (C publishes an event that A consumes asynchronously) or by moving shared logic into a library or a lower-level service. Enforce with tooling: draw the graph from tracing data (Chapter 52) and fail a review when a new edge points the wrong way or closes a cycle.

16.2 Fan-out amplification

One incoming request often triggers many outgoing ones: the gateway calls an aggregator, which calls ten services, each of which calls a few more. A request that fans out to 10 and then 10 again produces 100 downstream calls; a 10% traffic increase at the edge produces a proportional but large absolute increase at the leaves, and retries on top multiply it. Defences: cap fan-out per request; use batch endpoints (get 50 items in one call, not 50 calls); cache shared results; apply a deadline after which the aggregator returns partial results; and measure the amplification factor (total internal calls divided by external requests) per endpoint, treating a rising factor like a rising cost. Also beware the N+1 call pattern, where a loop calls a remote service once per item.

17. Startup order and dependencies down at boot

A fleet that can only start when all its dependencies are already running has a startup ordering problem, and after a full-region restart or a power event the order matters: if A needs B and B needs A's cache, nothing starts. Healthy design makes startup order-independent. Services should start, listen and report not-ready until they can safely serve, retrying their dependencies with backoff in the background, instead of crashing if a dependency is missing (crash loops add load through restarts, as in Section 3).

Concretely: separate liveness (am I running, should I be restarted?) from readiness (can I take traffic now?); never make liveness depend on remote dependencies. Initialise optional dependencies lazily, and for required configuration use the persisted last-known-good copy (Section 13). When a tier-1 dependency is missing at boot, remain not-ready rather than serving errors. In Kubernetes use a startup probe for slow initialisation so the liveness probe does not kill a service that is still warming up. And rehearse a cold start of the whole platform at least once, because that is where hidden circular boot dependencies (the secrets store needs the network, which needs the DNS service, which needs secrets) appear.

18. Health checks that do not cascade

A shallow health check reports whether the process itself is up and able to respond (event loop alive, threads available, no fatal internal state). A deep health check also tests dependencies: it queries the database, pings downstream services, checks the cache. Deep checks seem more thorough but are dangerous when used by a load balancer or orchestrator to decide routing and restarts.

Guidelines: use shallow checks for liveness and for routing decisions; report dependency state as metrics and a separate diagnostic endpoint for humans and dashboards; if a deep readiness check is truly needed (for example "I cannot serve without my database"), apply it only to tier-1 dependencies, cache the result for several seconds so a check is not a load source, and use a "panic threshold" so the load balancer never ejects more than a fraction of the fleet (Envoy and many cloud load balancers have a "fail open when all are unhealthy" behaviour, which is better than serving nothing). Finally, make checks cheap: an expensive health check runs often, from many places, and becomes part of the load.

19. Thundering herd on recovery and warm-up

When a failed dependency returns, the demand that was blocked arrives all at once: clients retry, queued work flushes, caches are empty so every request misses, connections are re-established simultaneously, and autoscalers or restarts bring up cold instances with empty caches and unjitted JIT compilers. The recovering service can be knocked over again immediately, producing a sawtooth of recover-collapse-recover. This is the metastable behaviour introduced in Section 3.

Mitigations: jittered backoff for client retries and reconnects, so the return of traffic is spread out; slow-start in the load balancer (a new instance receives a linearly rising share of traffic over, say, 30-60 seconds); cache warming before taking traffic (pre-load hot keys, run a readiness probe that exercises the main code path); admission control during recovery (rate limit or shed at the dependency until it stabilises, then raise the limit); staggered restarts and deployments (roll a few instances at a time, not the fleet); and a deliberate recovery runbook that includes "reduce incoming traffic first" (drain a region, enable a maintenance banner, disable retries) before restoring the dependency. Sometimes the correct action in a metastable failure is to cut the load to zero, let the system settle, and bring traffic back in steps.

20. Rate limiting between services

Rate limiting (see Chapter 16 for algorithms and distributed implementation) protects a service from its own callers. Internally, three kinds are useful. Per-caller quotas give each calling service an agreed rate against a shared dependency, so a bug or a batch job in one team's service cannot exhaust another's capacity; the quota is part of the contract (Section 23). Per-tenant limits prevent one customer in a multi-tenant system from affecting others. Concurrency limits bound in-flight requests, which is often a better signal than request rate because it directly reflects capacity, whatever the request cost.

Do the limiting at the callee (it is the one that knows its capacity), preferably with a local limiter in each instance plus a coarser global limit from a shared service, and have well-behaved clients that also self-limit on outbound calls, so rejected requests do not fill the network. Return clear signals (429 with Retry-After, rate-limit headers) and make sure client libraries handle them by backing off, not retrying immediately. Differentiate "you are over your quota" (the caller must slow down) from "I am overloaded" (everyone must slow down), because the right reactions differ.

21. Mesh-level resilience and its pitfalls

A service mesh (Chapter 50) moves timeouts, retries, circuit breaking, outlier detection, load balancing and mTLS into sidecar proxies configured centrally. The advantages are real: consistent behaviour across languages, policy changes without redeploying, uniform metrics. But the mesh also introduces ways to reduce resilience if used naively.

  • Duplicate retries. The sidecar and the application both retry (Section 4). Decide the owner; set the other to zero. If the mesh owns retries, configure them per route with retry-on conditions (connect failure, 503 on idempotent methods) and budgets, not blanket "retry 5xx 3 times".
  • Timeout mismatch. The mesh's route timeout (Envoy default 15 seconds) may be longer than the application's, or shorter than the application's needs, and per-try timeouts versus overall timeouts are different settings. Keep a single, documented budget from the edge to the leaf.
  • Hidden latency and failure modes. Two extra proxies per call add a small latency each, consume CPU and memory per pod, and are new components that can fail, run out of connections, or be misconfigured by a bad control-plane push. A bad config rolled out to the whole mesh at once is a fleet-wide outage; roll out mesh policy gradually and test it like code.
  • Control-plane dependency. Data-plane proxies should continue with their last known configuration if the control plane is unreachable. Verify this is true for your mesh, and that sidecars do not block pod startup on control-plane availability in a way that makes boot fragile.
  • Circuit-breaker semantics. Mesh "circuit breakers" are often connection and request caps (maximum pending requests) that behave like bulkheads, plus outlier detection, rather than the error-rate breaker of an application library. Know which one you are getting.
  • False sense of safety. The mesh cannot know if a request is idempotent, what a good fallback is, or which dependencies are optional. Those decisions remain in the application.
  • Connection-level load balancing mismatch. With HTTP/2 or gRPC, long-lived connections pinned to one backend defeat connection-level balancing; make sure the mesh balances per request.

22. Resilience testing

Resilience that has not been exercised is a hypothesis. The systems that stay up are the ones that fail on purpose, in controlled ways, regularly.

  • Fault injection in tests. Use a proxy or the mesh to inject latency, errors, connection resets and slow responses (the mesh's own fault-injection filters, Toxiproxy, or libraries that wrap clients) in integration and staging tests, and assert that fallbacks, timeouts and breakers behave as designed: that the page renders without recommendations, that checkout fails fast, that thread pools do not saturate.
  • Chaos experiments. Following the practice popularised by Netflix's Chaos Monkey and the "principles of chaos engineering": define a steady-state metric (orders per second, p99 latency), form a hypothesis ("if one zone's pods are killed, steady state holds"), inject the failure in a limited blast radius (small percent of traffic, one cell), observe, and stop if the abort criteria trip. Automate common experiments (kill an instance, add 500 ms latency to a dependency, drop 10% of packets, expire a certificate).
  • Game days. Scheduled, announced exercises where teams simulate a major failure (region loss, database failover, a dependency returning errors) in production or a production-like environment, with the on-call team following the real runbooks. Game days test people, tools and documentation, not only code.
  • Dependency failure drills. For each service, regularly take each dependency away in turn (block its network route, return 503, return slow responses) and verify the documented behaviour for its tier. The output is a table: dependency, failure mode, observed behaviour, expected behaviour, gap. These drills also catch "hidden" hard dependencies that nobody listed.
  • Load and overload tests. Test beyond expected peak, to find the point at which the service sheds correctly instead of collapsing, and check that latency stays flat under shedding. Test recovery as well: remove the overload and verify the system returns to normal without intervention.
  • Deployment and config safety. Many outages are self-inflicted; canary deployments, automated rollback and config validation are part of resilience, and the failure of a bad deploy should be limited to a cell or a small percentage of traffic.
Interview tip If asked "how do you know your resilience works?", describe a closed loop: steady-state metrics, a hypothesis, controlled fault injection with small blast radius, automated abort, and a fix-and-retest cycle. Mention that fallbacks need drills because untested fallbacks are the ones that fail.

23. SLO-based dependency contracts

Resilience between teams needs agreement, not just technology. An SLO (service level objective) is a target for a measurable indicator, such as "99.9% of requests succeed within 300 ms over 30 days". Teams that depend on each other should write down contracts in these terms (the observability side is in Chapter 52):

  • Callee publishes its SLOs for availability and latency per endpoint, and its capacity limits and per-caller quota.
  • Caller states its needs: expected request rate, peak, criticality tier and how it behaves on failure (fallback, retry policy).
  • Availability arithmetic is checked at design time: a service promising 99.95% cannot have three hard dependencies at 99.9% (their product is 99.7%). Either improve the dependencies, make them soft, or lower the promise.
  • Error budgets (the allowed unreliability implied by the SLO) are used to decide: when the callee's budget is spent, it slows feature work in favour of reliability; callers may need to switch to fallbacks or tighten timeouts.
  • Client behaviour is part of the contract: the callee requires clients to set timeouts and deadlines, to retry only at the agreed layer, to respect 429/503 with Retry-After, and to send a criticality label.
  • Deprecation and change: breaking changes and increases in latency are announced, because a callee that gets 20% slower can cause its callers' pools to exhaust even without any error.

24. Case walkthrough: a realistic incident

The following is a composite, written the way a real postmortem would be, to show how the earlier sections combine. The company runs an online retailer with a gateway, a Product service (renders product pages), a Recommendations service, a Cart/Checkout service, Payments and Inventory, and a shared Redis cache and a PostgreSQL cluster for Recommendations features.

24.1 Setup and numbers

  • Traffic: 2,000 requests per second at peak to the Product page. Each page view calls Recommendations synchronously. Normal latency of Recommendations: p50 40 ms, p99 150 ms.
  • The Product service has one shared HTTP client pool of 200 worker threads for all outbound calls. Its client timeout to Recommendations is 10 seconds ("to be safe"), with 3 attempts. The service mesh sidecar adds a further 3 attempts on 5xx. The gateway retries a timed-out request once.
  • Checkout shares the same gateway and the same Product-service worker pool for a "price and stock check" call that it makes into Product.
  • Health checks of the Product service call Recommendations and the database ("deep" check). No criticality labels exist. There is no load shedding.

24.3 What the postmortem concluded

A good postmortem is blameless and focuses on systemic causes. The immediate trigger (a cold replica) was ordinary and will happen again. The contributing causes were all in the call graph:

  1. An optional dependency was on the critical path with a very long timeout. Recommendations is tier 3, but a 10 second timeout and 3 attempts made it behave like tier 1.
  2. One shared pool. There was no bulkhead, so a slow tier-3 call starved tier-1 work including Checkout.
  3. Retries at three layers, with no budget. The load on the already slow database multiplied, delaying recovery by perhaps 20 minutes.
  4. Deep health checks. They removed pods precisely when more capacity was needed.
  5. No shedding, no priorities. Everything was equally important, so everything failed together.
  6. Recovery was not rehearsed. The first reaction (restart) was reasonable but made things worse, because nobody had written the rule "reduce load first".

25. Putting it together: a design checklist

The checklist below is the one to run through in a design review or in an interview when asked "how would you make this call graph resilient?". Each line is a question that exposes a missing decision.

25.1 For every outbound call

  • Is there a timeout, derived from the callee's p99, and is it smaller than the caller's own deadline?
  • Does the call carry a propagated deadline, and does the callee abandon work after it passes?
  • Who owns retries? Exactly one layer; capped attempts; budget; backoff with jitter; only for idempotent or safe failures.
  • Is there a concurrency limit (bulkhead) for this dependency, sized from rate x latency?
  • Is there a circuit breaker and what does the caller do when it is open?
  • What is the tier of the dependency and the fallback? Is the fallback tested and cheap?

25.2 For every service

  • Does it shed load cheaply and by priority before queues grow? Are queues bounded?
  • Does it expose shallow health for routing and keep dependency health in metrics?
  • Can it start with dependencies down and warm up gradually (slow-start, cache warm-up)?
  • Does it have per-caller and per-tenant quotas?
  • Is its place in the dependency graph acyclic and tier-respecting? What is its fan-out and amplification factor?
  • Can critical and non-critical work be separated in resources (pools, queues, deployments)?

25.3 For the platform and the organisation

  • Mesh and gateway policies reviewed so they do not duplicate application retries or timeouts.
  • SLOs and dependency contracts written, with error budgets and escalation.
  • Fault-injection tests in CI; regular chaos experiments, dependency drills and game days.
  • Runbooks for cascading failures: reduce load first, disable optional features by flag, ramp traffic back in steps.
  • Dashboards for retry ratio, in-flight requests, bulkhead rejections, breaker state, shed counts, queue age (see Chapter 52).

26. Interview questions and answers

What is a cascading failure and how do you stop one in a microservice architecture?

A cascading failure is one in which a problem in one component increases the load or failure probability of others until much of the system is down. The usual mechanism is a slow dependency that holds callers' threads, which exhausts their pools, which causes timeouts upstream, which causes retries that push more load onto the original dependency. You stop it by cutting the chain at several points: short timeouts so callers do not wait long, bulkheads so a slow dependency can use only a bounded share of resources, circuit breakers so you stop calling a failing dependency, retries owned by one layer with a budget, load shedding so overloaded services reject cheaply, and fallbacks so non-critical failures do not reach users. No single tool is enough, because each addresses one step of the cascade.

Why can retries make an outage worse, and what is a retry budget?

When a dependency is overloaded, a retry adds one more request to a saturated system and is likely to fail as well, so retries turn a partial failure into a larger one. The effect multiplies: with 3 attempts at each of 3 layers or hops, the bottom service can receive 27 requests for each original request. A retry budget limits retries to a fraction of the traffic, for example 10 to 20 percent of recent first attempts, so when the dependency is healthy nearly all transient failures are retried, and when it is down the extra load is capped at around 1.1 to 1.2 times normal. Combined with retrying at only one layer, exponential backoff, jitter and obeying Retry-After, this keeps retries beneficial instead of destructive.

How do you choose timeouts across a chain of services?

Work from the outside in. The outermost timeout comes from the user experience or the SLO, and each hop gets a smaller budget so that the caller always has time to receive an answer and respond. The individual values come from the callee's observed latency (a bit above p99 under normal load), not round numbers like 30 seconds, and every call also needs a short connect timeout. Better still, propagate a deadline: pass the remaining time downstream, as gRPC does with grpc-timeout, so each hop uses what is left, can skip calls that cannot finish in time, and can cancel work when the deadline passes. Inverted timeouts, where the callee waits longer than the caller, are a common cause of wasted work and thread exhaustion.

How should circuit breakers interact with the load balancer's health checking and outlier detection?

They work at different levels and complement each other. Outlier detection (in a sidecar or load balancer) watches individual instances and ejects one that returns consecutive errors for a period, with a cap on the percentage ejected so a systemic failure does not remove the whole pool. A client circuit breaker watches the aggregate behaviour of the dependency and stops calls when it is failing everywhere, giving it time to recover and letting the caller use a fallback. Active health checks find instances that are down, passive detection finds ones that look alive but fail real requests. You must also keep capacity headroom, because ejecting an instance pushes its load onto the rest, and prefer least-request or power-of-two-choices balancing, which steers away from slow instances naturally.

What is a bulkhead and how do you size one?

A bulkhead isolates resources so that a failure or overload in one part cannot consume everything. In practice it is a dedicated thread pool, semaphore or connection pool per dependency, per tenant or per priority class, with a hard limit and fast rejection when full. To size it, use Little's law: concurrent calls needed is peak request rate times the p99 latency of the dependency, plus some headroom. For example 300 requests per second at 120 ms needs about 36 concurrent calls, so a limit near 50 protects well, whereas a limit of 500 protects nothing. Adaptive concurrency limits can tune the number from observed latency, and the bulkhead rejections should be a monitored metric.

How do you decide which dependencies get fallbacks, and what makes a good fallback?

Classify each dependency by what the user loses when it is gone. Tier 1 dependencies, such as payment authorization, cannot be replaced, so the right behaviour is to fail fast and clearly. Tier 2 dependencies, such as a shipping quote, can use a conservative substitute like a rate table or last known value. Tier 3 dependencies, such as recommendations, are simply omitted. A good fallback is local (not another remote call), cheap, safe (never returns wrong data that causes harm, like claiming stock is available when it is unknown), observable (counts every use) and regularly tested, because an untested fallback often fails the first time it is needed, during an incident.

Why are deep health checks dangerous, and what do you use instead?

A deep health check that tests dependencies makes every instance depend on the same shared systems. If the database blips, all instances fail their checks at the same moment and the load balancer removes the whole fleet, turning a partial problem into a total outage, and then they all return at once and cause a herd. Use shallow checks, which test only that the process is able to serve, for liveness and for routing. Export dependency health as metrics and a diagnostic endpoint, and rely on breakers and fallbacks to handle failing dependencies. If a readiness check must include a dependency, restrict it to tier 1, cache the result and configure the load balancer to fail open or limit how much of the fleet can be removed.

How do load shedding and priorities work between services?

A service that is past capacity should reject some requests quickly rather than accept all and serve them slowly, because a rejected request costs microseconds and a queued, timed-out request wastes a whole request of work. The service decides using signals like in-flight requests, queue wait, CPU or the remaining deadline. To shed the least valuable work first, each request carries a criticality or priority label assigned at the edge from the user's journey and propagated through every hop, so batch traffic is dropped first, optional features next, and critical traffic last. The rejection uses 503 or 429 with Retry-After, clients must back off instead of retrying immediately, and labels must be validated so that no caller can promote itself to critical.

How do you prevent a thundering herd when a dependency recovers?

The herd forms because all blocked clients, queued work and cold instances hit the service at the same time, and its caches and pools are empty. Spread the demand out: jittered backoff on retries and reconnects, slow-start on the load balancer so a new instance gets a rising share of traffic, cache warming and readiness probes that exercise real paths, admission control or rate limiting at the recovering service which is relaxed gradually, and staggered restarts. Operationally, when a system is in a metastable state, the first move is often to reduce load (disable optional features, show a maintenance banner, drain traffic) and then bring traffic back in steps, rather than just restarting everything.

How would you test that a service is resilient to the failure of one of its dependencies?

I would test it at several levels. In CI, use fault injection (latency, errors, resets) against each dependency and assert the documented behaviour: fallback returned, bulkhead rejecting, breaker opening, timeouts honoured, thread pool not exhausted. In staging or production with limited blast radius, run chaos experiments with a steady-state metric and abort conditions, and schedule dependency failure drills and game days so people practise the runbooks. I would also run overload tests to confirm that shedding keeps latency flat and the system recovers by itself when overload is removed. Every drill should produce a table of expected versus observed behaviour, and gaps become tickets.

Should resilience logic live in the application or in the service mesh?

Both have a place, and the key is to avoid duplication. The mesh is good for uniform, language-independent mechanics: connection limits, outlier detection, per-route timeouts, mTLS, metrics, and sometimes retries with budgets. The application knows semantics the mesh cannot: whether an operation is idempotent, what a safe fallback is, which dependencies are optional, how to degrade a page, and what the deadline budget is. A workable split is that the mesh provides timeouts, outlier detection and connection caps by default, one layer (usually the application client or mesh route, but not both) owns retries, and the application owns fallbacks, tiers, bulkheads by dependency and criticality labelling. Changes to mesh policy should be rolled out gradually because a bad policy affects every service at once.

How do you reason about the availability of a service that depends on many others?

For hard dependencies in series, multiply availabilities, assuming independence: five dependencies at 99.9 percent give about 99.5 percent. Fan-out that needs every branch is worse, and for latency the tail of the combined call approaches the tail of the slowest branch. To improve the number, shorten the critical path, convert hard dependencies into soft ones with fallbacks, replace synchronous calls with asynchronous messaging where users do not need an immediate answer, and cache data with stale-if-error. Keep in mind that redundancy only helps against independent failures, and many real failures (a bad deployment, a shared database, a regional outage) are correlated, so the product is an optimistic upper bound and the SLO you promise should be lower than the arithmetic suggests.

27. Recap

  • Hard dependencies in series multiply availability: five at 99.9 percent give 99.5 percent. Shorten the critical path and turn dependencies into soft ones.
  • Cascading failures follow a script: slow dependency, held threads, exhausted pools, upstream timeouts, retries, health-check ejection, metastable collapse. Each defence cuts one step.
  • Retries multiply across hops and layers (attempts to the power of layers). Retry in one layer, with a budget, backoff and jitter, and only for safe failures.
  • Timeouts must shrink inward, based on measured p99. Propagate deadlines (gRPC grpc-timeout) so each hop uses the remaining time and abandons work that no one is awaiting.
  • Use breakers per dependency or operation together with host-level outlier detection and leave capacity headroom for ejection.
  • Bulkheads (per dependency, tenant, priority, or whole process) cap the damage from one slow part. Size with rate times p99 latency.
  • Tier dependencies, give each a tested, cheap, local fallback, and keep optional ones off the critical path. Use async messaging and stale-if-error caches to break coupling in time.
  • Shed load early and by priority using labels set at the edge; apply backpressure with bounded queues; avoid cycles and cap fan-out.
  • Services should start in any order, use shallow health checks, warm up gradually and recover without herds.
  • The mesh offers uniform mechanics but can duplicate retries and mismatch timeouts. Roll its policy out carefully.
  • Test with fault injection, chaos, game days and dependency drills; formalise expectations in SLO-based contracts with quotas and client obligations.
