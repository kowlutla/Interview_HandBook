Chapter 41 · Module 6 · Reliability and operations

High Availability and Disaster Recovery

Hardware fails, networks partition, data centers lose power, and people push bad changes. This chapter explains how to keep a system running through the small failures (high availability), how to recover from the large ones (disaster recovery), and how to turn a target such as "99.99%" into concrete design decisions and numbers.

1. Two related ideas: HA and DR

Beginners often use "high availability" and "disaster recovery" as if they meant the same thing. They are related but answer different questions.

  • High availability (HA) is about continuing to serve when a component fails. A server dies, a disk breaks, an availability zone loses power, and users barely notice because a redundant copy takes over automatically within seconds. HA deals with failures you expect to happen regularly. The goal is to keep downtime close to zero.
  • Disaster recovery (DR) is about getting back after a failure so large that normal redundancy was not enough: a whole region is lost, a bug or an attacker destroys data, an operator deletes the production database. Here you accept that there will be an outage and some risk of data loss, and the question becomes how long recovery takes and how much data you lose.

A useful way to remember it: HA is a seat belt, DR is the insurance policy. A system can be highly available and still have no real disaster recovery. For example, a database replicated across three zones survives any single zone failure, but a wrong DELETE statement is replicated to all three copies within milliseconds. Redundancy protects against infrastructure loss; it does not protect against data corruption. Section 13 returns to this distinction, because it is one of the most common gaps in real designs.

Key idea HA keeps you running through expected, frequent, small failures by having redundancy that takes over automatically. DR restores service after rare, large failures by having a separate copy of your data and infrastructure that you can bring up elsewhere. You need both, they cost different amounts, and you choose how much of each using business requirements (availability target, RPO, RTO), not instinct.

This chapter builds on Chapter 40, which covered the code-level patterns (timeouts, retries, circuit breakers, bulkheads) that stop one failing dependency from taking down its callers. Here we zoom out to the infrastructure level: copies of servers, data, and whole sites. The two chapters work together, because redundancy that your clients cannot use correctly (no timeouts, no retries to the other copy) does not help.

2. Availability math: nines, series, parallel

2.1 What "availability" means and the nines

Availability is the fraction of time (or of requests) in which a system works correctly. It is usually written as a percentage and described by the number of nines. The calculation for time-based availability is: availability = uptime ÷ (uptime + downtime). The table shows what each level allows. We use an average month of about 30.4 days (43,800 minutes) and a year of 365 days.

Two lessons come from this table. First, each extra nine cuts the allowed downtime by a factor of ten and typically multiplies the cost and complexity by much more. Second, at four nines the whole budget for a month is about four minutes. A human who gets paged, logs in, reads a runbook, and makes a decision takes longer than that, so four nines forces automatic failover, automated rollbacks and safe deployments. Five nines in practice means a design with no human in the recovery loop for common failures and, usually, multiple independent regions.

Many large services measure availability by requests rather than by time: successful requests ÷ total valid requests. Request-based measurement is fairer when traffic is uneven (an outage at 3 a.m. hurts less than one at noon) and it counts partial failures. Chapter 54 explains how to define the SLI (the measurement), the SLO (the target) and the error budget (the allowed failure). The link to this chapter is direct: the error budget is the downtime allowance in the table, and every design decision here spends or protects it. If your monthly budget is 4.38 minutes and a single database failover takes 90 seconds, one failover consumes about a third of the budget.

2.2 Components in series multiply

If a request must pass through several components and all of them must work, they are in series. The system is up only when every component is up, so availabilities multiply. A request that needs a load balancer (99.99%), an application tier (99.95%), a database (99.95%), and an authentication service (99.9%) has availability 0.9999 × 0.9995 × 0.9995 × 0.999 = about 0.9979, or 99.79%. That is roughly 15 hours of downtime a year, worse than every single component. The chain is weaker than its weakest link, because every link contributes its own failures.

The practical consequence is a rule of thumb: every hard dependency subtracts from your availability. If you promise 99.99% and you depend synchronously on a service that only offers 99.9%, you cannot meet the promise by clever coding alone; either you make that dependency optional (degrade without it, see sections 5 and 15) or you add redundancy to it.

2.3 Components in parallel (redundancy) improve availability

If a function is served by several copies and any one of them is enough, they are in parallel. The service is down only when all copies are down at once. With independent failures, the unavailability (1 minus availability) of the group is the product of the unavailabilities. Two servers, each 99% available, have a combined unavailability of 0.01 × 0.01 = 0.0001, so 99.99%. Three servers give 99.9999%. This is why redundancy is the basic tool of HA.

2.4 The independence assumption, and what really limits you

The parallel formula is dangerously optimistic. It assumes copies fail independently. In reality they share things: the same software version (a bug crashes all copies at once), the same deployment pipeline (one bad config push reaches every copy), the same network, the same power feed, the same DNS provider, the same human operators. These are correlated failures, and they dominate real outages. Three servers in the same rack are not three independent 99% machines, because a single top-of-rack switch failure removes all three.

This leads to two rules. (1) The redundancy mechanism itself is in the series chain: the load balancer, the health checker, the failover controller. If the failover logic is only 99.9% reliable, it caps the benefit. (2) After a few nines, availability is limited not by hardware failure rates but by changes: deployments, configuration pushes, schema migrations. Industry post-incident reviews show most large outages follow a change. So the most valuable HA techniques at four nines and beyond are staged rollouts, automatic rollback, and limiting blast radius (Chapter 42), not just more servers.

2.5 Composite availability and SLAs

When you run on cloud services you inherit their availability. If your product depends in series on a cloud database with a 99.95% SLA, an object store at 99.9%, and a queue at 99.9%, the best you can honestly promise from those alone is roughly 0.9995 × 0.999 × 0.999 = 99.7%, before counting your own code. This is the composite availability calculation, and it is what a good engineer does before agreeing to a customer SLA.

Three terms are often confused. A Service Level Indicator (SLI) is a measurement (fraction of successful requests). A Service Level Objective (SLO) is your internal target for it (99.95%). A Service Level Agreement (SLA) is a contract with customers that specifies remedies, usually service credits, if you fall below a threshold. The SLA is set looser than the SLO, so you have room to miss your internal target without paying. If the SLA says 99.9%, a sensible internal SLO might be 99.95%. Note too that SLA credits are a financial remedy; they do not repay the customer's lost business, which is why careful customers read the exclusions (scheduled maintenance, force majeure, "excluding customer-caused issues") as carefully as the headline number.

Watch out Do not state "we use three servers, so we have six nines." State what fails together. In an interview, saying "the formula assumes independence; our biggest correlated risks are a bad deploy and a shared dependency, so I limit those with staged rollout and cells" shows mature thinking.

3. Eliminating single points of failure, layer by layer

A single point of failure (SPOF) is any component whose failure alone stops the whole system. HA design is largely a hunt for SPOFs: look at the request path from the user to the data and ask, at each hop, "if this one thing disappears, what happens?" If the answer is "everything stops", add redundancy or remove the dependency. Here is the walk from the outside in.

3.1 DNS

If you host your domain with one provider and that provider has an outage, users cannot find you even though every server is healthy. This has caused major real-world outages. Mitigations: use two independent DNS providers serving the same zone, keep records for failover at a low TTL (time to live, how long resolvers may cache an answer; 30-60 seconds is common for records you intend to switch), and avoid putting the failover control only inside the system that might be down. Remember that many resolvers and clients ignore low TTLs to some degree, so DNS failover is never instant (section 14).

3.2 Load balancer

The load balancer (Chapter 9) fronts everything, so it must itself be redundant. Cloud managed load balancers are already spread across zones and handle this for you. For self-run load balancers, the classic pattern is an active-passive pair sharing a virtual IP address (using a protocol such as VRRP), where the passive node takes the address if the active stops sending heartbeats, or an active-active setup using several load balancers announced with anycast or ECMP routing. Also remember a hidden SPOF: the load balancer's configuration. A bad rule pushed to both nodes breaks both.

3.3 Application tier

The easiest layer, provided the servers are stateless (section 6). Run at least two instances in every zone you use, put them behind the load balancer with health checks, and use an autoscaling group or orchestrator that replaces dead instances. Beware shared fate: all instances launched from one bad image, or all pulling the same broken config, are effectively one component.

3.4 Cache

A cache is usually "optional" for correctness, but not for capacity: if the cache dies and every request goes to the database, the database may be overwhelmed (a cache stampede, Chapter 13). Run the cache as a replicated cluster with a replica per shard and automatic promotion, so a node loss costs a small fraction of the keyspace rather than all of it. Protect the database behind it with request coalescing (many concurrent misses for one key trigger one database read) and rate limits, and warm a replacement cache before sending full traffic.

3.5 Database

The database is the hardest layer because it holds state. The standard answer is replication (Chapter 24): a primary that takes writes and one or more replicas in other zones that can be promoted. Sections 7 and 8 cover failover in depth.

3.6 Queue and stream

A message broker that stores each message on only one node loses data when that node dies. Production brokers replicate partitions across nodes in different zones (a replication factor of 3 is typical) and let producers require acknowledgements from several replicas before a write counts as committed. The tradeoff is write latency against durability, the same synchronous versus asynchronous choice as in database replication.

3.7 Availability zone and region

An availability zone (AZ) is one or more data centers with independent power, cooling and networking, inside a region, connected to the other AZs by low-latency links (typically a few milliseconds or less). Spreading each tier across three zones means a zone failure removes about a third of capacity but not the service. A region is a geographic cluster of zones, and region failures, while rare, do happen (control plane failures, large networking incidents, natural disasters). Surviving them needs a second region, which is the DR and multi-region topic of sections 10 to 14.

4. Redundancy models

"Add a copy" can be done in several ways. They differ in how much spare capacity you pay for, how fast takeover is, and how hard they are to keep correct.

4.1 Active-active

All copies serve live traffic at the same time. If one fails, the others simply absorb its share. Because every copy is exercised constantly, you know it works, and there is no failover event, only a reduction in capacity. The costs: each copy must be able to take the extra load (headroom, section 15), and for stateful systems, running several writers requires solving data conflicts (multi-leader replication, Chapter 26). Stateless tiers are naturally active-active.

4.2 Active-passive

One copy serves; another stands by and takes over on failure. How ready the standby is gives three flavours:

  • Hot standby: running, fully up to date (data replicated continuously), able to take over in seconds. You pay for a full second set of machines doing no useful production work (though it can serve read-only queries in some databases).
  • Warm standby: running, but smaller or only partially configured, with data replicated; it needs minutes to scale up or finish startup before taking full traffic.
  • Cold standby: not running, or only the data and recipes (images, infrastructure code) exist. Recovery means provisioning and starting everything, taking tens of minutes to hours. Cheapest, slowest.

Active-passive is simpler for stateful systems because only one node writes, but the standby is an untested assumption until it is used, which is why failover drills matter (section 17). A passive copy that has never taken traffic often fails its first real takeover (stale config, missing permissions, undersized).

4.3 N+1 and N+M

These describe spare capacity rather than a pairing. N+1 means you need N units of something to carry the load, and you deploy N+1, so any single failure still leaves N. With 6 servers of work you run 7. N+M deploys M spares, tolerating M simultaneous failures: useful when failures are frequent, repairs slow, or you must survive a whole domain loss (losing an entire AZ removes many units at once, so M is large). The cost of N+1 is small when N is large (1 spare out of 20 is 5% overhead) but large when N is small (1 spare for 1 is 100%). That is why HA is cheaper per unit at scale, and why splitting into too many small independent groups makes redundancy costly.

5. Failure domains and AZ-aware placement

A failure domain is a set of components that can fail together because they share something. Good redundancy puts the copies in different failure domains. The domains form a hierarchy, from small to large:

  • Process or container: a crash or out-of-memory kill. Fixed by restart.
  • Host (server): hardware failure, kernel panic, host maintenance.
  • Rack: shared top-of-rack switch and power distribution unit. Losing one removes every server in it.
  • Availability zone: shared building-level power, cooling and network. Caused by power loss, fire, flood, major network failure.
  • Region: shared control plane, regional networking, regional services; also correlated events such as a regional software rollout.
  • Provider: account suspension, billing problem, a global control-plane or identity outage, or a provider-wide software bug. Rarely designed for, usually by the most critical systems or by regulation.

5.1 AZ-aware placement

Placement must be deliberate. Without rules, a scheduler may put all your instances on the same host or the same zone because it is the cheapest or least loaded. Use the platform's tools: anti-affinity and topology spread constraints (in container orchestrators), placement groups configured to spread (in cloud VMs), and autoscaling groups that span all zones with balanced distribution. For data systems, tell the database or broker which zone/rack each node is in (rack awareness) so it places replicas in different ones.

Choose the number of zones with the majority math in mind. Systems that need a majority (quorum, Chapter 29) want an odd number of members spread so no single domain holds a majority. Three zones is the sweet spot: a three-member group has one per zone, survives the loss of any zone, and the overhead of capacity headroom is only 50% (section 15), whereas two zones need 100% headroom and cannot form a majority after losing one.

Finally, cross-zone traffic has costs: latency (a few milliseconds per hop that may add up in deep call chains) and, in many clouds, data transfer charges between zones. A common optimization is zone-aware routing: prefer sending a request to a healthy instance in the caller's own zone and only cross zones when it must. Done naively, this can overload a small zone, so it needs a fallback and capacity awareness.

6. Stateless versus stateful HA

The difficulty of HA depends mostly on whether the component remembers anything.

A stateless service keeps nothing between requests that is needed for correctness: no session data in local memory, no files on local disk that matter. Every instance is interchangeable, so any request can go to any instance, a dead instance is simply replaced, and capacity is added or removed freely. HA for stateless services is therefore "run N+1 behind a load balancer across zones". Achieving it means pushing state out: sessions into a shared store or signed tokens, uploads into object storage, jobs into a durable queue. It is why most designs strive to keep the application tier stateless and concentrate difficulty in a few data stores.

A stateful service holds data that must survive and must be consistent: databases, caches with persistence, queues, search indexes, file stores. You cannot simply start a replacement from an image, because the replacement needs the data. HA for stateful systems needs (1) replication of the data to other nodes, (2) a way to detect failure, (3) a promotion procedure that picks exactly one new leader, (4) a way for clients to find the new leader, and (5) a policy for what to do about writes that had not yet been replicated. Each of these has failure modes, discussed next.

Interview tip State this early: "I keep the application tier stateless so HA there is trivial, then spend my effort on the stateful components: database, queue, and cache." It shows you know where the difficulty is and lets you structure the rest of the answer around data.

7. Database failover

Chapter 24 explains replication modes; here we focus on what happens when the primary dies. The sequence has the same shape in most systems (managed relational databases, Redis with a sentinel, MongoDB replica sets, and so on):

  1. Detect: something decides the primary is gone. Usually missed heartbeats or failed health checks for a timeout period.
  2. Decide: choose a successor. Typically the replica with the most up-to-date data (smallest replication lag or highest log position), possibly required to be in a different zone.
  3. Fence: make sure the old primary can no longer accept writes (section 9).
  4. Promote: the chosen replica becomes writable; the other replicas are reconfigured to follow it.
  5. Redirect: clients are pointed at the new primary, through a DNS name update, a virtual IP, a proxy, or a client library that watches cluster topology.
  6. Repair: the old primary, when it returns, must be discarded or rebuilt as a replica, and any writes it had that never reached the new primary are reconciled or lost.

Typical total time is from several seconds (Redis sentinel, tuned systems) to one to two minutes (managed relational databases, where DNS propagation and crash recovery add time). During this window writes fail, so applications must handle errors with retries and timeouts (Chapter 40) rather than crash.

7.1 Synchronous versus asynchronous replication and data loss

With asynchronous replication the primary acknowledges a write as soon as it is local, and ships it to replicas in the background. If the primary dies, the last few writes (the replication lag, often milliseconds to seconds) may exist only on the dead node. Promoting a replica then loses acknowledged writes: the RPO (section 10) is greater than zero. With synchronous replication the primary waits for at least one replica to confirm before acknowledging, so no acknowledged write is lost (RPO of zero for single failures), but every write pays extra latency (one network round trip to the replica; a few milliseconds within a region, tens to hundreds of milliseconds across regions) and, if the synchronous replica is down, writes may block unless the system is allowed to fall back to asynchronous.

A common production compromise: semi-synchronous or quorum commit, where a write is acknowledged once a majority (or at least one of several) of replicas have it. Consensus-based databases (Chapter 29) do this inherently: a write is committed only when a majority has it, so any new leader elected from a majority is guaranteed to hold all committed writes. This gives RPO zero with automatic failover and tolerates the loss of a minority of nodes, at the price of a majority round trip per write.

Even then, the manual step should be a single well-rehearsed command or button, not a research project (section 14.1).

8. Health detection and false positives

Failover is triggered by detection, and detection is a guess. A monitor cannot tell the difference between "the server is dead", "the server is very slow", and "the network between me and the server is broken". All three look like missing responses. This produces two kinds of error, and tuning one worsens the other:

  • False positive (a spurious failover): the monitor declares a healthy primary dead, perhaps because of a brief GC pause, a spike of load, or a network blip, and triggers an unnecessary failover. The failover itself causes downtime, cache cold starts and potentially data loss, so a spurious failover is worse than the problem it was guessing about.
  • False negative (slow detection): the monitor waits too long, so a real failure lasts longer than needed and consumes error budget.

Practical techniques:

  • Require several consecutive failures (for example 3 failed checks, 5 seconds apart) before acting. A tradeoff you can compute: detection time = interval × threshold. 3 × 5 s = 15 s of added downtime.
  • Use multiple independent observers and require a quorum of them to agree the node is down. If only one monitor loses contact, probably the problem is the monitor's own network.
  • Use deep health checks carefully. A shallow check ("process is up, port open") misses a node that is up but cannot reach its disk or database. A deep check (runs a trivial real query) catches more but can cause cascading outages: if the deep check calls a shared dependency and that dependency is slow, every node reports unhealthy and the load balancer removes the whole fleet. Many load balancers refuse to remove more than a given percentage of nodes (fail-open or "panic mode") for this reason.
  • Separate liveness from readiness. Liveness asks "should I restart this?" Readiness asks "should I send traffic?" A node busy warming its cache is alive but not ready, and restarting it would be harmful.
  • Add hysteresis. Require more successes to mark a node healthy again than failures to mark it unhealthy, so a flapping node does not bounce in and out of service.
  • Rate limit automation. A failover controller that can only act a limited number of times per hour cannot spiral into repeated failovers.
Watch out A classic outage: a network issue makes health checks fail everywhere, the automation "heals" by replacing or restarting everything at once, and the healing causes the full outage. Automation needs safety limits (max percentage of fleet acted on, cool-down periods) and a human-visible kill switch.

9. Split brain and fencing

Split brain occurs when two nodes both believe they are the primary and both accept writes. It typically arises from exactly the false positive in section 8: a network partition separates the old primary from the failover controller and the replicas. The controller cannot reach the old primary, concludes it is dead and promotes a replica. But the old primary is not dead; it is alive and still being reached by some clients. Now two primaries accept different writes, and their data diverges. Reconciling afterwards is hard and sometimes impossible (two different orders were confirmed to different customers for the last unit of stock; two different account balances).

Techniques to prevent split brain, from weakest to strongest:

  • STONITH ("shoot the other node in the head"): before promoting, the controller forcibly powers off or isolates the old primary through an out-of-band channel (power controller, cloud API that stops the instance, network ACL). Simple and effective, but requires that out-of-band channel to be reachable and reliable.
  • Quorum: a node may act as primary only if it can contact a majority of the cluster. In a partition, at most one side holds a majority, so the minority side (which includes a partitioned old primary) must stop accepting writes. This is why clusters use odd member counts, and why a two-node cluster needs a third witness. This is the consensus approach of Chapter 29.
  • Leases: a primary holds a time-limited lease and must renew it; if it cannot renew, it must stop writing before the lease expires, and a new primary is only chosen after expiry. This depends on bounded clock drift, so leases need a safety margin.
  • Fencing tokens: every new leadership term gets a larger number, and the resource being written (storage, a lock service, a downstream API) rejects any request carrying a smaller number than it has already seen. This protects even against a paused old leader that wakes up after a long garbage-collection stall believing it is still the leader. The check lives in the resource, which is the only place that can enforce it.

The key insight: do not rely on the old primary to behave. A fenced design works even if the old leader is slow, confused, or malicious, because the resource, not the leader, decides.

10. Disaster recovery: RPO and RTO

Disaster recovery starts by asking two questions about the worst plausible day, agreed with the business (not chosen by engineers alone):

  • RPO (Recovery Point Objective): how much data can we afford to lose, measured as time? If the last good copy of the data is from 10 minutes before the disaster, RPO achieved is 10 minutes. An RPO of 15 minutes means that at most the last 15 minutes of writes may be lost. An RPO of zero means no acknowledged write may be lost, which requires synchronous replication to a second location.
  • RTO (Recovery Time Objective): how long can the service be down? Measured from the start of the outage until service is acceptably restored. An RTO of 1 hour means we must be serving again within an hour.

Two points often confused. First, RPO and RTO are targets in a design, but what you actually achieve in a real event is measured, and only drills tell you the true number (section 17). Second, RPO and RTO are independent. A daily backup restored in 10 minutes has RPO of up to 24 hours and RTO of 10 minutes. A synchronously replicated database with no standby application servers has RPO zero and RTO of hours. Each one needs its own mechanism: RPO is about how data is copied; RTO is about how quickly compute, network and configuration can be brought up and traffic redirected.

Also set a MTD / maximum tolerable downtime (the point at which the business is damaged beyond repair) above the RTO, and a recovery level: do we need full functionality or is a degraded mode acceptable? Not every system deserves the same tier. A common practice is to classify systems: tier 0 (payments, auth: RPO near 0, RTO minutes), tier 1 (core product: RPO minutes, RTO an hour), tier 2 (reports, internal: RPO hours, RTO a day), and spend accordingly. Paying tier 0 prices for everything is a common and costly mistake.

12. Backups in depth

Replication copies bad changes as faithfully as good ones, so backups are the last line of defence and the answer to corruption, deletion and ransomware. A backup strategy has several parts.

12.1 Full, incremental and differential

A full backup copies everything. It is simple to restore (one artifact) but slow and large. An incremental backup copies only what changed since the previous backup (full or incremental), so it is small and fast, but a restore needs the last full backup plus every incremental after it, in order; losing one link breaks the chain. A differential backup copies everything changed since the last full backup, so a restore needs the full plus the latest differential, trading a larger backup for a simpler restore. A typical schedule is a weekly full plus daily incrementals, and the full is re-taken periodically so chains do not grow without bound.

12.2 Snapshots

A snapshot is a point-in-time image of a volume or database taken by the storage layer, usually using copy-on-write: the snapshot records pointers to existing blocks and preserves old blocks only when they are later changed. Snapshots are fast (seconds) and cheap, and are often incremental underneath. Two cautions: a snapshot taken while a database is mid-write may be only crash-consistent (like pulling the power; recoverable via the database's own crash recovery) rather than application-consistent (quiesced so the data is clean), and a snapshot stored in the same account and region as the original is not a true DR copy: it dies with the volume's account or region, or can be deleted by the same stolen credentials.

12.3 Point-in-time recovery (PITR) with the write-ahead log

Databases write every change to a sequential write-ahead log (WAL) (or binlog, redo log) before applying it to the data files. If you take a periodic base backup and also continuously archive the log, you can restore the base backup and then replay the log forward to any chosen moment, for example 14:32:10, just before someone ran a destructive command. This is PITR. It turns RPO from "time since last backup" (hours) into "time since last archived log segment" (often seconds to a minute), and it is the main defence against logical errors such as an accidental delete or a bad migration. Restore time is the base restore time plus the replay time, which grows with the amount of log to replay, so take base backups often enough to bound replay.

12.4 Backup verification and restore drills

A backup you have never restored is a hope, not a backup. Backups fail silently in many ways: the job reports success but backs up an empty directory, the encryption key is lost, the backup is corrupt, the restore tool version has drifted, the backup omits a new table, or the restore takes 14 hours when the RTO was 4. Controls: (1) automated verification after each backup (checksums, and ideally restoring to a scratch environment and running sanity queries such as row counts and a schema check); (2) regular full restore drills, timed, performed by the on-call team using the documented runbook, which also measures real RTO; (3) alerts on absence of a fresh backup, not only on job errors; (4) tracking restore time versus data growth, since restore time grows with data size and one day will exceed the RTO.

12.5 Immutability, ransomware and offsite copies

Ransomware and malicious insiders attack backups first, because deleting them removes your ability to recover. Defences:

  • Immutable (write-once, WORM) storage: object-lock features that make a backup undeletable and unmodifiable for a retention period, even by administrators and even with stolen credentials.
  • Separate account and credentials: backups live in a different cloud account (or a different provider) from production, written by a role that can add but not delete. A compromise of production does not reach them.
  • Offsite and cross-region: copies in another region so a regional disaster does not take both. The long-standing "3-2-1" rule: at least 3 copies, on 2 different kinds of media or systems, with 1 offsite. A common modern extension adds one immutable or offline copy and zero errors on verification ("3-2-1-1-0").
  • Encryption with managed keys and a plan for the keys: if the keys live only in the failed region, the backups are useless. Replicate or escrow keys separately.
  • Retention policy: keep daily copies for some weeks and monthly for longer. Corruption is often discovered late; if you only keep 7 days and the bad data was introduced 10 days ago, all your backups are bad.

13. Data corruption versus infrastructure loss

These are two different disasters needing different defences, and designs often defend against only one.

Infrastructure lossData corruption / logical error
ExamplesHost, zone, region failure; disk failureBad migration, buggy deploy writing wrong values, operator error (DROP TABLE), ransomware, malicious deletion
Replication helps?Yes, that is its purposeNo: the bad change is replicated faithfully, often within milliseconds

Useful defences against logical errors: delayed replicas (a replica deliberately kept, say, one hour behind, which holds the data from before a mistake long enough for you to notice); soft deletes (mark rows deleted and purge later); object versioning in storage; change review and guard rails on destructive operations (two-person approval for production data changes, requiring a WHERE, running migrations in transactions or staged); and data validation jobs that detect anomalies early. Recovery from corruption is rarely "restore everything": usually you restore a copy to a side environment, extract the correct rows for the affected tenants or time range, and merge them into the live database, because the live system has kept taking valid writes since the mistake. Planning for this reconciliation ahead of time is part of a good runbook.

14. Multi-region failover and traffic redirection

14.1 The failover runbook

Regional failover is high-stakes, rare and stressful, which is exactly when people make mistakes. So it should be a written, rehearsed, ideally partly automated runbook. A typical sequence for an active-passive (warm or hot standby) setup:

  1. Declare: an authorised incident commander decides to fail over. Criteria should be pre-agreed (for example: "primary region impact exceeds 15 minutes with no ETA, and the DR region reports healthy"), because debating criteria mid-incident wastes time. Announce in the incident channel and start a timeline log.
  2. Freeze changes: stop deployments and scheduled jobs in both regions, so nothing else moves.
  3. Fence the old primary: if reachable, put the primary database in read-only or shut it down; if not reachable, block writes at the traffic layer so that when the region returns, it cannot accept writes (section 9).
  4. Check data state: measure how far behind the DR replica is (the replication lag at the time of loss), and record the last replicated position. That number is the actual data loss, and it must be recorded for later reconciliation and for telling customers.
  5. Promote the DR database to primary, and reconfigure replication so that the old primary will later resync from the new one rather than the reverse.
  6. Scale up and verify the compute tier in the DR region (pilot light: start it; warm standby: scale it), confirm health checks, check dependencies, secrets and certificates exist there, and run smoke tests with synthetic traffic.
  7. Shift traffic: change DNS or global load balancer weights to the DR region, ideally gradually (10%, then 50%, then 100%) while watching error rates, to avoid a thundering herd on cold caches.
  8. Monitor and communicate: watch errors, latency, queue depth; post status updates at a regular interval.
  9. Failback (later, deliberately): when the original region is healthy, resync data in the reverse direction, validate, and move traffic back at a quiet time. Failback is a second migration and deserves its own rehearsed plan; many teams simply stay in the new region and make the old one the new standby.

The data-movement mechanics (replicating across regions, cutting over writers, handling in-flight writes) are covered in Chapter 26; this section is the operational view. Notice that step 5 is the point of no return: once you promote, any writes in the old region that were not replicated are effectively orphaned.

14.2 Traffic failover via DNS and global load balancing

Users must be sent to the surviving region. There are two main mechanisms, and they have different speeds.

DNS-based failover (health-checked records, weighted or failover routing policies): the DNS service checks each region and stops returning the failed region's addresses. It is cheap and works for any client, but its speed is bounded by caching: resolvers, operating systems, browsers and long-lived connections may continue using the old address after the TTL expires, so you plan for a tail of traffic that arrives late, typically a few minutes. It is coarse (no per-request routing) but simple.

Anycast and global load balancers: the same IP address is announced from many locations, and the network routes each user to the nearest entry point; the global load balancer then forwards over the provider's backbone to a healthy region and can reroute within seconds when health checks fail, without waiting on resolver caches. It also allows weighted shifting and per-request decisions. It costs more and ties you to a provider's product. Both mechanisms depend on correct health checks: a check that tests only "the load balancer answers" will not notice that the application behind it cannot reach its database, and traffic keeps flowing to a broken region. A check should reflect that the region can really serve, but (section 8) must avoid failing everything because one shared dependency blinked. See Chapter 9 for global load balancing.

15. Dependencies, static stability and failover capacity

15.1 Dependency failure and static stability

Your availability is bounded by what you depend on (section 2.2). The craft is to reduce hard dependencies on the failure path. Two ideas matter.

Soft dependencies and degradation. Classify each dependency as critical (cannot function without it) or optional (can degrade). If the recommendations service is down, show a generic list; if the review count is unavailable, hide it. The patterns (timeouts, fallbacks, circuit breakers, load shedding) are in Chapter 40.

Static stability. A system is statically stable if it keeps working, without taking any new action, when a dependency it normally uses for changes becomes unavailable. The key distinction is between the data plane (the path that serves user requests) and the control plane (the machinery that provisions, configures and scales). Control planes are more complex, are used less often, and tend to fail more than data planes. A statically stable design lets the data plane keep going when the control plane is down. Examples:

  • Pre-provision capacity instead of relying on autoscaling during a failure. If losing a zone means launching new instances through a cloud API, and the API is degraded exactly because many customers are doing the same thing, your recovery stalls. Run the spare capacity already (section 15.2).
  • Cache and local fallback for configuration and credentials: services keep running with their last known good configuration and cached credentials if the config service or identity provider is briefly down, rather than refusing to start or serve.
  • No dependence on the failed region for the failover itself: do not host your runbook, your DNS control, your secrets, your CI/CD or your monitoring only in the region you may need to leave. A very common embarrassing outage is "we could not fail over because the failover tool was in the failed region."
  • When a node's health check cannot reach the dependency, do not mass-remove nodes (the fail-open idea from section 8).

15.2 Capacity for failover: N+1 and the "survive losing an AZ" math

Redundancy that has no spare capacity fails when needed: if two servers are each 80% busy and one dies, the other is asked to carry 160% and collapses, and the failure cascades. So capacity planning must be done for the failed state, not the healthy one.

For a fleet spread evenly over Z zones that must survive the loss of one zone, the remaining Z-1 zones must carry the full load L. Each zone therefore needs capacity of at least L ÷ (Z-1), and the total provisioned is Z × L ÷ (Z-1). The steady-state utilization when all zones are healthy is then at most (Z-1) ÷ Z.

Zones (Z)Total capacity neededOverhead above loadMax steady utilizationUtilization after losing one zone
22.00 x L100%50%100% (no margin)
31.50 x L50%66.7%100% (no margin)
41.33 x L33%75%100% (no margin)
61.20 x L20%83%100% (no margin)

Running exactly at 100% after the loss is dangerous (no margin for variation, retry storms, or cache coldness), so real plans add a safety factor: target at most 70-80% of capacity after losing a zone. Worked example: peak load is 10,000 requests per second, and each instance handles 500 requests per second at full utilization. With 3 zones and a target of 75% utilization after a zone loss: after the loss, 2 zones must handle 10,000 rps at 75%, so need 10,000 ÷ (500 × 0.75) = 26.7, rounded up to 28 instances, so 14 per zone, 42 instances in total. In normal operation, 42 instances sharing 10,000 rps are at 10,000 ÷ (42 × 500) = 47.6% utilization. That looks "wasteful", but it is the price of tolerating a zone loss at peak with a margin. Autoscaling can reduce the standing overhead off-peak, but remember static stability: do not depend on the scale-up working during the very event you are protecting against.

Remember the same logic applies to the downstream tiers: the database must handle the post-failure load, connection pools must be sized for the surviving instances, and the cache must survive a cold start of the failed zone's share. Capacity for failover must be checked at each layer.

16. Worked example: designing for 99.99%

Requirement: a checkout API with a 99.99% monthly availability SLO, an RPO of zero for acknowledged orders for a single-zone failure and under 1 minute for a regional disaster, and an RTO of 30 minutes for regional disaster. Peak load 10,000 requests per second.

16.1 Step one: the budget

99.99% allows 0.01% of a 43,800 minute month, which is 4.38 minutes (263 seconds). Everything that goes wrong in the month, planned or not, must fit inside it, unless planned maintenance is excluded by the SLO definition (decide that explicitly). A single human-handled incident would exceed it, so the design must make the common failures self-healing.

16.2 Step two: allocate the budget by layer

16.3 Step three: check the composition

Take the pessimistic series chain: edge 99.99% × LB 99.99% × app (redundant) 99.995% × DB (failover) 99.99% × dependencies-as-optional ~99.999% = approximately 0.9999 × 0.9999 × 0.99995 × 0.9999 ≈ 0.99965, about 99.965%. That is under 99.99%, which shows that naive composition of several four-nines parts does not yield four nines. Therefore the design must (a) make the edge and LB layers part of the same managed multi-zone service where possible (one 99.99%+ component instead of two), (b) raise the app tier to far better than 99.99% by redundancy and safe deployments, and (c) make the database failover faster and rarer (reliable hardware, synchronous standby, tested automation). Realistic targets after those moves: edge+LB 99.995%, app 99.998%, DB 99.995%, deps ~99.999%, combined ≈ 99.987%, which is still just short of 99.99%. That is the honest result: four nines is hard and the margin is thin. You push the remaining gap by reducing blast radius: with the user base partitioned into independent cells (Chapter 42), a failure affects only a fraction of requests, and a request-based SLI improves accordingly (a cell holding 10% of users that fails entirely for 10 minutes costs 1 minute in the request-weighted SLI).

16.4 Step four: the data and DR plan

  • Single-zone failure (RPO 0): synchronous replication to a standby in another zone; commit requires standby confirmation (adds about 1-2 ms of commit latency inside a region). Automatic failover with fencing (quorum with a witness in zone 3, and a fencing token on the storage volume).
  • Regional disaster (RPO under 1 min, RTO 30 min): asynchronous replica in a second region, with lag alarmed at 30 seconds. Warm standby: database replica running, application tier at 25% scale (about 10 instances), able to scale to full in under 15 minutes because capacity is reserved and images are pre-baked. Manual, rehearsed regional failover (section 14.1) decided by the incident commander, using a global load balancer to shift traffic in minutes. Expected loss is the replication lag at the moment of failure, a few seconds.
  • Corruption: PITR with continuous WAL archiving to a separate account, base backup nightly, restore drill monthly, immutable copy retained 35 days, a delayed replica 1 hour behind.
  • Operations: staged rollout (one cell, then 10%, then all) with automatic rollback on SLO burn; game day every quarter for zone loss and every six months for regional failover.

16.5 Step five: the cost

Say a single zone-sized slice costs 1 unit. Surviving a zone loss means 1.5x for the app tier compared with the minimal capacity; a synchronous standby doubles database cost; a warm standby region adds roughly 25% app capacity plus a full database replica plus replicated storage. Overall, the system costs on the order of 2 to 2.5 times a bare single-zone deployment. If the business says that is too much, the decision is made explicit: lower the target to 99.95% (drop the regional warm standby and rely on backup/restore, RTO a few hours) and save perhaps a third. Communicating this tradeoff in dollars is part of the answer.

What a strong answer shows It starts from the error budget in minutes, assigns that budget to components, notes that composing four-nines parts does not give four nines, uses blast-radius reduction to close the gap, separates infrastructure loss from corruption, matches the DR strategy to RPO and RTO, and names the cost.

17. Game days and DR testing

An untested recovery plan almost never works as written. Systems drift, credentials expire, runbooks go stale, and the person who built the failover left. The only way to know your real RTO and RPO is to rehearse. Levels of testing, from safest to most realistic:

  1. Tabletop exercise: the team talks through a scenario ("the primary region is gone; what do we do?") with the runbook in hand. Cheap, finds gaps in decision-making and ownership, but proves nothing about the technology.
  2. Component failure tests: terminate an instance, kill a database primary in staging, remove a node from a cluster, and confirm automatic recovery and alerting.
  3. Game day: a scheduled, announced exercise in which you deliberately inject a significant failure (disable a zone, block a dependency, fail over a database), usually in production or a production-like environment, with the team running response as if it were real, and observers measuring time to detect, time to mitigate, and customer impact. Goals are learning, not blame.
  4. Chaos engineering: continuous, automated, small-scale fault injection (random instance kills, latency injection, dependency errors) with safeguards and abort conditions, so resilience is validated constantly rather than annually. Needs mature observability first.
  5. Full DR failover test: actually shift production traffic to the DR region, run there for a period, and then fail back. It is the only real proof of the regional plan, and also the most disruptive, so it is done carefully and with prior communication. Active-active systems do this implicitly every day.

Practical guidance: define the hypothesis and success criteria beforehand ("traffic moves in under 5 minutes; no more than 0.1% of requests fail"); define abort conditions and a one-step rollback; start in a small blast radius and grow; make sure monitoring and communication tools are available (and not in the failing domain); record timings; and turn each finding into a tracked fix. Also practise the restore side (section 12.4) and test failure of the failover tooling itself, the permissions of the on-call group, and the "break glass" access path that works when normal single sign-on is down.

18. Incident response basics

HA and DR design reduce the number and impact of incidents; they never remove them, so how people respond matters as much as the architecture. An incident is an unplanned event that degrades the service. Good response has structure:

  • Detect: alerts based on user-visible symptoms (error rate, latency, SLO burn rate) rather than only on causes. Alert on the budget being spent too fast (multi-window burn-rate alerts, Chapter 54), not on every blip.
  • Roles: one incident commander who coordinates and decides but does not debug; operations/tech leads who investigate and change things; a communications lead who updates stakeholders and customers on a regular schedule; and a scribe who keeps the timeline. Clear roles prevent ten people changing things at once.
  • Severity levels: predefined (for example Sev1: major customer impact; Sev3: minor), each with expected response, paging and update rules.
  • Mitigate first, diagnose later: the first goal is to stop customer harm, using the fastest safe action: roll back the last deploy, fail over, disable a feature flag, shed load, scale up. Root-causing happens after the bleeding stops. "Roll back" should be a one-click, rehearsed move.
  • Communicate: a status page and regular updates, even when the update is "still investigating". Silence breeds escalation and duplicated work.
  • Runbooks and on-call: a documented first-response guide per alert, a sustainable on-call rotation with escalation paths, and handoffs.
  • Close: declare resolved only when metrics confirm recovery, record the customer impact (duration and affected users), and schedule the postmortem.

18.1 Postmortems

A postmortem (incident review) is a written analysis done after significant incidents. The valuable ones are blameless: they assume people acted reasonably with the information they had and ask what in the system, tooling or process allowed the error or made it hard to detect or fix, rather than who to blame. Blame causes people to hide mistakes, which makes the next incident worse. A good postmortem contains: a summary and impact (duration, users, error budget consumed), a timeline with times for detection, escalation, mitigation and resolution, root cause and contributing factors (usually several: for example a bad config, plus a missing validation, plus a failed alert, plus a slow rollback), what went well and what was lucky, and concrete action items with owners and due dates, prioritised against feature work. The error budget makes this fair: if repeated incidents burn the budget, reliability work takes priority over new features until it recovers. Track recurring causes across postmortems, because patterns (always deploys, always one dependency) show where to invest.

19. Common mistakes and how they show up in interviews

  • "We replicate, so we're safe from deletion." Replication copies mistakes. You need point-in-time recovery and immutable backups.
  • Adding a second region without capacity or tested failover. A standby that has never taken traffic often cannot. Rehearse.
  • Failover that creates a bigger outage. Overly eager health checks, no fencing, mass restarts. Add hysteresis, quorum and rate limits.
  • Ignoring the control plane. Plans that depend on autoscaling, a provisioning API or a console login during a regional event fail when everyone does it at once.
  • Calling 99.99% a goal without a budget. Always translate the target to minutes and allocate it.
  • Two-zone designs for quorum systems. Two members cannot hold a majority after one failure; use three zones or add a witness.
  • Equating RPO/RTO targets with achieved results. Only drills measure them.
  • Ignoring correlated failure. A bad deploy to all regions at once defeats multi-region. Stagger rollouts across regions and cells.

20. Interview Q&A

What is the difference between high availability and disaster recovery?

High availability keeps the service running through common, small failures such as a dead host or a lost availability zone, by having redundant components that take over automatically within seconds, so users see little or no impact. Disaster recovery is about restoring service after a large event that redundancy within a site could not absorb, for example losing a whole region or having the data corrupted or deleted; it accepts an outage and some data loss and is measured by RTO and RPO. HA mostly protects against infrastructure failure through replication, while DR also needs backups and a second site, because replication would faithfully copy a corrupting change. A complete design has both, sized per system tier by business requirements.

Explain RPO and RTO and what mechanisms drive each.

RPO, the recovery point objective, is how much data loss is tolerable, expressed as time: if the last recoverable copy is 5 minutes old at the time of disaster, we lose up to 5 minutes of writes. RTO, the recovery time objective, is how long the service may remain down before it is restored. RPO is driven by how data is copied: synchronous replication gives near zero, asynchronous replication gives seconds of lag, periodic backups give hours, and continuous log archiving gives PITR with seconds to minutes. RTO is driven by how prepared the compute and traffic path are: hot or active-active standby gives seconds to minutes, pilot light tens of minutes, and restore from backup hours. They are independent, and the achieved values are known only after you test them.

How do you compute the availability of a system with several dependencies, and what does it imply?

For components that are all required in series, you multiply their availabilities, so the result is lower than the weakest component: four hard dependencies at 99.95%, 99.95%, 99.9% and 99.99% give about 99.79%. For redundant copies in parallel, you multiply the unavailabilities, so two 99% copies give 99.99%, provided failures are independent. The implications are that each hard dependency costs availability, so I make non-critical dependencies optional with fallbacks, add redundancy to critical ones, and recognise that shared causes such as a bad deploy, a common config, or one power feed break the independence assumption. At high nines, the change process is as important as hardware redundancy.

How many nines is 4.38 minutes a month, and can humans meet that?

4.38 minutes a month is 99.99%, four nines. A human responding to a page typically needs several minutes just to acknowledge, log in, and understand the situation, so a single human-handled incident can consume the entire monthly budget. To meet it, common failures must be handled by automation: automatic failover of the database and instances, automatic rollback of bad deployments, and traffic rerouting. Humans are reserved for rare, ambiguous scenarios and for improving the system afterwards. Four nines therefore requires investment in automation, fencing, testing, and in limiting blast radius so that failures affect only a fraction of users.

Should database failover be automatic or manual?

For local failures within a region, such as a host or a zone, I prefer automatic failover with synchronous or quorum replication, because the time budget at high availability targets leaves no room for human response, and the data loss risk is zero when replicas are synchronous. The danger of automation is false positives and split brain, so it needs multiple observers, consecutive-failure thresholds, fencing of the old primary, and rate limits. For cross-region failover I prefer a human decision using a rehearsed runbook, since the replication is typically asynchronous, a wrong decision can lose data or split the brain across regions, and the operator can judge whether the primary region will recover soon. The manual step should still be a single prepared action, not a research project.

What is split brain and how do you prevent it?

Split brain is when two nodes both believe they are the leader and both accept writes, typically because a network partition made the controller promote a new leader while the old one was alive. The data diverges and is hard to reconcile. Prevention relies on mechanisms that do not trust the old leader to behave: quorum, where a node may lead only if it reaches a majority so at most one side of a partition can; leases that expire so the old leader stops by itself; STONITH, which forcibly powers off or isolates the old node before promotion; and fencing tokens, where each leadership term gets a larger number and the storage rejects requests with an older one. Consensus protocols such as Raft bundle these ideas.

Walk through the four DR strategies and when you would use each.

Backup and restore keeps only backups in the DR region and rebuilds everything from infrastructure code on demand: cheapest, with RTO of hours and RPO tied to backup frequency; fine for internal and low-tier systems. Pilot light replicates the data continuously and keeps only the core, usually the database, running, starting the rest on demand: RTO tens of minutes, RPO seconds to minutes, modest cost. Warm standby runs a scaled-down but complete copy that is scaled up and switched on failure, giving minutes of RTO at moderate cost. Multi-site active-active runs full capacity in several regions serving traffic, giving near-zero RTO but the highest cost and the added complexity of multi-writer data and conflict handling. I pick the cheapest that meets the tier's RPO and RTO.

Why is replication not a backup?

Replication keeps copies synchronised, so any change, including a destructive one, is applied to every replica within moments. An accidental DROP TABLE, a buggy migration that overwrites values, a ransomware encryption, or a malicious delete is faithfully propagated, leaving no good copy. Backups are point-in-time, versioned copies kept separately, so you can go back to before the mistake, particularly with PITR using the write-ahead log. Backups should also be immutable and stored in a separate account and region to withstand stolen credentials. Replication handles infrastructure failure; backups handle logical failure. A delayed replica, which deliberately lags by an hour, is a useful middle ground.

How much spare capacity do you need to survive the loss of an availability zone?

With Z zones sharing load L equally, after losing one the remaining Z minus 1 zones must carry all of L, so total provisioned capacity must be at least Z times L divided by Z minus 1. For three zones that is 1.5 times the load, which means normal utilization must stay at or below about 67 percent, and I would add a safety margin to keep post-failure utilization under 70 to 80 percent. Two zones need 100 percent overhead; more zones reduce the overhead, which is a reason to use three or more. I provision this capacity in advance rather than counting on autoscaling during the failure, because the cloud control plane may be degraded at that moment, and I check that databases, connection pools and caches can handle the shifted load too.

What is static stability?

A statically stable system continues to operate correctly when a dependency it uses for making changes becomes unavailable, without needing to take any action. It comes from separating the data plane, which serves requests, from the control plane, which provisions and configures resources, and ensuring the data plane does not need the control plane to keep working. Concretely, you pre-provision spare capacity instead of launching instances during a failure, cache configuration and credentials locally so services keep running if the config or identity service blinks, and keep failover tooling and runbooks outside the failure domain. The benefit is that during a large event, when control planes are under heavy demand and most likely to fail, your recovery does not depend on them.

How do you test that your DR plan actually works?

I escalate through levels. Tabletop exercises test decisions and ownership. Component failure tests and chaos experiments verify automatic recovery of hosts, nodes, and dependencies continuously. Game days inject a larger failure such as losing a zone or failing over a database, with defined hypotheses, success criteria, abort conditions and observers timing detection, mitigation and impact. Finally I do periodic full regional failover tests that move real traffic to the DR region and later fail back. I also run timed backup restore drills, because that is the only way to learn the real RTO and to discover that credentials, keys, quotas or runbooks were out of date. Each finding becomes a tracked action item.

What goes into a good postmortem?

A blameless written report produced soon after a significant incident. It states the customer impact and duration, including how much error budget was consumed, gives a timeline of detection, escalation, mitigation and resolution, and analyses root cause and contributing factors rather than a single culprit, since incidents usually come from several weaknesses combining: for example a bad config, missing validation, a slow alert, and a slow rollback. It records what went well and what was lucky, and ends with concrete action items that have owners and due dates and are prioritised, with the error budget providing leverage to put reliability work ahead of features when the budget is exhausted. Blamelessness matters because blame makes people hide information, which makes the next incident worse.

21. Recap

  • HA keeps serving through expected failures with automatic redundancy; DR restores service after large failures and is defined by RPO (data loss) and RTO (downtime). Replication does not protect against corruption, backups do.
  • Availability multiplies in series and improves in parallel only for independent failures. A target such as 99.99% is a 4.38-minute monthly budget that must be allocated to layers, and it forces automation, safe deployments and blast-radius control. SLO is the internal target, SLA the looser contract.
  • Hunt SPOFs layer by layer: DNS, load balancer, app, cache, database, queue, zone, region. Place copies in different failure domains with enforced anti-affinity, use three zones for quorum, and keep the application tier stateless.
  • Database failover is detect, decide, fence, promote, redirect, repair. Synchronous or quorum replication gives zero loss; asynchronous gives lag. Automatic failover for local failures, rehearsed manual for regional ones.
  • Health detection is a guess: use thresholds, multiple observers, hysteresis and rate limits. Prevent split brain with quorum, leases, STONITH and fencing tokens enforced by the resource.
  • DR strategies range from backup and restore through pilot light and warm standby to active-active; pick the cheapest that meets each tier's RPO and RTO.
  • Backups need full and incremental schedules, PITR through the WAL, automated verification, timed restore drills, immutability, separate accounts and offsite copies.
  • Plan capacity for the failed state: surviving one of three zones needs about 1.5 times the load, pre-provisioned. Design for static stability so recovery does not depend on the control plane.
  • Rehearse with tabletop exercises, game days, chaos tests and real failovers; respond to incidents with roles, mitigation first, clear communication, and learn through blameless postmortems.
