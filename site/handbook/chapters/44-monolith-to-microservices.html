Monolith to Microservices

Microservices are an organizational and operational trade, not a free upgrade. This chapter explains what a monolith and a microservice really are, what each costs, when splitting is worth it and when it is a mistake, and how to migrate safely with the strangler fig and its companion techniques.

1. What a microservice is, and what it is not

Before arguing about monoliths and microservices, we need precise definitions, because most bad decisions in this area come from fuzzy ones.

A monolith is an application that is built, tested, and deployed as a single unit. All of its features (catalog, cart, orders, payments, notifications) live in one codebase or one deployable artifact, and they call each other with ordinary in-process function calls. Usually they share one database. "Monolith" describes the deployment unit, not the quality of the code inside. A monolith can be beautifully organized or a tangled mess.

A microservice is a small, independently deployable service that owns one cohesive piece of business capability and the data behind it, and that communicates with other services over the network. Four properties matter more than the word "small":

  • Independently deployable. You can release the service without releasing, coordinating with, or even notifying any other service. If two services must always ship together, they are one service wearing two hats.
  • Owns its data. Only that service reads and writes its tables. Everyone else goes through its API. If three services share one database schema, they are coupled through it. Chapter 46 covers data ownership in depth.
  • Organized around a business capability. A service is "Payments" or "Inventory", not "the database layer" or "the string utilities". Boundaries follow the business, not the technology stack. Chapter 45 covers how to find boundaries.
  • Owned by a team. One team builds it, runs it, and carries the pager for it. "You build it, you run it."

It helps equally to say what microservices are not:

  • They are not defined by lines of code. A 50,000-line service with one clear owner and one clear job is a fine microservice. A 200-line service that cannot do anything without calling four others is not.
  • They are not "a monolith split into many repositories". Splitting code across repos while keeping a shared database and lockstep releases gives you the costs of both worlds (section 10).
  • They are not a performance optimization. Splitting a system adds network hops, so individual requests usually get slower, not faster. The gains are in team autonomy and scaling granularity.
  • They are not the same as containers, Kubernetes, or serverless. Those are tools you often use to run microservices, but you can run a monolith in a container and a service without any of them.
  • They are not a goal. The goal is to ship valuable changes quickly and safely, and to keep the system understandable as the organization grows. Microservices are one possible means.
Key idea A microservice is defined by independent deployability and data ownership, not by size. The test is simple: can this team ship a change on a Tuesday afternoon without asking anyone else? If not, the boundary is not really a service boundary yet.

2. What monoliths do well

The industry spent a decade treating "monolith" as an insult. That was unfair. A monolith is the right architecture for a large share of systems, and a strong candidate says so out loud. Here is what you get for free in a single deployable.

2.1 Simplicity

There is one thing to build, one thing to deploy, one thing to run locally, and one set of logs to read. A new engineer clones one repository and starts the application. There is no service discovery, no API gateway, no distributed tracing setup, no network policy to configure. The number of moving parts is the number of things that can break, and a monolith has very few.

2.2 Transactions

When "place order" must reserve stock, create an order, and record a payment attempt, a monolith with one relational database does all three inside a single ACID transaction. Either every step commits or all of them roll back. The database enforces consistency and you write almost no code for it. In a distributed system you lose this and must build sagas, compensating actions, and idempotency by hand (see Chapter 46). This single point is often the strongest reason to delay splitting.

2.3 Refactoring

Moving a function between modules, renaming a concept, or changing a method signature is one commit in a monolith. The compiler and the test suite tell you everything you broke. Across services, the same change is a multi-step contract migration: add the new endpoint, migrate every caller, remove the old one, over days or weeks, with no compiler to help. Early in a product's life, when you do not yet know where the right boundaries are, cheap refactoring is extremely valuable.

2.4 Latency and efficiency

An in-process call costs nanoseconds to microseconds. A network call inside one data center costs roughly half a millisecond to a few milliseconds once serialization, queueing, and the network are included, and its tail latency is much worse. A request that touches six modules is essentially free in a monolith and can add several milliseconds of cumulative latency, plus six more chances to fail, in a service architecture. Monoliths also avoid the CPU and bandwidth spent serializing and deserializing data at every boundary.

2.5 Operational simplicity and cost

Fewer deployables means fewer pipelines, fewer dashboards, fewer certificates, fewer on-call rotations, and lower infrastructure overhead. Running ten copies of one large application is often cheaper than running thirty small services, each with its own minimum footprint, sidecars, and monitoring agents.

Interview tip Start the discussion with "I would begin with a well-structured monolith unless there is a concrete reason not to." Then name those concrete reasons. Interviewers read this as judgment, not timidity. Jumping straight to twenty services for a product with one team signals pattern-matching rather than engineering.

3. Real monolith pain

None of the above means monoliths are free of problems. As an organization and a codebase grow, specific, measurable pains appear. These are the legitimate reasons to consider splitting. Notice that most of them are about people and release process, not about technology.

3.1 Deploy coupling

Because everything ships together, a one-line change to the notification text must go through the same pipeline, the same regression suite, and the same release train as a rewrite of the pricing engine. Teams must coordinate release dates. One team's unfinished or buggy change can block everyone else's release ("the train is red"). The more teams, the more the release frequency is dragged toward the slowest, riskiest change.

3.2 Build and test times

A codebase with millions of lines can take tens of minutes to compile and hours to run its full test suite. Developers wait for feedback, batch changes into larger and riskier commits, or skip tests. Feedback loop length is one of the biggest drivers of engineering productivity, and a growing monolith lengthens it steadily. (Better build tooling, incremental builds, and test selection can postpone this problem a long way, and it is worth trying them before splitting.)

3.3 Scaling granularity

You can only scale a monolith as a whole. If the image-resizing code needs 40 cores during a sale but everything else is idle, you must replicate the entire application, including memory for modules that do nothing, to get those cores. Different parts of a system also have different resource shapes: one part is CPU bound, another memory hungry, another needs a GPU. A single deployable cannot be tuned for all of them.

3.4 Team contention

When fifty engineers commit to one codebase, they trip over each other: merge conflicts, shared files that everyone edits, unclear ownership of modules, and code review queues where nobody has context. Without strong module boundaries, any team can reach into any other team's internals, and over time the structure erodes into a "big ball of mud" where every change has surprising effects elsewhere.

3.5 Blast radius

In a monolith, a memory leak, an infinite loop, or a bad deploy in any module can take down the entire application, including unrelated and critical features. A bug in the recommendation widget can stop checkout. There is no isolation between modules because they share a process, a heap, a thread pool, and a connection pool.

3.6 Technology lock-in

The whole application usually shares one language, one framework version, and one runtime. Upgrading a major framework version is an all-or-nothing project touching every module. Adopting a better-fit tool for one problem (say, a different language for a machine learning component) is hard.

4. What microservices give you

When the pains in section 3 are real, microservices address them directly. Each benefit below maps to a pain.

  • Independent deployment. A team ships its service when it is ready, many times a day, without a release train. This is the headline benefit and the one that justifies most of the cost. It shortens lead time and reduces the size and risk of each change.
  • Independent scaling. You scale only the hotspot: fifty copies of the search service, three of the admin service. Each service can use the instance type that fits it.
  • Fault isolation. A crash or leak in the recommendation service does not take down checkout, provided callers handle the failure gracefully (timeouts, fallbacks, circuit breakers). Isolation is a possibility you must engineer, not an automatic gift.
  • Team autonomy and ownership. A team owns a service end to end: design, code, deploy, operate. Decisions are local, communication overhead drops, and accountability is clear.
  • Technology freedom. A service can use the language, datastore, and framework that suit it, and can upgrade them on its own schedule. (In practice most organizations constrain this heavily, because every extra stack has an operating cost.)
  • Smaller, comprehensible codebases. A new engineer can understand a single service in days. The cognitive load per team is bounded.
  • Incremental replacement. You can rewrite one service without rewriting the system, which is exactly what makes the strangler fig migration (section 9) possible.

5. The cost of distribution

Every benefit above is bought with a cost, and the costs are not small. Many failed migrations happened because the team counted the benefits carefully and the costs vaguely. This section is the honest ledger.

5.1 The network is unreliable

An in-process call either returns or throws. A network call has many more outcomes: it can succeed, fail fast, fail slowly, time out after actually succeeding (so you do not know whether the work was done), return a partial result, or hang. The famous "fallacies of distributed computing" begin with "the network is reliable", and it is not. Every remote call now needs a timeout, a retry policy, idempotency so retries are safe, and a plan for when the dependency is down. That is real code, in every service, for every dependency.

5.2 Latency adds up

Suppose a page needs data from five services, each averaging 5 ms with a 99th percentile of 50 ms. If called in sequence the average adds up to 25 ms, and the chance that at least one of the five is slow is much higher than the chance for any single one. With a fan-out of 5 calls each slow 1% of the time, about 5% of requests hit at least one slow call. Tail latency amplifies with depth and breadth of call graphs. Systems with deep chains (A calls B calls C calls D) multiply this effect.

5.3 Consistency gets hard

Once data is split into separate databases, you cannot use a cross-table transaction. Updating "order" and "payment" becomes two writes in two systems that can each succeed or fail. You will accept eventual consistency, design sagas with compensations, use the outbox pattern to publish events reliably, and make every handler idempotent. Reports that used to be a single SQL join now require API composition or a replicated read model. All of that is covered in Chapter 46; here, just note it is a large and permanent cost.

5.4 Operational overhead

Each service needs a build pipeline, deployment configuration, secrets, health checks, dashboards, alerts, log routing, autoscaling policy, runbooks, and an on-call owner. Multiply by the number of services. You also need shared infrastructure: a container platform, service discovery, API gateway, service mesh or equivalent, centralized logging, metrics, and distributed tracing. Without a platform that makes this cheap, every new service costs a team weeks (Chapter 53).

5.5 Testing is harder

Unit tests stay easy, but confidence in the whole system is harder. End-to-end tests across many services are slow, flaky, and need a full environment. The industry answer is contract testing (verify that each consumer and provider agree on the interface, see Chapter 47), strong in-service tests, and testing in production with canaries and feature flags. This is a different skill set that the team must acquire.

5.6 Debugging and observability

A single user request becomes a tree of calls over many processes. A stack trace no longer shows the whole story. You need correlation IDs propagated everywhere, distributed tracing to see the call tree and where time went, and logs from many services searchable in one place. Without them, "why was this slow?" can take days.

5.7 Versioning and compatibility

Because services deploy independently, version N of the caller will meet versions N-1 and N+1 of the provider. APIs and event schemas must evolve in a backward-compatible way: add fields, do not remove or repurpose them, run old and new side by side. A breaking change needs a deprecation window and coordination, which partially defeats the "no coordination" promise.

5.8 Cost

You pay for more infrastructure (each service has a minimum footprint and often several replicas for availability), more network traffic (cross-zone data transfer is billed), more tooling licenses, and above all more people time: platform engineers, on-call load, and the engineering effort of the migration itself.

6. When to split, and when not to

The decision to split should be driven by pain you can name and measure, not by fashion. Use the following signals, and the prerequisites that must exist before you begin.

6.1 Signals that favor splitting

  • Team size. Many teams (a common rule of thumb is more than roughly 50 to 100 engineers, or more than 6 to 8 teams) working in one codebase and stepping on each other. A single team of eight rarely needs it.
  • Clear domain boundaries. You understand the business well enough to draw lines that are unlikely to move. Capabilities like payments, identity, search, and notifications have stable, well-known contours.
  • Scaling hotspots. One component needs far more (or different) resources than the rest, and it can be separated without dragging a web of dependencies.
  • Different rates of change. One area changes daily while another changes quarterly, and the slow one is holding back the fast one.
  • Different reliability or compliance needs. Payments with strict audit and isolation requirements (PCI scope), or a critical path that must stay up when a non-critical one fails.
  • Different technology needs. A component that truly needs another runtime, such as a machine learning inference service.

6.2 Signals against splitting

  • Small team (fewer than about 10 to 15 engineers). One team can own a well-built monolith with low overhead.
  • Unclear domain. A startup still finding product-market fit changes its model every few weeks. Wrong service boundaries are far more expensive to fix than wrong module boundaries.
  • The pain is really a code quality problem (a tangled codebase). Splitting a tangled monolith produces tangled services connected by a network (section 10).
  • Strong transactional coupling between the parts. If most operations need ACID across the candidate boundary, the boundary is wrong or the time is not right.
  • No platform, no observability, no automated deployment. You would multiply manual work and blindness.

6.3 Organizational prerequisites

Before extracting a single service, a team should be able to do the following. These are the entry ticket, not nice-to-haves.

7. Conway's Law, the Inverse Conway Maneuver, and team topologies

7.1 Conway's Law

In 1967 Melvin Conway observed that organizations design systems that mirror their own communication structure. Put differently: if you have four teams building a compiler, you will get a four-pass compiler. Software boundaries tend to appear wherever the organization has boundaries, because people who talk constantly build tightly integrated code, and people who rarely talk build separate pieces with narrow interfaces between them.

This is not just a curiosity. It means architecture and org chart cannot be designed independently. If your services follow one shape and your teams another, one of them will bend. A common failure is a service owned jointly by three teams: every change needs three-way coordination, which is exactly the contention microservices were meant to remove.

7.2 The Inverse Conway Maneuver

If structure follows communication, you can use that on purpose. The Inverse Conway Maneuver means deliberately shaping teams to match the architecture you want. If you want an independent Payments service, first create a Payments team with the skills and authority to own it (developers, testers, operators, a product owner), then let the architecture grow into that boundary. Splitting the code before splitting the team usually leaves the old communication paths intact, and the code drifts back toward the old shape.

7.3 Team Topologies

The book Team Topologies (Skelton and Pais) turns this into a vocabulary of four team types, of which interviewers most often care about two:

  • Stream-aligned team. A team aligned to a continuous flow of business change, such as "checkout" or "search", with the skills to take features from idea to production and operate them. This is the default team, and a stream-aligned team typically owns a handful of services.
  • Platform team. Provides internal products (deployment pipelines, observability stack, service templates, runtime) that reduce the cognitive load of stream-aligned teams. The platform is treated as a product with the stream-aligned teams as customers. This is what makes dozens of services affordable.
  • Enabling team. Specialists (for example security or performance) who help other teams adopt new capabilities temporarily, then move on.
  • Complicated-subsystem team. Owns a part needing deep specialist knowledge, such as a pricing algorithm or video codec, so that others do not need that knowledge.

The underlying idea is cognitive load: a team can only hold so much in its head. The right service size is the one a single team can fully understand and operate. If a team must understand twenty services to do its job, there are too many or the ownership is wrong.

7.4 Two-pizza teams

Amazon popularized the rule that a team should be small enough to be fed by two pizzas, roughly 5 to 9 people. Small teams communicate efficiently (the number of communication paths grows with the square of the team size: n people have n(n-1)/2 pairs, so 8 people have 28 and 16 people have 120). The two-pizza team owns its services end to end. Treat it as a heuristic about keeping team and ownership small, not as a precise law, and note that a team owning too many tiny services, or one service too big for its size, signals a mismatch.

8. The middle path: the modular monolith

Between "one big ball of mud" and "fifty services" lies a well-regarded option: the modular monolith. It is still one deployable, but internally it is divided into modules aligned with business capabilities (the bounded contexts described in Chapter 39), and the boundaries are enforced, not merely suggested.

What makes the modularity real:

  • Each module exposes a small public interface (a facade or API package). Other modules may only use that interface, never its internals. Build tools or architecture tests (for example automated checks that fail the build on illegal imports) enforce this.
  • Each module owns its tables. No other module queries them directly, and there are no cross-module joins. If Orders needs a payment status, it asks the Payments module through its interface. Separate schemas inside the same database make the rule physical.
  • Modules communicate through interfaces or in-process events, which can later be swapped for network calls without changing the callers (the idea behind branch by abstraction, section 9.2).
  • Each module can have a clear owning team.

The modular monolith captures many benefits cheaply: clear ownership, reduced team contention, enforced boundaries, a codebase that stays understandable, and still one transaction, one deploy, no network failures. It does not deliver independent deployment, independent scaling, or runtime fault isolation, so it does not remove those specific pains. But it has a very important second role: it is the best preparation for extraction. A module with a clean interface and its own tables can later be lifted out into a service with modest effort. A tangled monolith cannot.

Practical rule Make the monolith modular first. Many teams discover that this fixes most of their pain. Those who still need independent deploy or scaling for a specific module can extract exactly that module, with far less risk.

9. Migration strategies

Suppose you have decided to extract. How do you do it without a risky "big bang" rewrite, which historically fails more often than it succeeds (the old system keeps changing while you rebuild it, so you chase a moving target, and the new system must reach full parity before it provides any value)? The answer is incremental migration, using a set of complementary techniques.

9.1 The strangler fig pattern

Named by Martin Fowler after strangler fig vines that grow around a host tree and gradually replace it, the pattern works as follows. You put a routing layer (a facade, reverse proxy, or API gateway) in front of the monolith. Initially it forwards every request to the monolith. You build one new service for one capability. When it is ready, you change the routing so that requests for that capability go to the new service, and everything else still goes to the monolith. You repeat for the next capability. Over time the monolith shrinks, until it can be switched off or is left as a small core.

Details that make or break a strangler migration:

  • Routing rules should be dynamic. Route by path, header, tenant, or a percentage, controlled by configuration or a feature flag, so you can send 1% of traffic to the new service, watch, and ramp up, or switch back in seconds.
  • The facade must not become a new monolith. Keep it thin: routing, authentication, rate limiting. Business logic belongs in services.
  • Move behavior, not only code. Be sure that every caller of the old code (batch jobs, admin tools, internal modules) is also redirected, otherwise two implementations drift apart.
  • Finish the job. A strangler that stalls halfway leaves you with a monolith plus a few services, so more systems to operate and no end state. Plan to delete the old code once traffic is zero.

9.2 Branch by abstraction

The strangler fig works at the network edge, when the thing you are replacing is reachable by a request. But what if the functionality is deep inside the monolith, called by other internal code and never directly by a client? Branch by abstraction handles that. The steps:

  1. Introduce an interface (the abstraction) in front of the code you want to replace, and change all internal callers to use the interface instead of the concrete implementation.
  2. Keep the existing implementation behind the interface. Nothing has changed in behavior.
  3. Write a new implementation of the interface that calls the new service over the network.
  4. Use a flag to select which implementation is active, and shift traffic gradually, comparing results.
  5. When the new implementation is trusted, remove the old one and, optionally, the abstraction.

The "branch" is in the code, not in version control, so everyone keeps committing to the main line without long-lived branches and painful merges.

9.3 Parallel run and shadow traffic

How do you know the new service gives the same answers as the old code? Run both and compare, before trusting the new one with real effects.

  • Shadow (dark) traffic. The facade or abstraction sends a copy of each real request to the new service as well, but only the old system's response is returned to the user. The new service's response is discarded, or compared and logged. The new service must not cause side effects during this (do not charge a card twice), so shadowing suits reads or calls into a sandbox mode.
  • Parallel run. Both implementations process the same input, results are compared automatically, differences are investigated, and the old result is still authoritative. A well-known tool in this style is the "scientist" pattern: run the candidate and control, compare, report mismatches.
  • Canary release. Real traffic for a small fraction of users (or a few internal users first) is served by the new service. This carries real risk but only for that fraction.

The typical progression is shadow, then canary at 1%, 5%, 25%, 50%, then 100%, with automated checks on error rate and latency at each step.

9.4 Anti-corruption layer

The monolith's data model and vocabulary are often messy and historical: a "customer" may be a row in a table with forty columns and cryptic status codes. If your clean new service must speak that language directly, it gets polluted by it. An anti-corruption layer (ACL) is a translation layer between the new service and the legacy system. It converts legacy concepts into the new service's clean domain model and back, so the new design is not bent to match the old. The ACL can be an adapter inside the new service or a small separate component. It also acts as a place to concentrate all knowledge of the legacy quirks, so that when the legacy piece is finally removed, you delete the layer and nothing else changes.

9.5 Extract by seams

A seam (a term from Michael Feathers) is a place where you can change behavior without editing the surrounding code. Before extracting, look for natural seams in the monolith: an existing interface, a module boundary, a queue, an HTTP call, a database view, a package with few inbound dependencies. Good extraction candidates are code that already has a narrow, well-defined seam. A practical sequence is to first create the seam inside the monolith (make the module modular, route all access through a facade, remove cross-module joins), prove it works, and only then move the code behind it to a separate process. Tools that analyze dependency graphs and call frequency can show which modules are tightly knotted and which are loosely attached.

9.6 Data: extract last or first?

The hardest part of any extraction is the data, because the monolith's database usually has tables connected by foreign keys and joined everywhere. Two schools exist, plus a hybrid.

9.7 What to extract first

The order of extraction determines how quickly you learn and how much risk you take. Think of a 2-by-2 of value versus difficulty, and begin with something valuable but not too hard, to build the platform and the team's skills.

  • Edge and least coupled first. Pick a capability at the periphery of the dependency graph with few inbound calls and little shared data: notifications, email rendering, PDF generation, image processing, search indexing, reporting. A mistake here is cheap, and the first extraction exercises the pipeline, routing, and observability for the next ones.
  • High change rate. A module that changes every day and is constantly blocked by the release train pays back the cost of extraction fastest, because independent deployment helps most where change is frequent.
  • Scaling hotspots. A module that needs ten times the resources of the rest (search, media, recommendations) gives immediate infrastructure savings and performance isolation.
  • Compliance or reliability isolation. Payment processing, authentication, or anything whose failure or audit scope should be separated.
  • Defer the tangled core last. The central domain (orders, pricing, inventory) is usually the most entangled with everything. Leave it until you have experience, or perhaps never extract it at all. Many successful migrations end with a smaller but still substantial core monolith.

10. The distributed monolith anti-pattern

The worst outcome of a migration is not failing to reach microservices. It is reaching a state that has the costs of distribution and none of the benefits: a distributed monolith. The system is split into many services, but they are so tightly coupled that they behave like one application that happens to communicate over a network. You pay for the network failures, latency, and operations burden, and you still cannot deploy, scale, or reason about parts independently.

10.1 Symptoms

  • Lockstep deploys. Service A cannot be released without simultaneously releasing B and C, or releases are scheduled "together" to avoid breaking each other. Independent deployment, the core promise, is gone.
  • Shared database. Several services read and write the same tables. A schema change in one breaks the others. This is coupling through data, the strongest and least visible kind.
  • Chatty synchronous calls. A single user request fans out into dozens of synchronous calls, or long chains A to B to C to D. Latency stacks up and availability multiplies down: if each of five chained services is 99.9% available, the chain is about 99.5%.
  • Shared libraries that carry business logic or domain models. A common "core" library with the entity classes used by all services means any model change forces a rebuild and redeploy of everything. (Shared libraries for technical concerns such as logging or auth clients are fine, if versions are tolerant.)
  • Cascading failures. One slow service stalls callers, which exhaust threads and stall their callers, until the whole system is down.
  • Cross-team change. Most features need changes in three or four services owned by different teams, with coordination meetings. The service boundary does not match the unit of change.
  • Distributed transactions by habit. Two-phase commit or complex call sequences trying to restore monolithic ACID behavior across services.
  • Circular dependencies. A calls B calls A. Clear sign that boundaries are wrong.

10.2 Nano-services

A related failure is nano-services: splitting so finely that each service does almost nothing, for example one service per database table or per function. Each service pays the full fixed overhead (pipeline, monitoring, deployment, on-call) while delivering a tiny slice of logic, and any real use case needs a chain of calls across many of them. Tell-tale signs: services that cannot do anything useful without three others, teams owning ten or more services each, and "CRUD wrapper" services for single tables.

10.3 The granularity debate: macro versus micro

There is a real, ongoing disagreement about the right size. One camp advocates very small services (micro) for maximum independence. Another prefers "macro" or "right-sized" services: services aligned to a whole business capability or bounded context, often owned by one team and perhaps containing several modules and tables. Practitioners increasingly lean toward the second view, for several reasons: fewer network hops, fewer deployables to run, and transactions that stay local more often because related data stays together.

A workable principle: size by team ownership and cohesion, not by lines of code. A good boundary keeps things that change together in one place (high cohesion) and rarely requires cross-service coordination (low coupling). If you often change two services together, merge them. If one service has two clearly separate parts with different change rates and owners, split it. It is easier to split a service later than to merge badly split ones. Chapter 45 goes through the heuristics and boundary smells in detail.

GranularityBenefitCost
Too coarse (monolith-like services)Fewer calls, local transactionsContention returns, large blast radius, slow deploys
Right-sized (bounded context per team)Autonomy with manageable overheadNeeds domain insight to find the boundary

11. Reversals: companies moving back

Some well-known teams have publicly written about consolidating services back into larger ones or into monoliths. The usual pattern in these stories is not "microservices are bad" but "this particular workload, at this size, with this team, did not benefit". It is worth knowing the shape of those arguments, without leaning on specifics that you may misremember in an interview.

  • A video-monitoring workload described moving a pipeline built from serverless functions and queues back into a single process, because the cost of moving large data between components, and the orchestration limits, dominated. The components were tightly coupled in data flow and did not need independent scaling.
  • Early-stage companies have written about merging dozens of fine-grained services back into fewer, because a small team spent most of its time on operating overhead and cross-service debugging instead of building product.
  • A common pattern in postmortems: the split was done for an anticipated scale or team size that never arrived, or boundaries were drawn before the domain was understood and had to be redrawn many times at high cost.

The lessons are consistent: match the architecture to the team and the problem; avoid splitting parts that are tightly coupled in data volume and flow; treat decisions as reversible and measure the outcome; consolidating is a legitimate engineering decision, not a failure. In an interview, saying "I would revisit the split if the measured benefits did not materialize" shows maturity.

12. Migration risks, dual-running, rollback, and measuring success

12.1 The main risks

  • Wrong boundaries. The costliest mistake. Cut services along technical layers or guess at domains and you will be merging and re-splitting. Mitigation: modularize first, observe real change patterns and call graphs, start with obvious boundaries.
  • Data inconsistency during the move. Dual writes and replication can diverge. Mitigation: a single writer at any moment, change data capture rather than ad hoc dual writes, reconciliation jobs that compare both sides.
  • Performance regression. New network hops slow requests. Mitigation: measure latency before and after, set a latency budget per endpoint, cache, and batch calls.
  • Stalled migration. The team moves the easy parts and stops, leaving a hybrid forever. Mitigation: define the end state and the exit criteria, retire old code actively.
  • Feature freeze or parallel-change drag. The old and new code both need new features during the transition. Mitigation: freeze changes in the code being replaced, or change only through the shared interface.
  • Team overload. Building the platform, migrating, and delivering features at the same time. Mitigation: dedicated platform investment and a realistic roadmap.

12.2 Dual-running and rollback

Dual-running means that for some period both the old implementation and the new service are live and, in some form, kept consistent. Plan for it explicitly:

  • Who is the source of truth right now? Exactly one system owns writes for a given piece of data at any moment. The flip of ownership is a distinct, rehearsed step.
  • Sync in both directions during the transition. While the old system might still be rolled back to, changes in the new service's store may need to flow back so that rolling back does not lose data. This is the hardest part of reversibility.
  • Keep rollback cheap. Prefer a routing flag over a redeploy. Keep the old code path alive and exercised until the new one has run at 100% for a defined period (for example one or two full business cycles, such as a month-end close).
  • Expand, then contract. For schema moves, first add the new structure alongside the old (expand), migrate writers and readers, and only then remove the old (contract). Never combine the two steps in one release.
  • Define the point of no return. Know which step, once taken, cannot be undone cheaply, and put extra checks in front of it.

12.3 Measuring success

You should be able to say, with numbers, whether the migration helped. The standard set is the four DORA metrics from the DevOps Research and Assessment program, which correlate with software delivery performance:

DORA metricWhat it measuresWhat you hope to see after splitting
Deployment frequencyHow often you release to productionRises: from weekly or monthly to daily per service
Lead time for changesCommit to running in productionFalls: no release train, smaller pipelines
Change failure rateShare of deployments causing an incident or rollbackStays flat or falls: smaller changes, smaller blast radius
Time to restore service (MTTR)Time to recover from a production failureFalls, if observability is good; rises if it is not

Add supporting measures: p50 and p99 latency of key user journeys (should not regress), infrastructure cost per transaction, number of cross-team coordination meetings per release, merge-conflict rate, and developer satisfaction. If after a year deployment frequency has not improved and incidents are up, the migration is not working, which is a signal to fix the boundaries or stop.

Interview tip Mention that you would define success metrics before starting, capture baselines on the monolith (deploy frequency, lead time, change failure rate, MTTR), and review them after each extraction. This frames migration as an experiment with an exit, not a belief.

13. Worked example: extracting Payments from an e-commerce monolith

Let us walk through a realistic extraction. The company runs an e-commerce monolith: a single Java (or similar) application with modules for Catalog, Cart, Orders, Inventory, Payments, Customers, and Notifications, and one relational database. There are 60 engineers in 7 teams. Releases happen weekly and one in five needs a hotfix. Payments is a special case: it is subject to payment card compliance, is changed by a small specialist team, calls external payment providers that are slow and occasionally down, and a bug in it has twice blocked the whole release. Leadership wants Payments isolated. (The full decomposition of this system is the subject of the case study Decompose an E-commerce Monolith.)

13.1 Why Payments, and why now

It scores well on the criteria from section 9.7: compliance isolation (a smaller scope for audit), distinct reliability needs (external dependency with retries), a clear owner team, and a real pain (release blocking). Its coupling is moderate: Orders calls it, and it reads some order and customer data. The prerequisites are checked first: the pipeline is automated, tracing and central logging exist, a service template exists. So the decision passes the flow in section 6.

13.2 Phases

13.3 Phase 1: modularize in place

The payment code is scattered: Orders calls payment helper methods directly, Customer joins the payment methods table, and Reporting reads payment rows. The team moves all payment code into one module, with a single public interface such as authorize(orderId, amount, method), capture(paymentId), refund(paymentId, amount), and getStatus(orderId). Direct joins from other modules to payment tables are replaced by interface calls (Reporting uses a read-only view for now). An architecture test fails the build on any illegal import. Nothing is deployed differently and the risk is small, but this step typically takes the longest, perhaps two to three months, since it exposes the real dependencies.

13.4 Phase 2: abstraction and the new service

Orders now depends on a PaymentGateway interface. The existing module is the "old implementation". Meanwhile the payments team builds the Payments service from the service template: its own database, an HTTP/gRPC API with idempotency keys on every mutating call (so retries cannot double charge), and an anti-corruption layer that translates the monolith's legacy order and customer representations into the service's clean model. A "new implementation" of the interface calls the service. The flag is at 0%.

13.5 Phase 3: shadow and compare

Side effects are the danger in payments, so shadowing is done in the provider's sandbox mode. For each real authorization request the old path does the real call, while the new path sends a copy to the sandbox, and the results (approved, declined, error codes, amounts, currency rounding) are compared. Suppose one million authorizations are shadowed in two weeks and 0.4% mismatch. Investigation finds currency rounding differences for a zero-decimal currency and a legacy status code the ACL did not map. These are fixed and the mismatch falls below 0.01%.

13.6 Phase 4: canary and the data move

The data must move without losing money records. The sequence:

  1. Use change data capture from the monolith's payment tables to populate the service's database (historical backfill, then ongoing stream). The monolith is still the single writer.
  2. Verify with a reconciliation job that counts and sums (for example total captured amount per day) match between the two stores.
  3. Shift traffic with the flag: 1% of new payments for internal users, then 5%, 25%, 100% over two weeks, watching error rate, p99 latency, and the reconciliation report. For these payments the service is the writer, and changes are replicated back to the monolith's tables so that rollback is possible and Reporting still sees them.
  4. Reporting and Orders stop reading payment tables directly; they use the service's API and events.

Orders now publishes an OrderPlaced event or calls the service synchronously for authorization. Authorization is synchronous because the customer waits for the result, with a timeout, a retry limited by the idempotency key, and a circuit breaker. If the Payments service is down, checkout shows a clear "try again" message instead of hanging. Capture and refund follow a saga (Chapter 46): if capture fails after inventory was reserved, a compensating step releases the stock. Because payment events matter, they are published through an outbox so a service crash does not lose them.

13.7 Phase 5: retire

After 100% of traffic has run on the service for a full billing and refund cycle (about a month), with reconciliation clean, reverse sync is turned off. The old module, the old tables (after an archive), and the flag are deleted. The monolith is smaller and the "payments" release no longer blocks anyone.

14. Interview Q&A

Our monolith is slow to deploy and developers keep colliding. Should we move to microservices?

I would first diagnose which pain is real and whether a cheaper fix exists. Slow deploys might be addressed by faster pipelines, trunk-based development, and feature flags; collisions by a modular monolith with enforced boundaries and clear module ownership. If after that the organization still has many teams blocked on a shared release and clear, stable domain boundaries, I would extract incrementally with a strangler fig, starting with a loosely coupled, high-value capability. I would also check prerequisites (CI/CD, observability, a platform) and set DORA baselines so we can verify that the extraction actually helped.

What is a distributed monolith and how do you detect one?

It is a system split into separate deployables that remain tightly coupled, so it has the operational and failure costs of distribution without independent deployment or scaling. Symptoms include lockstep releases, several services sharing one database, long chains of synchronous calls, shared libraries holding domain models, circular dependencies, and features that always need changes in three or four services. You detect it by looking at deployment history (which services always ship together), the data-access map (who touches which tables), and the call graph from tracing. The fix is usually to merge services that change together, give each service its own data, and replace chatty sync calls with better-placed boundaries or events.

Explain the strangler fig pattern and how you would implement the routing.

Place a facade, such as an API gateway or reverse proxy, in front of the monolith, initially passing all traffic through. Build the new service for one capability, then route that capability's requests to it by path, header, tenant, or percentage, while everything else keeps going to the monolith. Because routing is configuration controlled by a feature flag, you can ramp from 1% to 100% and roll back in seconds. Repeat per capability, keep the facade thin, and delete the old code once traffic is zero. The risk to watch is stalling halfway and leaving a permanent hybrid.

How does branch by abstraction differ from the strangler fig, and when do you use each?

The strangler fig intercepts at the network edge, so it fits functionality that clients call directly through a request. Branch by abstraction works inside the code: you put an interface in front of the logic, make internal callers use it, add a new implementation that calls the new service, and switch between them with a flag. Use it when the capability is called by other internal modules rather than from outside, such as a payment or pricing library. The two often combine: the abstraction exists in the monolith while the facade handles external traffic.

Should you move the data first or the code first?

The robust approach separates the data logically first: put the tables behind one module, remove cross-module joins and foreign keys, and make all access go through an interface. Then move the code into a service, still potentially reading the same database for a short, explicitly temporary period, and finally move the physical data with change data capture and reconciliation. Moving code only and leaving a shared database indefinitely produces a distributed monolith. Moving data first is safer for boundaries but slower to show value, so teams usually interleave, with a hard exit date for any shared-database stage.

What does Conway's Law imply for a migration plan?

It says systems mirror the communication structure of the organization that builds them, so you cannot redesign architecture without addressing teams. The Inverse Conway Maneuver reorganizes teams first to match the target boundaries, giving each service one owning team with the skills to build and run it. If you cut code into services but leave three teams jointly owning one of them, the coordination cost comes back. A platform team that makes services cheap to create and run completes the picture, following the Team Topologies model.

What would you extract first, and why?

Something valuable but at the edge of the dependency graph with few inbound calls and little shared data, such as notifications, search indexing, or media processing. A mistake there is cheap and the first extraction forces you to build the routing, pipeline, tracing, and ownership practices that later extractions reuse. After that I would prioritize high-change-rate modules and scaling hotspots, then compliance-sensitive pieces such as payments, and leave the tangled domain core (orders, pricing, inventory) for last or keep it in a smaller monolith.

Why do some companies move back from microservices to a monolith, and what should we learn?

Reports usually show a mismatch between architecture and context: a small team drowning in operational overhead, boundaries drawn before the domain was understood, or components that exchange large amounts of data in tight loops so network and serialization costs dominated. The lesson is not that microservices are bad but that they are a trade that pays only at certain organizational scale and with clear, stable boundaries. Treat the architecture as reversible, measure outcomes, and be willing to consolidate services that always change together.

How do you keep a migration reversible, and what is the point of no return?

Use routing flags rather than redeploys so a traffic shift can be reversed in seconds. Keep the old code path alive and exercised, and sync data in both directions while rollback is still possible so no writes are lost. Use expand-then-contract for schema changes. The point of no return is deleting the old implementation and tables, so it should wait until the new service has served 100% of traffic for a full business cycle with clean reconciliation, and it should be a separate, deliberate step.

How do you know the migration is working?

Capture baselines for the four DORA metrics before starting (deployment frequency, lead time for changes, change failure rate, time to restore), then compare after each extraction. Pair them with user-facing latency percentiles, cost per transaction, and cross-team coordination load. A good result is higher deployment frequency and shorter lead time without worse failure rate or latency. If frequency and lead time do not improve, the boundaries or ownership are probably wrong, and we should stop and fix those before extracting more.

Is a modular monolith just a stepping stone, or can it be the end state?

It can be both. For many products with a handful of teams, a well-enforced modular monolith is the end state because it delivers clear ownership and manageable complexity with one deploy and real transactions. It is also the best preparation for extraction because a module with a narrow interface and its own tables can be lifted into a service with modest effort. What it cannot give you is independent deployment, independent scaling, or runtime isolation, so extract only the modules where those matter.

15. Recap

  • A microservice is defined by independent deployability, data ownership, business-capability alignment, and team ownership, not by size.
  • Monoliths win on simplicity, ACID transactions, cheap refactoring, low latency, and low operating cost. Their real pains are deploy coupling, long builds, scaling granularity, team contention, and blast radius.
  • Distribution costs: unreliable networks, latency and tail amplification, eventual consistency, operational overhead, harder testing and debugging, versioning skew, and higher spend. These are paid permanently, up front.
  • Split only for named, measured pain, with clear stable boundaries and CI/CD, observability, and a platform in place. Try cheaper fixes (modules, flags, faster builds) first.
  • Conway's Law means teams and services must be designed together. Use the Inverse Conway Maneuver, stream-aligned teams, a platform team, and two-pizza sizing to bound cognitive load.
  • The modular monolith (Chapter 39) is both a legitimate end state and the best preparation for extraction.
  • Migrate incrementally: strangler fig at the edge, branch by abstraction inside the code, shadow and parallel run to verify, an anti-corruption layer to protect the new model, and seams to choose what to cut. Separate data logically first and move physical data with CDC and reconciliation.
  • Extract edge, high-change, and hotspot capabilities first; leave the tangled core for last.
  • Avoid the distributed monolith (lockstep deploys, shared database, chatty calls, shared domain libraries) and nano-services. Size services by team and cohesion; right-sized beats tiny.
  • Plan dual-running, cheap rollback, and a deliberate point of no return. Prove success with DORA metrics against baselines, and be willing to consolidate.
