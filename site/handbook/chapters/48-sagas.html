Chapter 48 · Module 7 · Microservices in Depth

Sagas in Depth

A saga lets several services complete one business transaction without any distributed lock, by chaining local transactions and undoing them with compensating actions when something fails. This chapter goes far beyond the definition: both coordination styles with a full worked example, the saga state machine, how to design compensations that actually work, what happens when the compensation itself fails, the isolation problems sagas cause and how to contain them, and how to run, observe and test sagas in production.

1. Short recap and what this chapter adds

Chapter 31 explained why two-phase commit (2PC) is a poor fit for microservices: it holds locks across services while a coordinator decides, it blocks when the coordinator dies, and many modern stores and message brokers cannot take part in it at all. It introduced the saga as the usual alternative. In one paragraph: a saga is a business transaction split into a sequence of local transactions, each one an ordinary ACID transaction inside a single service's own database. When a local transaction commits, the saga moves to the next one. If a step fails, the saga runs compensating transactions for the steps that already committed, in reverse order, to semantically undo their effect.

Chapter 31 stopped there. This chapter is about everything an interviewer asks next: who decides what step comes next (choreography or orchestration), where the saga's progress is stored, what a compensation really is when the original action was sending an email, how you survive a crashed coordinator or a failing compensation, why concurrent sagas corrupt each other's data in subtle ways, and how you debug a saga that is stuck at step 4 of 7 at three in the morning. The data model that makes sagas necessary is in Chapter 46 (one database per service), the transport between steps is in Chapter 47, and event design is in Chapter 36.

Key idea A saga gives you atomicity of outcome (eventually either all steps took effect or all were compensated) but not isolation. Between T1 and the last step, other users and other sagas can see and act on the intermediate state. Almost every hard topic in this chapter is either a way to get the outcome guarantee reliably (retries, persistence, idempotency, outbox) or a way to live with the missing isolation (semantic locks, versions, commutative updates).

2. The running example: order, payment, inventory, shipping

We use one example through the first half of the chapter. An online shop has four services, each with its own database. The business transaction is place order:

  1. Order service records the order. It starts in state PENDING.
  2. Payment service authorizes and captures the customer's card for the order total.
  3. Inventory service reserves the purchased items so they cannot be sold twice.
  4. Shipping service books a courier pickup and creates a shipment.
  5. The order moves to CONFIRMED.

No single database transaction can cover these four databases. If payment succeeds and inventory then finds the item out of stock, the customer has been charged for something we cannot ship. We need either a full success or a clean undo. The compensations are: cancel the order, refund the payment, release the reserved stock, cancel the courier booking. The two ways to coordinate the steps follow.

3. Choreography: services react to each other's events

In choreography there is no central brain. Each service publishes an event when its local transaction commits, and other services subscribe to the events they care about and decide for themselves what to do. The saga exists only as an emergent chain of reactions, like dancers who each know their own steps and watch their neighbours. It rides on the event-driven machinery from Chapter 36 and the messaging patterns from Chapter 35.

3.2 What choreography does well and badly

Choreography has real strengths. There is no single point of failure and no extra component to run: the broker you already have is the only infrastructure. Services stay loosely coupled in time, since the publisher does not know or wait for subscribers, and adding a new participant (say, a loyalty-points service that listens to OrderConfirmed) needs no change to existing services. For short sagas with two to four steps and a linear flow it is the simplest thing that works.

Its weaknesses show as the flow grows. The business process is nowhere written down as one thing; to learn what "place order" does you must read four codebases and the subscription configuration. Services develop hidden coupling (each one must know the failure events of services further downstream, so Payment needs to know about ShipmentFailed). Cyclic dependencies appear easily: Order publishes to Payment, Payment listens to Order's cancellations, Inventory listens to both, and soon every service listens to every other. It is hard to answer "where is order 8841 right now?" without tracing across services, and hard to add a step in the middle without editing several subscribers.

4. Orchestration: a coordinator tells each service what to do

In orchestration one component, the orchestrator (also called the saga coordinator or process manager), owns the saga's logic. It sends a command to a participant ("capture payment for order 8841"), waits for a reply (success or failure), records the outcome, and then decides the next command. Participants stay simple: each exposes its local transaction and its compensation and knows nothing about the other participants or the saga's overall shape. The orchestrator is, in effect, a state machine (section 6) whose transitions are triggered by replies.

4.1 The same example, orchestrated

Now a PlaceOrderSaga lives inside the Order service (or in a dedicated orchestration service). It sends four commands, in order: CapturePayment to Payment, ReserveStock to Inventory, BookShipment to Shipping, and finally it locally marks the order confirmed. If a reply says failure, it sends compensating commands in reverse order: for a failure at shipping that is ReleaseStock to Inventory, then RefundPayment to Payment, then it marks the order cancelled.

4.2 Strengths and costs

Orchestration puts the whole process in one readable place. Adding a step is an edit to one class. Status is trivial: the orchestrator's persisted state is the saga status. Compensation order is explicit. Participants have no cyclic dependency on each other; they depend only on the orchestrator's command contract. Testing the process means testing one component with stubbed participants.

The costs are real. The orchestrator is a component you must run, scale and make highly available, and a poorly designed one becomes a god service that holds business rules belonging to the participants (section 21). Participants are called by command, so coupling in time is a bit tighter than in choreography. The coordinator must persist its state reliably, which is where most of the engineering effort goes.

6. The saga state machine and its persistence

An orchestrated saga is a state machine, and the most important engineering fact about it is that its state must be durable. The orchestrator process can crash after sending a command and before receiving the reply, or after receiving the reply and before sending the next command. When a new instance picks up, it must know exactly where the saga was. If the state lived only in memory, the saga would silently vanish, leaving a customer charged and no order.

6.1 The saga log

The standard design is a saga instance table plus a saga log (an append-only history). The instance row holds the saga's identity, type, overall state, a pointer to the current step, and its business payload. The log records every step transition with a timestamp. In a relational store it looks like this:

saga_instance
  saga_id        uuid  primary key        -- correlation id used on every message
  saga_type      text                     -- 'PlaceOrder'
  state          text                     -- RUNNING | COMPENSATING | COMPLETED | COMPENSATED | FAILED_NEEDS_OPS
  current_step   int
  payload        jsonb                    -- order id, amount, items, payment id once known
  version        bigint                   -- optimistic lock: only one worker advances it
  deadline_at    timestamptz              -- when the current step times out
  created_at, updated_at

saga_step
  saga_id, step_no, step_name, kind       -- kind: COMPENSATABLE | PIVOT | RETRIABLE
  status         text                     -- PENDING | SENT | SUCCEEDED | FAILED | COMPENSATING | COMPENSATED | COMP_FAILED
  attempts       int
  last_error     text
  updated_at

On every reply the orchestrator executes a single local transaction that (1) loads the saga with its version, (2) applies the transition (marks step SUCCEEDED, picks the next step), (3) writes the next outgoing command to an outbox table (section 13), and (4) bumps the version. Because transition and outgoing command commit atomically, a crash cannot lose a step or send a command whose state was not recorded. The version check means that if two workers wake up for the same saga (duplicate reply, timer racing a reply), only one wins.

6.2 Recovery

A background recovery job scans for sagas that are not terminal and whose deadline_at has passed or whose last update is older than expected. For each it looks at the current step's status. If the step is SENT and the reply never came, it resends the command (safe only because participants are idempotent, section 12) or asks the participant for the step's status. This single mechanism is what turns "process crashed" and "message lost" from disasters into delays.

7. Step types: compensatable, pivot, retriable

Not all steps are alike, and a well-designed saga orders them by type. Chris Richardson's classification, widely used, has three kinds.

  • Compensatable transactions can be undone by a compensating transaction. Create order (cancel it), reserve stock (release it), hold a hotel room (cancel the hold).
  • The pivot transaction is the go/no-go point. If it commits, the saga will run to the end and will not be compensated; if it fails, the saga compensates everything before it. It is typically the step that is hard or impossible to undo, such as capturing money that cannot be un-captured cleanly, or the point where the business has made an irrevocable commitment. There is at most one pivot.
  • Retriable transactions come after the pivot. They are guaranteed to eventually succeed if retried (they are idempotent and have no business reason to fail), so the saga never needs to compensate past the pivot. Examples: send the confirmation email, emit an invoice, update a search index, publish a notification.

This shapes the design rule: put steps that can fail for business reasons (validation, stock check, fraud check, credit limit) before the pivot, and steps that must not fail after it. If your saga has a step after the pivot that can legitimately fail (say, "customer's address is not deliverable"), you have misplaced the pivot or you need a business process to handle the exception, not a technical retry.

8. Designing compensations

8.1 Semantic undo, not rollback

A database rollback erases changes that nobody else saw. A compensation cannot do that: the original local transaction has already committed, and other transactions may have read its effects. So a compensation is a new forward transaction that restores the business meaning. Cancelling an order sets status = CANCELLED and keeps the row. Refunding a payment inserts a refund record and keeps the original charge on the statement. This is "semantic undo". The system's history shows both events, which is also what auditors want.

A compensation must be designed with the same care as the forward step: it needs its own idempotency, its own failure handling, and it must make sense in any state the forward step could have left things. For instance, "release stock" must be safe if the reservation was never created (the forward step failed halfway), safe if it was already released (duplicate message), and safe if the reservation has already been converted into a shipped item (in which case the saga design is wrong, see pivots).

8.3 Ordering steps: risky first, irreversible last

Because compensations are costly and sometimes imperfect, the cheapest way to improve a saga is to reorder it so that the steps most likely to fail run first and the steps hardest to undo run last. Checking stock (cheap to undo, fails often) belongs before capturing payment (expensive to undo, fails rarely). That is why section 7's pivot sits after stock reservation, and why many real designs use a pattern like "reserve, then authorize, then capture, then ship". Another technique is to use tentative or pending states as the forward step: instead of "charge the card" use "place a hold", with a later "confirm" step that is retriable and rarely fails. This is the TCC (Try-Confirm-Cancel) shape from Chapter 31, and in effect every saga with reservations is a TCC-flavoured saga.

Watch out In the choreography example earlier, payment was captured before stock was reserved. That order is acceptable for teaching, but in a real shop you would reserve stock first, because an out-of-stock rejection is common and a refund is expensive and slow. Reordering the steps changes which services must compensate and how often.

9. Timeouts and deadlines per step

In a distributed system a missing reply is ambiguous: the participant may be slow, may have crashed before acting, or may have acted and the reply was lost. A saga therefore needs a deadline per step, stored with the saga (deadline_at above) and enforced by a timer or the recovery scan. The deadline for a step should reflect its business meaning, not a default HTTP timeout. A payment authorization may deserve 10 seconds. A "warehouse confirms picking" step may deserve 4 hours. A human approval may deserve 3 days.

It is also worth a saga-wide deadline: "if the whole saga is not done in 15 minutes, abort and compensate". Without one, a saga stuck at step 3 can hold a semantic lock (section 15) on stock indefinitely. When a deadline fires, the orchestrator does not simply assume failure, because the late step may still complete. It either queries the participant ("what is the status of command X?") or, more commonly, sends the compensation. That is safe only if the compensation is idempotent and also handles the case of the forward step arriving after it (a "tombstone" or "cancelled" record so a late forward command is ignored).

10. Retries versus compensation

When a step does not succeed, there are three possible reactions, and the saga must choose correctly for each kind of failure.

Failure kindExamplesRight reaction
Transient technicalTimeout, connection reset, 503, broker redeliveryRetry with exponential backoff and jitter, bounded in time. The step is idempotent so retry is safe
Business rejectionCard declined, out of stock, credit limit exceeded, validation errorDo not retry. Start compensation; the answer will not change
Unknown outcomeTimeout where the participant may have committedRetry with the same idempotency key (so it is applied at most once), or query status first, then decide
Persistent technicalParticipant down for hours, bug in a participantRetry up to a budget, then alert. For steps before the pivot compensate if the business tolerates it; after the pivot keep retrying and page a human

The key discipline is classifying errors at the participant boundary. A participant should return a clear distinction between "I refused, and will always refuse this" and "I could not process this now". A generic "500 error" forces the orchestrator to guess. This is part of the contract design discussed in Chapter 47.

11. When the compensation itself fails

This is the question that separates textbook answers from production ones. The forward path can fail, so the backward path can too. The principle is: compensations must be designed so that they can be retried until they succeed. A saga that has started compensating has no safe way back, so it can only go forward to "fully compensated". Practical handling, in escalating order:

  1. Retry with backoff. Most compensation failures are transient (the payment provider is briefly down). Keep retrying, with a long ceiling on the interval, potentially for days. This is why compensations must be idempotent.
  2. Retry forever with alerting. There is no sensible "give up" for a refund. Set a time-based alert (for example after 15 minutes of failure) while the retries continue.
  3. Dead-letter and park. If a compensation keeps failing with a non-transient error (invalid account, refund window expired), move the saga to NEEDS_OPS and put the command in a dead-letter queue with the full saga context. Dead-letter queues are covered in Chapter 35.
  4. Manual intervention with ops tooling. Operators need a screen or CLI that shows the saga, its log, the failed step and the error, and lets them (a) retry the step, (b) mark a step as manually completed with a note ("refund issued through the bank console"), (c) skip it, or (d) force the saga to a terminal state. Every such action is audited. Without this tooling, the first stuck saga becomes an engineer running SQL against production.
  5. Reconciliation. A periodic job compares services' views (orders versus payments versus reservations) and flags drift that sagas missed. It is the safety net behind the whole design.
Key idea Treat compensation failure as an expected operational event with a runbook, not as an exception that "should never happen". The saga's design goal is that every inconsistency is eventually either resolved automatically or surfaced to a human with enough context to resolve it in minutes.

12. Idempotent steps and compensations

Messaging systems give at-least-once delivery (see Chapter 35), and orchestrators retry on timeout, so every command can arrive twice or more. An idempotent operation produces the same result when applied several times as when applied once. Without it, a duplicate CapturePayment charges the customer twice.

The standard technique is an idempotency key: the orchestrator generates a stable identifier per step (for example sagaId + ":" + stepNo) and sends it with the command. The participant keeps a table of processed keys. On receiving a command it does, in one local transaction: check whether the key exists; if so, return the stored result without acting; if not, perform the business change and insert the key and the result. A unique constraint on the key makes the check race-free.

-- Payment service, handling CapturePayment(sagaId, stepNo, orderId, amount)
BEGIN;
  INSERT INTO processed_command(key, result) VALUES ('s42:2', NULL)
    ON CONFLICT (key) DO NOTHING;
  -- if 0 rows inserted: duplicate, fetch and return stored result, COMMIT, stop
  INSERT INTO payment(order_id, amount, status) VALUES (:orderId, :amount, 'CAPTURED');
  UPDATE processed_command SET result = 'OK:pay-991' WHERE key = 's42:2';
COMMIT;

Compensations need the same treatment, plus one more subtlety: the compensation may arrive before the forward action, for example when a timeout fired and the orchestrator compensated while the original command was delayed in a queue. The participant must record "compensated for key X" so that when the late forward command arrives, it is rejected rather than executed. This is called a tombstone or "cancel-before-try" guard.

13. Reliable messaging: transactional outbox and inbox

A saga step has two actions that must happen together: update the local database, and send a message (an event or a command). Doing both naively creates the dual write problem. If the process commits the database change and then crashes before publishing, the next step never starts. If it publishes first and the database commit fails, the next step runs for a change that never happened. Chapter 31 introduced the fix; here is how it works in saga context.

With the transactional outbox, the service writes the business change and a row in an outbox table in one local transaction. A separate relay (a poller or a change-data-capture reader, see Chapter 36) reads unsent outbox rows and publishes them to the broker, marking them sent. The message is therefore published at least once and only if the change committed. With the inbox on the receiving side, the consumer records each incoming message id in a table within the same transaction as its business change, so a redelivered message is detected and ignored. Outbox plus inbox yields effectively-once processing from at-least-once transport.

14. The isolation problem

ACID transactions give isolation: concurrent transactions behave as if they ran one after another. A saga gives up this property (the "I" in ACID) because each local transaction commits immediately and its effects are visible to everyone before the saga finishes. Sagas are therefore ACD: atomic in outcome, consistent, durable, but not isolated. Three classic anomalies result, all caused by the window between T1 committing and the saga finishing or compensating.

  • Lost update. Saga A reads a value, saga B changes it and commits, and then A (or A's compensation) overwrites it without seeing B's change. Example: saga A reserved stock and later compensates by setting available = 10 (the value it remembers), wiping out a sale that saga B made in between.
  • Dirty read. Saga B reads data that saga A wrote, and A is later compensated. Example: A tentatively raises a customer's loyalty tier, B grants a discount because of the tier, then A fails and the tier is reverted. B acted on data that, in business terms, never really existed.
  • Non-repeatable (fuzzy) read. A saga reads the same data twice at different steps and gets different answers because another saga changed it in between. Example: step 1 reads the item price as 50, step 4 reads 55; the invoice is inconsistent with the charge.

16. Concurrency of sagas, and sagas versus 2PC

Beyond data anomalies, there are concurrency issues in the saga machinery itself. Many sagas run at once, thousands per second, so the orchestrator's database must be sharded or partitioned by saga_id. Each saga instance must be advanced by one worker at a time, enforced by the version check of section 6 or by routing all messages for a saga to a single partition (partition key = saga id, as in Chapter 35). Two sagas touching the same entity, for example two orders for the last unit, are serialized by the participant's own local transaction on that entity, which is the right place for the invariant ("available never negative") to be enforced. Two different sagas affecting one customer's credit limit are the kind of cross-saga conflict that needs a semantic lock or commutative updates.

The honest summary for an interview: sagas trade isolation for availability and autonomy. If your business cannot tolerate any intermediate state (moving money between two ledgers that must always balance at every instant), keep both rows in one database or use a stricter protocol. If it can (placing orders, booking travel), a saga is almost always the right choice.

17. Long-running sagas and human steps

Sagas are not limited to millisecond API calls. A loan application saga might run for days: credit check, document upload, manual underwriter approval, contract signing. Such sagas introduce new requirements.

  • No thread or connection is held. Between steps the saga is just a row in the database (or a workflow history in an engine). Waiting costs storage, not compute.
  • Human tasks are modelled as a step that sends a command to a task system ("approve loan 551") and then waits for a callback or event. The deadline is large and has a business escalation: after 2 days remind the approver, after 5 days escalate to a manager, after 10 days auto-reject and compensate.
  • Timers must be durable: a delayed message or a persisted deadline, not an in-process sleep (see Chapter 38).
  • Versioning. A saga started under version 1 of the process may still be running when version 2 is deployed. The orchestrator must keep the old logic for in-flight instances, or migrate them deliberately.
  • Semantic locks become business reservations with an expiry. A held seat or reserved credit must have a time-to-live, otherwise abandoned sagas lock resources forever.
  • Cancellation by the user is a normal event, not an error: a "cancel" signal arriving mid-saga should move it into the compensating state at the next safe point.

18. Saga observability

An orchestrated saga is easy to observe because its state is stored; a choreographed one is hard because it is spread over services. In both cases, good practice is the same. Full distributed tracing and log aggregation are covered in Chapter 52; here are the saga-specific pieces.

  1. Correlation id. The saga id is generated at the start and attached to every command, event, log line and trace span. A single search for sagaId=s42 across the log system then returns the entire story. In choreography, where no component owns an id, the first service must mint one and every service must propagate it in the event envelope.
  2. Status API. Expose GET /sagas/{id} returning state, current step, per-step status and attempts, timestamps, and the last error. Customer support and product screens ("your order is being processed") use it. With choreography you build this by running a small read model that subscribes to all saga-related events and folds them into a per-saga status record, an application of the CQRS idea from Chapter 36.
  3. Metrics. Count sagas started, completed, compensated and needs-ops, per saga type. Histogram the end-to-end duration and the per-step duration. Gauge the number of sagas currently in each state and, critically, the age of the oldest non-terminal saga.
  4. Dashboards and alerts. A dashboard with success rate, compensation rate (a rising compensation rate means an upstream problem such as an inventory bug), step latency, retry counts and the stuck list. Alert on: any saga in NEEDS_OPS, any saga older than its deadline plus a margin, and a compensation rate above baseline.
  5. Trace spans. Wrap the whole saga in a parent span and each step in a child span so that the waterfall shows where time went, including waits between steps.

19. Testing sagas

Sagas are bug-prone because most code paths run only when something goes wrong. A disciplined test pyramid:

  • Unit tests of the state machine. With an orchestrator this is easy: feed in a sequence of replies and assert the next commands. Test every failure position: for an n-step saga, inject a failure at step k for each k and assert the correct compensations in the correct order.
  • Idempotency tests. Deliver each command and each reply twice, and out of order, and assert the final state is unchanged. Deliver a compensation before its forward command.
  • Crash tests. Kill the orchestrator between "command sent" and "reply processed", restart, and assert the saga completes. Property-based tests that randomly interleave messages, delays and crashes find the bugs humans do not imagine.
  • Contract tests. Each participant publishes the commands, replies and events it supports and tests against them, so a change to PaymentFailed does not silently break the orchestrator or the choreographed subscribers (see Chapter 47).
  • End-to-end tests with a few full-environment scenarios: happy path, one failure with compensation, one timeout. Keep them few because they are slow and flaky.
  • Chaos and game days in a staging environment: drop messages, delay replies, take a participant down, and check that recovery and ops tooling behave.

20. Workflow engines for orchestration

Hand-writing an orchestrator means reimplementing persistence, timers, retries, versioning and visibility. Durable workflow engines provide these. They are covered as general tools in Chapter 38; here is how they fit sagas.

  • Temporal (and its ancestor Cadence) lets you write the saga as ordinary code in a general-purpose language. The engine records every step result in a durable event history; if the worker crashes, another worker replays the history to rebuild the exact state and continue. Activities (the calls to services) have configurable retry policies and timeouts. The common saga idiom is to push a compensation onto a list after each successful step and, in a catch block, run the list in reverse. Long sleeps and human-approval signals are first-class.
  • AWS Step Functions expresses the saga as a state machine in a declarative JSON-based language (Amazon States Language). Each state can call a Lambda or an AWS service, has built-in Retry and Catch clauses with backoff, and a Catch can route to compensation states. Standard workflows can run for up to a year, and "wait for callback" tasks support human steps.
  • Others include Azure Durable Functions, Camunda and Zeebe (BPMN-based), Netflix Conductor, and in the Java world the Eventuate Tram Sagas and Axon libraries.

The tradeoffs: an engine removes the hardest infrastructure work and gives a ready-made UI and history, but adds an operational dependency (a Temporal cluster, or vendor lock-in with Step Functions), a programming model with constraints (Temporal workflow code must be deterministic), and cost per state transition on managed offerings. For a small number of simple sagas a library or a hand-rolled table may be lighter; for many sagas, long timers or human tasks, an engine is usually the right call.

21. Anti-patterns

  • The distributed monolith via orchestrator. The orchestrator grows to contain business rules (pricing, eligibility, discount logic) that belong to participants, and participants become thin CRUD wrappers. Every feature change touches the orchestrator, and teams can no longer deploy independently. Keep the orchestrator to sequencing and state; keep decisions in the owning service.
  • Chatty sagas. A saga with fifteen steps and a round trip per field. Latency, failure probability and compensation count grow with step count. Redraw service boundaries so that one service can complete a coherent unit of work in one local transaction; the number of steps is a design smell indicating wrong boundaries (see Chapter 46).
  • Hidden coupling in choreography. Services quietly depend on each other's event ordering and failure events, with no document describing the saga. A change in one service breaks the flow somewhere else. Cure: document the flow, add a status read model, and consider moving to orchestration.
  • A saga for something that should be one transaction. If two entities always change together and are owned by one team, put them in one service and database.
  • Compensations that are not tested or not idempotent. The most common production failure.
  • Sharing a database to "simplify" the saga. Defeats service autonomy and reintroduces the coupling the saga was meant to avoid.
  • Synchronous chains disguised as a saga. Service A calls B, which calls C, with no persisted state. A crash in the middle leaves no record and no compensation. A saga needs persisted progress.
  • Using exceptions for business outcomes. Treating "card declined" as an infrastructure failure retried 10 times.

22. Second worked example: travel booking

A trip booking combines a flight, a hotel and a rental car, each with a different external provider. The customer wants all three or none. This example has a different shape from the order saga: the participants are third parties with their own cancellation rules, and the steps take seconds to minutes.

Design details that make this realistic. The flight hold has a provider-side expiry, so the saga deadline must be shorter than the hold (for example 10 minutes of a 15-minute hold), or the hold must be extended. Hotel and car providers differ in cancellation rules, so the saga picks refundable rates while the saga is open and converts to a non-refundable discount rate only after the pivot, if desired. The ticketing step is retriable: if the airline API is down, the saga keeps retrying and the customer sees "booking confirmed, tickets being issued". If ticketing fails permanently (fare no longer valid), the saga cannot compensate past the pivot, so it opens a manual exception case and a customer-service agent resolves it, which is the honest final answer for post-pivot failure. Idempotency keys are passed to each provider where supported (sagaId:step); for providers that do not accept keys, the saga first queries "booking by our reference" before retrying.

23. Interview Q&A

What is a saga, and why not just use 2PC?

A saga splits a business transaction into local transactions, one per service, each committing independently, with a compensating transaction for each step so that a later failure can semantically undo the earlier work. 2PC would need every service to support a prepare phase and to hold locks until a coordinator decides, which hurts availability, is blocked by a coordinator crash and is unsupported by many brokers and NoSQL stores. The price of choosing a saga is the loss of isolation, which I must handle in the application with semantic locks and similar techniques.

Choreography or orchestration: how do you decide?

For short, linear flows of up to about four steps where other services also want the events, choreography is simple and needs no extra component. As the flow grows, branches, needs timeouts or must be audited, the logic scattered across subscribers becomes unmanageable and cyclic dependencies appear, so I move to orchestration where one component owns the sequence and the compensation order and the saga status is just its state. I would also factor in team ownership: orchestration needs one team to own the end-to-end flow.

What do you do if a compensating transaction fails?

Compensations must be designed to be retried until they succeed, so first they are idempotent and retried with backoff, potentially for days, with alerting. If the failure is non-transient (for example an expired refund window), the saga moves to a needs-ops state, the command goes to a dead-letter queue with full context, and an operator uses an admin tool to retry, mark manually completed or skip with an audit trail. A reconciliation job behind it detects any drift that slipped through.

How do you make saga steps idempotent?

The orchestrator generates a deterministic idempotency key per step, such as saga id plus step number, and sends it with every attempt. The participant, in the same local transaction as its business change, inserts the key into a processed-commands table with a unique constraint, and on a duplicate it returns the stored result without acting. Compensations use the same mechanism, plus a tombstone so a compensation that arrives before its forward command makes the late forward command a no-op.

What is the dual write problem and how does the outbox solve it?

A step must update its database and publish a message, but a database and a broker cannot be committed atomically, so a crash between the two either loses the message or publishes one for a change that did not commit. With the transactional outbox, the service writes the business change and the outgoing message to an outbox table in one local transaction, and a relay or change-data-capture process publishes it afterwards at least once. The receiver's inbox table deduplicates, which gives effectively-once processing.

What isolation anomalies do sagas have and how do you mitigate them?

Because each local transaction commits early, other sagas can see intermediate data, producing lost updates, dirty reads and non-repeatable reads. Mitigations include semantic locks (a PENDING flag that others respect), commutative updates (increment and decrement instead of assigning absolute values), a pessimistic view (reduce available stock first so readers never over-sell), rereading the value before overwriting, version records to order operations, and choosing a stronger mechanism by business value for high-risk cases. The choice depends on how bad the anomaly is for the business.

What is the pivot transaction?

The pivot is the step that marks the point of no return: before it, failures lead to compensation; after it, the saga must finish, so the remaining steps are retriable and are retried until they succeed. It usually is the step that is hardest to undo, such as capturing payment or issuing a ticket. Good design places failure-prone, cheap-to-undo steps before the pivot and only steps that cannot legitimately fail after it.

How would you observe and debug a saga stuck in production?

Every message and log line carries the saga id, so I search for it across services and the tracing system. An orchestrated saga also has a persisted record, exposed through a status API, showing the current step, attempts and last error. A dashboard shows the count of sagas by state and the age of the oldest non-terminal saga, with alerts on needs-ops and past-deadline sagas, and the ops tool can retry or force the step. For choreography I would build a read model from the events to get the same view.

When would you use a workflow engine such as Temporal or Step Functions?

When there are many sagas, long timers, human approval steps, or strong audit needs, because the engine supplies durable state, retries, timeouts, versioning and a visibility UI that are expensive to build correctly. Temporal runs ordinary code with replay from a stored history, while Step Functions uses a declarative state machine with Retry and Catch for compensation. The costs are an operational dependency or vendor lock-in and a constrained programming model, so for a tiny saga a simple table-driven orchestrator may be better.

You must send a confirmation email in a saga. Where does it go and what is its compensation?

An email cannot be recalled, so it should not be a compensatable step at all. I make it a retriable step after the pivot so it is sent only when the saga is guaranteed to succeed, and I do not compensate it. If the saga can still fail afterwards, I would send a separate cancellation email as the compensating action rather than trying to unsend the first. Delaying the email until the end is the cheapest design.

24. Recap

Recap

  • A saga is a chain of local transactions with compensations in reverse order; it provides atomic outcome but no isolation.
  • Choreography uses events only: simple and decoupled for short flows, but the process is implicit, status is hard and cyclic dependencies grow. Orchestration uses a coordinator state machine: explicit flow, easy status and testing, at the cost of running a durable coordinator and avoiding a god service.
  • Persist saga state and a step log in a transaction with the next outgoing command; recover stuck sagas with deadlines and a recovery scan.
  • Classify steps as compensatable, pivot or retriable; put risky steps first and irreversible ones after the pivot.
  • Compensations are semantic undo as new transactions; some things (email, physical actions) cannot be undone and must be placed after the pivot.
  • Give every step a deadline; retry transient failures, compensate on business rejection, use idempotency keys when the outcome is unknown.
  • Compensation must be retried until it succeeds; escalate to dead-letter, ops tooling and reconciliation.
  • Use outbox and inbox plus idempotent handlers for effectively-once messaging (Chapter 31, Chapter 35).
  • Handle missing isolation with semantic locks, commutative updates, pessimistic view, reread, version records and by-value choice.
  • Observe with saga ids, a status API, state counts and oldest-age alerts (Chapter 52); test every failure position, duplicates and crashes; consider Temporal or Step Functions (Chapter 38).
