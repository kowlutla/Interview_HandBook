Case study 14 · Worked solutions

Design a Payment System

A payment system moves money, and money has a property most data does not: doing something twice, or losing track of whether you did it, is a real financial and legal problem. This worked solution designs a payment platform that charges cards through an external provider, never double-charges, never loses a cent in its books, and can prove every number to an auditor.

How to approach this problem (read this first)

What the interviewer is really testing. A payment system is not a throughput problem. The page's own estimate is about 1,200 payments per second at peak, which a few database shards handle easily. The three hard parts are all about correctness. First, never charging twice when clients retry, workers crash and webhooks repeat. Second, never losing track of money when the outside payment provider times out and you do not know whether the charge happened. Third, keeping books you can prove, which means an append-only ledger and a reconciliation process. A candidate who talks mostly about scaling has missed the point.

The thinking order, step by step

  1. Fix what you are building. Ask whether you are the card network or a service that calls a provider (PSP), which payment methods exist, and whether charges are authorize then capture. Say: "I will not build the card network; I integrate with a PSP and my job is correctness around an unreliable external system." You go first here because it removes a huge scope and shows where the difficulty lives.
  2. State the non-negotiables before any number. Say: "No double charge, no lost payment, a ledger that always balances; I will trade availability for these." These three sentences are the grading rubric, and every later choice is justified against them.
  3. Estimate, and notice what is small. 10 million payments per day is about 116 per second, about 1,200 at peak with a 10 times factor. Say: "Throughput is modest; storage and retention are what grow." This tells you a sharded relational database is enough and frees your time for correctness.
  4. Draft the API around the idempotency key. Show the create call with a required key header, amounts as integers in minor units, and a status that can honestly be "processing". Say: "Every call that moves money carries a client key, so a retry is safe." The key is the first safety device, so it belongs in the first API you draw.
  5. Pick the store from the guarantee you need. The idempotency promise is literally a unique index, so choose a relational database with transactions. Say: "I need uniqueness and multi-row atomicity, which a document store would make me rebuild."
  6. Show the simple design and break it four ways. One service, one database, a synchronous PSP call. It breaks on crash after charge, client retry, a transaction held open across a network call, and out-of-order webhooks. Each break becomes a component: state machine, outbox, inbox, workers.
  7. Deep dive in order of risk. Idempotency across every hop, then the state machine and outbox, then the ledger, then the unknown outcome after a timeout, then reconciliation. Say: "Each boundary that can repeat a message gets its own key."
  8. Close with failures, security and evolution. Walk what happens when the PSP is slow, a webhook is lost or forged, and the database primary fails. Mention PCI scope reduction through tokens, and phasing.

The decision points

  • Relational or NoSQL? Ask: do I need unique constraints and atomic multi-row writes? Idempotency and the ledger both do, so relational.
  • Distributed transaction or saga? Ask: can every participant take part in two-phase commit? The PSP cannot, so use a saga with compensations plus an outbox.
  • Store balances or derive them? Ask: do I need history and audit? Yes, so append-only entries are the truth and balances are a cached view.
  • On a timeout, fail or wait? Ask: could the money have moved? Yes, so the answer is "unknown", resolved by asking the PSP with the same key, never by guessing.
  • Handle card data or not? Ask: what does touching it cost me? A large audit scope, so use tokens and hosted fields.

Common mistakes on this problem

  • Spending the time on scale. Fix: compute 1,200 per second early, say it is small, and move on to correctness.
  • Check-then-insert for idempotency. Fix: use a database unique index so two concurrent retries cannot both pass.
  • Treating a timeout as a failure. Fix: call it unknown, record the attempt before the call, retry with the same PSP key.
  • Holding a database transaction open across the PSP call. Fix: commit intent plus an outbox row, then let workers make the call.
  • Updating a balance column as the truth. Fix: append balanced ledger entries and derive balances.
  • Using floating point for money. Fix: integers in minor units with an explicit currency code.
Interview tip Say "exactly once does not exist across a network; I build at-least-once delivery plus idempotent effects" within your first ten minutes. It names the real problem and the real solution in one line, and everything you draw afterwards becomes a consequence of it.

1. Problem statement and clarifying questions

The prompt is usually one line: "Design a payment system for an e-commerce platform." That is far too open, so the first minutes go to narrowing it. The framework in Chapter 1 says to ask, then state your assumptions out loud. Here are the questions worth asking and the answers this page assumes.

QuestionAssumed answer
Are we building the card network, or a system that talks to one?The latter. We are a payment service inside a marketplace or merchant platform. We call an external PSP (payment service provider, such as Stripe, Adyen, Braintree). The PSP talks to the card networks (Visa, Mastercard) and the banks. We do not build an acquiring bank.
Who are the users?Shoppers paying merchants, and merchants (our internal order service, or sellers) who get paid out.
Which payment methods?Cards first, plus a redirect-style method (bank transfer / wallet) to show the asynchronous pattern. Cash, crypto, and cheques are out.
Auth then capture, or one-step charge?Both. Physical goods are usually authorized at checkout and captured at shipment. Digital goods often capture immediately.
Refunds, partial capture, chargebacks?Refunds and partial captures yes. Chargebacks (a bank forcibly reverses a payment after a customer dispute) we ingest and record, but dispute handling workflow is a stretch goal.
Currencies?Multiple. A payment has one currency; our books can hold balances in many currencies.
Scale?10 million successful payments per day, about 116 per second on average, with peaks at 10 times that during flash sales.
Correctness versus availability?Correctness wins. A short "try again in a minute" is acceptable; a double charge or an unbalanced ledger is not.
Do we store card numbers?No. We want to stay out of the heaviest compliance scope by never touching raw card data (section 10).
Interview tip Say the sentence "I will not build the card network; I integrate with a PSP and my job is to be correct around an unreliable, asynchronous external dependency." It tells the interviewer you understand where the real difficulty lives. In payments it is rarely throughput. It is correctness under retries, timeouts and duplicate messages.

2. Requirements

2.1 Functional requirements

  • Create a payment for an order: amount, currency, payment method token, merchant.
  • Authorize (reserve funds on the shopper's card), capture (actually take them, fully or partly), void (release an authorization), and refund (return captured money, fully or partly).
  • Receive asynchronous results from the PSP through webhooks (HTTP callbacks the PSP sends to us), because many outcomes (3-D Secure, bank transfers, disputes) are not known when the first call returns.
  • Maintain a ledger: an append-only, double-entry record of where every unit of money is.
  • Reconcile our records against the PSP's settlement files and the bank statement, and surface differences.
  • Pay out to merchants (the payout variant, section 11.2) and support stored-value wallets.
  • Query status: shoppers, merchants and support staff can read the current and historical state of any payment.

2.2 Non-functional requirements

  • No double charge. The same customer intent must produce at most one money movement, even with client retries, network timeouts, duplicate webhooks and crashing workers. This is the headline requirement.
  • No lost payment. If the shopper was charged, we must eventually know and fulfill, or refund.
  • Ledger integrity. Every transaction's debits equal its credits. Ledger rows are never updated or deleted.
  • Durability: committed payment state survives any single node or zone loss (zero recovery point objective for the ledger).
  • Availability: 99.95% for creating payments; status reads 99.99%. Degrade by rejecting or queueing, never by guessing.
  • Latency: the checkout call returns in under 2 seconds at p99 when the PSP is healthy. The PSP is the slow part (typically 300 ms to 1.5 s).
  • Auditability and security: every state change is attributable and retained for years (often 7 or more, depending on jurisdiction). Stay out of PCI card-data scope.

2.3 Out of scope

Building acquiring or issuing, card network routing, tax calculation, accounting-system general ledger beyond our own payment ledger, full fraud model training (we define only the hook), subscriptions and dunning (retrying failed recurring charges), and regulatory filing. We mention each where it touches the design.

3. Estimation

Payments are a modest-throughput, high-value-per-request workload. Do the arithmetic anyway: it shows which numbers matter (storage and retention, not QPS).

3.1 Traffic

  • 10,000,000 successful payments per day. A day has 86,400 seconds, so the average is 10,000,000 ÷ 86,400 ≈ 116 payments per second.
  • Peak factor 10 (flash sale, billing-day clusters): about 1,200 payments per second. Also count failed and abandoned attempts; assume 1.3 attempts per success, so the intake API sees roughly 1,500 create calls per second at peak.
  • Each payment drives about 4 PSP calls on average (create or authorize, capture, maybe a status poll, a share of refunds), so peak outbound PSP traffic is around 5,000 calls per second. This is the number to check against PSP rate limits.
  • Webhooks: about 3 per payment (authorized, captured, settled or updated), so 30 million per day, 350 per second average and about 3,500 per second at peak.
  • Status reads: shoppers and merchants poll or load order pages. Assume 5 reads per payment, 50 million reads per day, 580 per second average, so read traffic is modest and is cacheable for terminal states only.

The ledger is the data we can never lose and never rewrite, so it is replicated synchronously (section 8). Old events and webhook logs move to object storage (Chapter 21) after 90 days.

3.3 Money in flight

Average order value of 40 dollars means about 400 million dollars per day in volume, or 146 billion per year. At that size a one-in-a-million error rate still loses or misplaces about 400 dollars daily, which is why we design for provable invariants rather than "usually right" and run reconciliation (section 7.5) every day.

4. API design

Payments APIs follow one rule above all: every call that changes money requires a client-supplied idempotency key, a unique string identifying the intent. Chapter 32 covers the mechanism; Chapter 5 covers REST design generally. Our API:

  Body:     { "order_id": "o_981", "merchant_id": "m_42",

                                      payment.failed, refund.succeeded (via event bus)

Several choices deserve a sentence. Amounts are integers in minor units (4999 means 49.99 USD), never floating point, because floating point cannot represent 0.10 exactly and rounding drift is unacceptable with money. The currency's exponent matters: JPY has 0 decimal places, KWD has 3. Responses expose a status, not a guarantee: processing is a legal, honest answer when the PSP has not answered yet, and the client learns the final outcome by webhook-driven event or by reading the payment. The server generates the payment id, as a time-sortable unique id (Chapter 32), so ids are not guessable and index well.

5. Data model and storage choice

5.1 Why a relational database

Payments need multi-row atomic transactions, unique constraints (the idempotency guarantee is literally a unique index), strong consistency, and ad hoc audit queries. That is the textbook case for a relational database with ACID transactions (Chapter 18), sharded when it outgrows one primary (Chapter 25). A key-value or document store would force us to rebuild uniqueness and atomicity in application code. We use eventually consistent stores only for derived, rebuildable views such as search indexes and dashboards.

Sharding choice: shard payments, events, idempotency records, and outbox by merchant_id hashed (or by payment_id) so a payment's rows live together and one transaction stays local to one shard. Ledger entries shard with their payment's shard; merchant-level balances are derived by summing, with periodic balance snapshots. Cross-shard atomicity is deliberately avoided: it is the very problem that forces two-phase commit (Chapter 31), and we replace it with sagas.

6. First design, then the scaled architecture

6.1 The simple version

Start with a single service and a single database. The checkout calls POST /v1/payments. The service inserts a payment row in state created, calls the PSP synchronously, updates the row to the result, writes ledger entries, and replies. It is a perfectly good design for a small shop. It has four defects that the rest of this page repairs.

  1. If the service crashes after the PSP charged the card but before it updated the row, the shopper was charged and we do not know. (Unknown outcome, section 7.4.)
  2. If the client times out and retries, the shopper may be charged twice. (Idempotency, section 7.1.)
  3. Holding a database transaction open across a slow network call to the PSP ties up connections and locks. (Never do that; use the outbox and a state machine.)
  4. PSP webhooks arrive out of order and duplicated, and nothing applies them safely. (State machine plus inbox.)

6.2 The scaled architecture

Walk the boxes once. The Payment API is stateless, validates the request, and enforces the idempotency key. The risk hook is called synchronously before authorization. The orchestrator is the only component that changes payment state, and it does so through a strict state machine (7.2). It never calls the PSP inside a database transaction; it records intent plus an outbox row, commits, and the PSP workers perform the network call. The webhook receiver persists the raw event, acknowledges it, and feeds the orchestrator. The ledger is written atomically with the state change that moves money. Reconciliation is the independent check that all of the above, plus the PSP, agree.

  1. The client sends the create request with an idempotency key. The API starts one database transaction that inserts the key row (unique) and the payment row in state created. If the key already exists, the API returns the stored result instead (7.1).
  2. The orchestrator calls the risk hook (section 10.3). If risk declines, the payment ends in failed with no PSP call.
  3. The orchestrator moves the state to authorizing and inserts an outbox row in the same transaction. This commits our intent before anything leaves our system.
  4. The relay publishes the job; a PSP worker calls the PSP with a PSP-level idempotency key derived from our payment id and operation. If the call or response is lost, the worker may repeat it safely.
  5. The result moves the payment to authorized, requires_action (3-D Secure: the shopper must authenticate with their bank), declined or, if the outcome is not known, stays in authorizing pending resolution (7.4).
  6. On capture, the same pattern repeats. When the capture is confirmed (response or webhook), the orchestrator, in one database transaction, sets the state to captured, appends the ledger transaction, and writes an outbox event.

7. Deep dives

7.1 Idempotency keys end to end

Idempotent means that performing the same operation many times has the same effect as performing it once. Networks do not guarantee that a request is delivered exactly once: a timeout tells the sender nothing about whether the receiver acted. So the sender must retry, and the receiver must tolerate retries. The only way to do both is to give each intent a unique identifier that is repeated on every retry of that intent. Chapter 32 explains the principle; here is the application to payments, link by link.

Walkthrough. The top row is a chain of five boxes: client, Payment API, orchestrator, PSP worker and PSP, joined by arrows left to right. The four green boxes below name the layer of protection at each kind of boundary. Read it in order. 1. The client creates key K1 once per intent and reuses it on every retry. 2. The API holds a unique index on merchant plus K1, so a repeat returns the stored answer, and the same key with a different body is rejected. 3. The orchestrator stores attempt id A1 before calling out. 4. The worker sends the PSP a key K2 derived from stable ids, so a retried call looks identical to the PSP. 5. Internal consumers and the webhook inbox each keep their own dedupe record. The design uses a separate key at each hop because each hop can repeat a message independently, and one check at the front door cannot protect the later ones. On a whiteboard, draw the chain first and write a key under every arrow. If layer 2 is removed, a timeout retry can charge twice. If layer 4 is removed, a repeated webhook can book the ledger twice.

Layer 1: the client-facing key. The client generates a key per business intent (for example a UUID created when the shopper clicks "Pay", reused on every automatic retry, or derived from the order id and attempt number). The API stores the key with a hash of the request body. The decision table:

SituationBehaviour
Key never seenInsert key row with in_progress and a short lock, do the work, then store the response and mark done, all committed with the payment row.
Key seen, done, same request hashReturn the exact stored status code and body. No new side effects.
Key seen, in_progress (concurrent duplicate)Return 409 "request in progress" with a retry hint, or wait briefly. Never start a second execution.
Key seen, different request hashReject with 422: the key was reused for a different intent, which is a client bug.
Key seen, in_progress but lock expired (crash)Resume from the recorded state; the state machine and PSP key make resumption safe, not restart-from-scratch.

The uniqueness comes from a database unique index, not from "check then insert" in code, because two concurrent retries would both pass a check. The loser of the insert race gets a constraint violation and takes the "in progress" path. Keys are scoped per merchant (so one tenant cannot collide with another) and retained for a window such as 24 hours to 7 days; past that, the business-level unique constraint (merchant, order) is the backstop.

Layer 2: the PSP key. Most PSPs accept their own idempotency header. We derive it deterministically from stable identifiers (for example the payment id plus the operation name plus the attempt counter), and we store it in psp_attempts before the call. The critical consequence is that a retry after a timeout is the same request from the PSP's point of view, so a charge that actually succeeded is returned rather than repeated. If a PSP did not support idempotency keys, we would have to fall back to "query by our reference before retrying", which is slower and has a race, so provider choice should weigh this feature heavily.

Layers 3 and 4: internal and inbound. Message buses give at-least-once delivery, so the same event can arrive twice (Chapter 34, Chapter 35). Consumers record processed event ids in the same transaction as their effect, or make the effect itself a unique-constrained insert (a ledger transaction keyed by payment, kind and step). Webhooks from the PSP are deduplicated by the provider's event id in the inbox table before they touch the state machine.

Key idea "Exactly once" processing does not exist across a network. What we build is at-least-once delivery plus idempotent effects, which together produce exactly-once effects: the money moves once even if messages arrive many times.

7.2 The payment state machine, sagas and the outbox

Payment progress is modelled as an explicit finite state machine. A state machine turns "what should happen when a webhook arrives while we are retrying a capture?" into a table lookup, and it lets us reject impossible transitions (a refund of something never captured) instead of silently corrupting data.

Walkthrough. Each pill is a state. Blue is in progress, amber is waiting or uncertain, green is a good outcome, and red is a failure or end. Follow the main line. 1. created is where the payment starts. 2. authorizing means the PSP call is out. 3. requires_action means the shopper must pass a bank challenge. 4. authorized means funds are reserved. 5. From authorized, a down arrow leads to capturing, then captured, and a left arrow leads to refunding and then refunded. 6. A right arrow from authorized leads to voided. The red arrow from authorizing leads to failed. The dashed arrows go to unknown or review when a PSP call has no clear answer. The picture is drawn as a table of allowed moves so a late webhook that would go backwards can be recognised and ignored. Point at unknown, because it is the honest answer after a timeout. Without it candidates are forced to guess success or failure. Without compare-and-set on the version, two actors could both move the same payment.

Transitions are applied with compare-and-set on a version column: UPDATE payments SET status = 'captured', version = version + 1 WHERE payment_id = ? AND version = ? AND status = 'capturing'. If zero rows change, another actor already moved the payment, and the caller re-reads and decides. Because a transition table says which target states are allowed from which source states, a late or duplicate webhook that would move a payment "backwards" is recorded as an event but ignored for state purposes. Ordering of webhooks is not guaranteed (a captured notice can arrive before the response to our capture call), which is why we trust the state machine and the PSP's own timestamps or sequence numbers, not arrival order (Chapter 27).

The outbox: making "update state and emit message" atomic

When a state change must also trigger a message (publish "payment.captured" to the order service, or enqueue a PSP job), there is a classic trap: if we write the database and then publish to the bus, a crash between the two loses the message; if we publish first and then write, a crash produces a message about something that did not happen. We cannot make a database and a message broker commit together without a distributed transaction. The transactional outbox pattern avoids that: insert the message into an outbox table in the same database transaction as the state change. A separate relay process reads unpublished outbox rows (by polling, or by change-data capture of the log) and publishes them, marking them done afterward. The relay may publish a message twice (crash after publish, before marking), so consumers must be idempotent; that is already guaranteed by layer 3 above. See Chapter 36 for the pattern in general.

Walkthrough. From left to right: the orchestrator, a large green box that stands for one Payments DB transaction, the relay, and the event bus. Inside the green box are two small green boxes: the state update and the outbox insert. 1. The orchestrator handles an event, such as a PSP result. 2. It opens one database transaction and does two writes inside it, updating the payment state and inserting an outbox row describing the event. 3. Both writes commit together or neither does. 4. The relay, running on its own, polls the outbox or reads the change log. 5. It publishes the row to the bus, then marks it published. The reason it is drawn this way is that no database can commit together with a message broker, so we turn that problem into a single-database commit and move the publish to a separate step. The cost is a possible duplicate publish, which consumers must tolerate. Point at the box boundary. If the outbox insert were outside the transaction, a crash would lose messages. If the relay is removed, events sit unpublished.

Sagas for multi-step money flows

A checkout spans services that own separate databases: inventory, payment, shipping, loyalty points. A saga (Chapter 48, Chapter 31) breaks the business operation into local transactions, each with a compensating action that undoes it semantically if a later step fails. Order placement is an orchestrated saga: reserve inventory, authorize payment, confirm order; if authorization fails, release the inventory (compensation); if shipping later fails, void or refund. Compensation is not rollback: a captured payment cannot be un-captured, only refunded, and the refund is a new ledger transaction. The payment orchestrator is itself a small saga with the PSP: "authorize" has the compensation "void", "capture" has "refund". Every step and compensation must be idempotent, because a saga coordinator that crashes will re-issue steps on recovery.

7.3 Double-entry ledger and immutability

The payments table says what state a payment is in. The ledger says where the money is. They are separate on purpose: the payment row is mutable operational state; the ledger is the financial truth and may only be appended to.

Double-entry bookkeeping is a 700-year-old technique with a property software people should love: every money event is recorded as two or more entries, at least one debit and one credit, whose amounts sum to zero. Money is never created or destroyed in the books, only moved between accounts. If the sum across the entire ledger is ever not zero, there is a bug, and we can detect it with a single query.

Walkthrough. On the left is a small table with three accounts and two columns, debit and credit. On the right, a green box lists the ledger rules and an amber box shows a later refund. Read the table row by row. 1. PSP receivable is debited 100.00, because we are owed that money. 2. Merchant payable is credited 97.00, because we owe the seller. 3. Platform revenue is credited 3.00, our commission. 4. Debits of 100.00 equal credits of 97.00 plus 3.00, so the transaction balances. The right side says why this works: every transaction sums to zero, rows are insert-only, and a mistake is fixed by a new reversing transaction. The amber box shows a 30.00 refund as a new pair of entries while the original rows stay untouched. It is drawn as a table because interviewers want to see that you can write real entries. Point at the sum. If debits and credits were allowed to differ, money could appear from nowhere. If rows could be edited, an auditor could not replay history.

A wrinkle worth spotting: the example is simplified. The PSP fee is usually known only from the settlement file, so it is booked then as its own balanced transaction (debit PSP fee expense, credit PSP receivable) rather than at capture time. State that nuance briefly if asked; do not get lost in accounting.

Why immutability

  • Audit: an auditor can replay the ledger from the first row and arrive at every balance. If rows could be edited, the answer to "what did the balance look like on March 3rd" would be unknowable.
  • Concurrency: inserts do not conflict with each other the way updates to a hot balance row do. Balance computation (sum of entries, or last snapshot plus later entries) is a read.
  • Debuggability and security: an attacker or buggy job with write access cannot quietly rewrite history; at most it adds entries, which are visible.
  • Replication: an append-only log is simple to ship to a warehouse and to verify with checksums or a hash chain (each row includes the hash of the previous, so tampering is detectable).

Account balances are derived, not stored as the source of truth. For fast reads we keep a cached running balance per account, updated in the same transaction as the entries, plus a daily snapshot. A nightly job recomputes balances from entries and compares to the cache; a mismatch pages someone. Preventing overdraft (for wallets, section 11.2) needs a locking decision: lock the account's balance row for the transaction (SELECT ... FOR UPDATE) or use a conditional update WHERE balance >= amount. Hot accounts (the merchant payable of a huge seller, or the platform's own revenue account) can bottleneck on that lock, which is why we split them into sub-accounts or shard them and sum, covered in section 8.

7.4 Retries and unknown-outcome handling

The hardest practical problem: we call the PSP to capture 100 dollars, the connection times out, and we got no answer. Did the charge happen? We do not know. This is the "unknown outcome" and it is not rare: at 5,000 PSP calls per second, a 0.1% timeout rate is about 5 per second at peak.

Walkthrough. This is a sequence diagram with three lifelines: the PSP worker, the PSP and our database. The red dashed arrow is the failure. Read it in order. 1. The worker writes attempt A1 to our database before doing anything else. 2. It sends the capture to the PSP with key K2. 3. The reply never arrives, so the call times out. 4. The worker marks A1 unknown, and the payment stays in capturing. 5. After a backoff with jitter, it sends the same capture with the same K2. 6. The PSP either replays the original result, or a status lookup by our reference says captured or not found. 7. The worker records the resolution and writes the ledger. If it stays unknown after the retry budget, it moves to a review state. The design records first and asks later because a crash during the timeout would otherwise erase the fact that a call was made. Point at step 1. Remove it and a dead worker forgets. Reuse a new key at step 5 and you risk a second charge.

The rules, in order of importance:

  1. Write intent before the call, result after. The psp_attempts row exists before the network call. If our worker dies mid-call, a recovery sweeper finds attempts that are old and unresolved and drives them to resolution. Without this record a crash simply forgets the call happened.
  2. Never treat a timeout as a failure. A timeout means unknown. Declaring failure and releasing the order while the capture actually succeeded produces a charged shopper with no order. Declaring success without proof produces an order with no money.
  3. Resolve by asking, not guessing. Retry with the same PSP idempotency key (the PSP replays its result), or query the PSP for the payment by our reference. Poll with exponential backoff and jitter (Chapter 40, Chapter 51) so a PSP brownout is not made worse by a retry storm. Add a circuit breaker per PSP.
  4. Distinguish error classes. A hard decline ("insufficient funds") is final: do not retry. A validation error (400) is a bug: do not retry. A 5xx, a timeout, or a connection reset is retryable. A 429 means slow down. Retrying a decline just annoys the issuer and can trigger fraud flags.
  5. Bound the uncertainty. If still unknown after the retry budget (say, 5 attempts over 10 minutes), move to unknown / review, hold the order in a "payment pending" state toward the shopper (never "failed"), and let webhook arrival or the next reconciliation resolve it. Authorizations that were actually granted but never captured expire on their own at the issuer, usually after 7 days or so, which is a safety net.
  6. Webhooks are the second channel. Even when the synchronous response is lost, the PSP typically still sends a webhook. Handling both channels through the same idempotent state machine means whichever arrives first resolves it and the other is a no-op.
Watch out Retrying at several layers multiplies: if the client retries 3 times, the API retries the PSP 3 times, and the PSP worker retries 3 times, one click can become 27 calls. Put the retry budget at one layer (the worker owning the PSP call), keep the rest to a single attempt, and cap total elapsed time.

7.5 Reconciliation

Even a perfect design disagrees with reality sometimes: a webhook was lost, the PSP changed a fee, a bank returned a transfer, an engineer fixed something by hand. Reconciliation is the independent process that compares three views of the same money and flags every difference. It is what turns "we believe" into "we can prove".

Walkthrough. Read left to right. Three sources on the left (PSP settlement file, bank statement, our ledger and payments) feed an ingest box, then a matcher, and the matcher splits into three outcomes on the right. 1. Each source arrives in its own format. 2. The ingest step parses them and converts to one format with integer minor units and one id scheme. 3. The matcher first joins on the PSP reference, then falls back to amount plus date. 4. Items that agree go to the green matched box and are closed. 5. Items that disagree go to the red exceptions box, labelled missing, amount, status, duplicate or extra. 6. The amber box fixes them with an adjusting ledger transaction or sends them to a person. The picture uses three sources because the PSP file alone could be wrong, and the bank is the final proof of cash. If the matcher is removed, nobody finds a lost webhook. If fixes edited rows instead of adding entries, the audit trail would break.

The exception types tell you which bug occurred. In PSP file, not in ours: we missed a webhook or crashed after calling; create or complete the payment record. In ours, not in PSP file: we recorded a capture that did not happen (or it settles tomorrow); investigate before refunding. Amount mismatch: partial captures, currency conversion differences, or fee changes; book an adjusting ledger transaction. Duplicate in PSP file: a real double charge that escaped all earlier defences; refund automatically and raise an incident. Fixes are always new ledger transactions that reference the exception, never edits, so reconciliation itself leaves an audit trail. The bank statement is matched against PSP payout totals (a batch payout of 18,432.10 USD equals the sum of that day's captured minus refunds minus fees), the last link in the chain to real cash.

8.1 Consistency choices

Inside a shard we get serializable-enough guarantees: the payment state change, the ledger transactions and the outbox row commit atomically. Across services we accept eventual consistency with explicit states: the order service learns about a capture through an event, and between capture and event the order says "payment pending". Across the PSP we have no transaction at all, just idempotent calls plus reconciliation. Using the vocabulary of Chapter 28, the system is CP for money (when the primary shard is unreachable we refuse to guess and return 503) and AP-ish only for derived read models.

Two-phase commit between our database and the PSP is impossible (the PSP does not participate), and 2PC between our own services would couple their availability and hold locks across slow calls. Hence sagas and outbox, not distributed transactions. This is the single most common "why not just use a transaction?" probe, and the answer is that the thing you must coordinate with is outside your trust and control boundary.

8.2 Multi-region

Payments are rarely active-active across regions for the same payment: concurrent writes to the same ledger from two regions invite conflicts that cannot be merged ("both regions spent the last 10 dollars"). The usual design is one home region per merchant or per shard (active-passive per shard, with other regions as standby using synchronous or near-synchronous replication, Chapter 26, Chapter 41), global routing to the home region, and failover drills. Reads and shoppers' checkout pages can be served locally; the money write goes home. Data residency laws also pin some merchants' data to a region.

8.3 Multi-currency

Rules that avoid costly mistakes: store every amount as an integer in minor units with an explicit ISO currency code, and never add amounts of different currencies. A ledger transaction balances per currency. A currency conversion is its own transaction pair: debit the USD account, credit a "FX clearing" USD account, debit "FX clearing" EUR, credit the EUR account, with the rate, its source and timestamp recorded, so FX gain and loss is visible as the clearing account's balance. Quote the shopper a rate and lock it for the authorization. Rounding follows a documented policy (typically half-even on the converted amount, with remainder cents assigned deterministically). Settlement currency can differ from charge currency, so reconciliation compares converted amounts with a tolerance and flags drift beyond it.

10. Security, PCI, fraud, monitoring and cost

10.1 PCI scope and tokenization

PCI DSS (the Payment Card Industry Data Security Standard) is the rulebook for anyone who stores, processes or transmits card data. The more of your systems touch the card number (the PAN, primary account number), the larger the audit scope, the cost and the blast radius of a breach. The central design move is to keep raw card data out of our systems entirely.

  • Hosted fields or redirect: the PSP renders the card input (in an iframe or via its JavaScript fields), so the PAN goes from the shopper's browser straight to the PSP. Our servers never see it.
  • Tokenization: the PSP returns an opaque token (tok_9f3...), which stands for the card but is useless to a thief. We store and send tokens only. Reusable tokens enable "one click" repurchase and subscriptions.
  • We may keep the last four digits, brand, expiry month and year, and the issuer country for display. We never store the CVV after authorization (it is prohibited), and we keep PANs out of logs, traces, error reports and analytics (a classic accidental leak).
  • If we truly must store PANs (to avoid PSP lock-in, say), that happens in a small, isolated card vault service on its own network segment, with envelope encryption using keys held in an HSM or KMS, strict access logging and its own audit (Chapter 55). The rest of the system still sees only tokens.

Other controls: TLS 1.2 or higher everywhere (Chapter 3), mutual TLS or signed requests to PSPs, secrets in a vault with rotation, least-privilege database roles (the application role has no UPDATE or DELETE on ledger tables), separate roles for support staff with field masking, and encryption at rest. Privacy law interplay (data retention versus the right to erasure) is handled by deleting personal fields while keeping the financial record, which has a legal retention requirement (Chapter 56).

10.2 Strong customer authentication

Many regions require 3-D Secure (SCA): the bank challenges the shopper (an app approval, a one-time code). That is why the state machine has requires_action: the API returns a next_action with a redirect or client secret, the shopper authenticates, and the PSP calls a webhook with the final result. The flow is asynchronous by nature and may never complete (shopper closes the tab), so a timeout moves the payment to failed after, say, 30 minutes, and any late success webhook is handled idempotently by voiding or by completing the order if still valid.

10.3 Fraud-check hook

Fraud detection is its own system; we define the contract. Before authorization, the orchestrator sends the risk service a compact feature set (amount, currency, merchant, hashed card token, device id, IP, shopper account age, velocity counters such as attempts per card in the last hour). The service returns allow, deny, or review (manual or step-up authentication) within a latency budget of about 100 ms, with a timeout policy (section 9). The decision, its score and the model version are stored on the payment for audit and for later disputes. After the fact, chargebacks and confirmed-fraud labels flow back as training data. Cheap, strong signals we enforce ourselves: per-card and per-IP rate limits, velocity limits on small test charges ("card testing" attacks), and blocklists.

10.4 Abuse, audit and monitoring

  • Audit: every state transition and every manual action (who, what, when, why) goes to payment_events and an append-only audit log shipped to write-once storage. Changes to ledger-affecting configuration require two-person approval.
  • SLIs and SLOs (Chapter 54): create-payment success rate excluding genuine declines (target 99.95%); p99 create latency (under 2 s, with the PSP call as the dominant span); webhook processing lag (p99 under 30 s); outbox lag (under 5 s); stuck-payment count (payments in a non-terminal state longer than 15 minutes, target zero); reconciliation auto-match rate above 99.9%; ledger imbalance count, which must be zero always.
  • Alerts that matter: authorization approval-rate drop per PSP or per issuer BIN range (a sudden fall means a PSP or issuer problem, not "users got poorer"), spike in unknown outcomes, duplicate-key conflicts with differing bodies, and any ledger check failure. Distributed tracing carries the payment id and idempotency key through every hop (Chapter 52).

10.5 Cost

The dominant cost is not infrastructure: PSP fees are typically around 2 to 3 percent plus a fixed amount per transaction, so on 400 million dollars per day of volume the fee line is in the order of 10 million dollars daily, dwarfing servers and databases (a few tens of shards cost perhaps tens of thousands per month). Engineering effort goes where money is: better approval rates (smart retries on soft declines, network tokens, routing to the PSP with the best rate per card type) are worth far more than saving a database. Archive old webhook logs and events to cold object storage; keep the ledger hot for the reporting window.

11. Evolution, variants and alternatives

11.1 Phasing: what to build first

  1. Phase 1 (weeks): one PSP, synchronous create, idempotency table, payment state machine, webhook inbox, basic ledger, manual reconciliation. Correctness features first, because retrofitting idempotency is painful.
  2. Phase 2: outbox and event bus, orchestrated saga with the order service, automated daily reconciliation with exception queue, risk hook, refunds and partial captures.
  3. Phase 3: sharding, second PSP and routing, multi-currency, payouts, dispute workflow, intraday reconciliation, region failover drills.
  4. At 10x scale (100 million per day, 12,000 per second peak): more shards (go from, say, 16 to 128; plan consistent hashing or a directory so resharding is a data move, Chapter 25), a dedicated ledger service with its own sharding, Kafka partitions scaled to match, PSP connection pooling and multiple PSP accounts to spread rate limits, a streaming reconciliation pipeline (Chapter 37) instead of daily batch, and a cellular layout (Chapter 42) so one cell's failure affects a fraction of merchants.

11.2 Wallet and payout variant

Marketplaces need to move money out (pay sellers) and keep stored value (a wallet balance a user can spend). The ledger design above extends directly.

Walkthrough. The boxes in the middle column are ledger accounts: platform cash, wallet liability and merchant payable. Purple boxes are outside parties, and the blue box is the payout job. Follow the three flows. 1. Top-up: the shopper's card pays through the PSP, platform cash is debited and wallet liability is credited, because we now owe the user that balance. 2. Spend: the arrow to wallet spend moves value from the wallet to the merchant as one internal transfer that checks balance is at least the amount. 3. Sale: a card payment goes straight to merchant payable. 4. Payout: the payout job takes merchant payable, sends a bank transfer to the seller bank, and books it as paid when the bank confirms. Every arrow is a pair of balanced entries. The picture shows that a wallet needs no new machinery, only more accounts. Point at the difference between internal arrows, which are atomic, and the bank arrow, which is external and irreversible. Without a reserve in payouts, refunds can exceed what is left.

Differences from card capture: wallet spends are internal, so they are a single-database ledger transaction with a conditional balance check (balance >= amount) and are truly atomic with no external uncertainty, which makes them far simpler than card payments. Payouts are external and irreversible: a bank transfer sent to the wrong account is hard to claw back. Controls: payouts run from a scheduled job over a snapshot of merchant payable (minus a reserve for refunds and chargebacks), each payout is a ledger transaction moving money from "merchant payable" to "payout in transit" and then to "cash" on bank confirmation, each has a unique payout id as its idempotency key sent to the bank API, and large or unusual payouts need a second approval. A returned transfer (wrong IBAN) arrives in a bank file and becomes a reversing transaction. Compliance (KYC, sanctions screening, limits) gates the beginning of the flow.

12. Interview Q&A

How do you guarantee a customer is never charged twice?

Layered idempotency. The client sends a unique idempotency key per payment intent and the API enforces a unique index on (merchant, key), returning the stored response to any repeat. Our worker then calls the PSP with a deterministic PSP-level key, so a retry after a timeout is the same request and the PSP returns the original result instead of charging again. Inbound webhooks are deduplicated by provider event id, and ledger transactions are unique per payment, kind and step. Finally, daily reconciliation against PSP files catches any double charge that escaped, and we refund it automatically. No single layer is trusted alone.

The PSP call timed out. What do you do?

Treat it as unknown, not failed. The attempt was recorded before the call, so we retry with the same PSP idempotency key after exponential backoff with jitter, or query the PSP for the payment by our reference. Either returns the real outcome. Meanwhile the payment stays in capturing and the shopper sees "processing". If the retry budget is exhausted we move to an unknown/review state and let the webhook or reconciliation resolve it. We never release the order or mark it paid without proof, because both guesses lose money in one direction or the other.

Why not use a distributed transaction across the payment, order and inventory services?

Two-phase commit needs every participant to support prepare and commit and to hold locks while waiting for the coordinator, which couples availability and hurts throughput. The PSP, the most important participant, does not support it at all. So we use an orchestrated saga: each step is a local transaction with a compensating action (release inventory, void authorization, refund a capture), every step is idempotent, and the orchestrator persists its progress so it can resume after a crash. We accept brief intermediate states, which the state machine makes explicit and visible.

Why a double-entry ledger instead of just updating balances?

Double entry makes every transaction self-checking: debits equal credits, so money cannot appear or vanish without a visible imbalance. Because entries are append-only, we can reconstruct any balance at any past moment, which auditors require, and inserts do not suffer the lost-update and lock problems of updating a hot balance row. Corrections are reversing transactions, so history shows both the mistake and the fix. Balances are a derived, cacheable view that a nightly job recomputes from entries to detect drift.

How do you handle webhooks that arrive twice, late, or out of order?

Persist the raw event to an inbox table with a unique key of provider and event id, and acknowledge quickly with a 2xx so the PSP stops retrying; processing happens asynchronously. Duplicates hit the unique constraint and are dropped. For ordering, the payment state machine only allows forward transitions and applies them with compare-and-set on a version, so a late "authorized" after "captured" is recorded but does not regress state. For high-stakes transitions we re-fetch the payment from the PSP API rather than trust the payload, which also defeats forged webhooks. Signature verification and a timestamp window are mandatory.

How do you make the database update and the event publication atomic?

With the transactional outbox. The state change and an outbox row are written in one local database transaction, so they commit or fail together. A relay process reads unpublished rows, by polling or change-data capture, publishes to the bus, and marks them sent. A crash between publish and mark causes a duplicate publish, never a loss, and consumers are idempotent through processed-event tables. This gives at-least-once delivery with effectively exactly-once effects without needing a transaction spanning the database and the broker.

What does reconciliation do and why is it needed if everything is idempotent?

Idempotency prevents the known failure modes, but systems also fail in unanticipated ways: a lost webhook nobody retried, a PSP bug, a manual fix, a changed fee, a bank return. Reconciliation is the independent control that compares our ledger with the PSP settlement files and the bank statement, matching on the PSP reference and then amount and date. Matches close; differences become exceptions classified as missing, extra, amount mismatch or duplicate, and are fixed with new adjusting ledger entries. It also yields a measurable match rate that serves as a health metric of the whole system.

How do you keep the system out of PCI scope?

We never let card numbers reach our servers. The browser or app sends the card straight to the PSP through hosted fields or a redirect, and the PSP returns a token that we store and use for all later operations. We keep only non-sensitive attributes like brand and last four digits, never store the CVV, and scrub card data from logs. This reduces our compliance obligation to the simplest category, though we still protect secrets, access and transport. If a vault is required, we isolate it as a small, separately audited service so the rest of the platform stays tokens-only.

How would you scale ledger writes for a very hot account, such as the platform's revenue account?

Appending entries is cheap; the contention comes from maintaining a single running balance row that every transaction must lock. We split the hot account into N sub-accounts (for example by payment id modulo 16) so writes spread across rows and shards, and the logical balance is the sum of the sub-accounts, read from a periodic snapshot plus later entries. For accounts that need overdraft protection, such as wallets, we keep a single row per user, which is rarely hot, and use a conditional update. Revenue-type accounts have no overdraft check, so they can be sharded freely.

What do you do when the fraud service is down?

It is a policy decision made ahead of time and recorded in configuration rather than an outage-time debate. A common rule is to fail open for small amounts with tighter velocity limits applied locally, and fail closed or route to review above a threshold, because the cost of a false decline on a 5 dollar order is far less than approving a 5,000 dollar fraudulent one. Whatever happens, the payment records that the risk check was skipped and why, so the decision is auditable and those payments can be re-scored when the service returns.

How would you support multiple currencies without rounding errors?

All amounts are integers in minor units with an ISO currency code, and the ledger balances per currency; mixing currencies in one entry is rejected. A conversion is an explicit pair of transactions through FX clearing accounts that records the rate, its source and timestamp, so gain or loss is visible. We lock the quoted rate for the life of the authorization, use a documented rounding mode (often half-even) with deterministic remainder handling, and let reconciliation tolerate small converted-amount differences while flagging larger drift. Currencies with different exponents, such as JPY with none, are handled through a currency table, never by assuming two decimals.

Recap

  • We integrate with a PSP; the hard part is correctness around an unreliable, asynchronous external dependency, not throughput (about 116 payments per second average, 1,200 at peak).
  • Idempotency keys are layered: client to API, worker to PSP, bus consumers, webhooks and ledger, each with its own uniqueness constraint. At-least-once delivery plus idempotent effects gives exactly-once effects.
  • A strict state machine with compare-and-set transitions makes retries, duplicate and out-of-order webhooks safe; "unknown" is a real state.
  • Never hold a database transaction across a PSP call. Record intent first, use the transactional outbox for messaging, and sagas with compensations across services.
  • The ledger is double-entry and append-only: transactions balance per currency, corrections are reversing entries, balances are derived and verified nightly.
  • A timeout means unknown: retry with the same PSP key, query by reference, bound the retries, and let webhooks and reconciliation settle the rest.
  • Reconciliation of ledger against PSP files and bank statements is the final control and a health metric.
  • Stay out of PCI scope with hosted fields and tokens; hook fraud checks before authorization with a clear fail-open or fail-closed policy; shard by merchant or payment id so the critical transaction is local; wallets and payouts reuse the same ledger.
