Case study 24 · Worked solutions

Design a Hotel Booking System

A traveller searches for a double room in Lisbon from the 12th to the 15th of June, picks one, and pays. This worked solution shows how to model inventory by date, how to hold a room while the guest pays, why a hotel may deliberately sell more rooms than it has, how to keep search fast without lying too much about availability, and how to take payment safely.

How to approach this problem (read this first)

What the interviewer is really testing. There are three hard parts. First, a stay covers several nights, so you must book all nights or none, and the model of "what is free" decides how easy that is. Second, the business rules are unusual: rooms of one type are interchangeable, the hotel may sell more rooms than it has, and a guest holds a room for a few minutes while paying. Third, search traffic is about 500 times booking traffic, so you must keep a fast approximate search path apart from a slow exact booking path.

The thinking order

  1. Clarify what is sold. Say: "Is the guest buying a room type for a range of nights, with the room number assigned later?" This comes first because the answer (a count per type per night) shapes the whole data model.
  2. Ask about the rules that bend inventory. Say: "Can the hotel overbook, is there a hold while the guest pays, and can other sites sell the same rooms?" These three answers are what make this problem different from ticket booking, so settle them before any numbers.
  3. Estimate the two halves separately. Say: "Bookings are about 90 per second at peak, but searches are about 30,000 per second." Estimate the look-to-book ratio first, because it tells you where the load is and that the exact path is small.
  4. Draft the hold call first. Say: "The key API is a hold on a room type and a date range, with an idempotency key and a price token." It is the hardest call, and search can be described as a read afterwards.
  5. Pick the entity that drives the model. Say: "One inventory row per room type per night, with total, overbook limit, booked and held." Then shard by property so one stay stays on one shard.
  6. Show the simple design and break it. Say: "One database with SELECT FOR UPDATE works, until 30,000 searches per second scan the same tables." The first bottleneck is search, not booking, so you split search out.
  7. Deep dive the atomic claim. Say: "A conditional update per night, and I check that rows updated equals nights." This is the part the interviewer will probe, so do it while there is time left.
  8. Cover holds, overbooking and payment. Say: "A hold is a row with a TTL released by a sweeper, and checkout is a saga with idempotency keys." Money comes after inventory because you reserve before you charge.
  9. Finish with failures and evolution. Say: "The cache may be stale, but the hold step is the authority." End on what you would build first.

Decision points

  • Counter per night or row per physical room? Ask: do we need overbooking and interchangeable rooms? Yes, so counters.
  • Conditional update, SELECT FOR UPDATE or a version column? Ask: how much contention on one row? Low writes per row, and counters can absorb many bookings, so a conditional update.
  • Where does availability live for search? Ask: how fast does it change compared with property data? Much faster, so keep it out of the search index and use a bitmap cache.
  • Hold expiry: sweeper, compute on read, or Redis TTL? Ask: which one is durable and cheap on the hot path? A counter plus a sweeper.
  • Orchestrated or choreographed payment? Ask: is this a short flow with money? Yes, so orchestrated.

Common mistakes

  • Sharding by date. A three-night stay then spans three shards. Fix: shard by property.
  • Read, check, then write. Two guests can take the last room. Fix: put the check in the WHERE clause of one update.
  • Treating a failed night as fine. The guest gets two of three nights. Fix: compare rows updated with nights and roll back.
  • Searching the inventory database. It destroys booking latency. Fix: index for candidates, bitmap cache for dates.
  • Charging before reserving. You can take money for a room you cannot give. Fix: hold first, then charge.
  • Refusing overbooking as a bug. It is a business rule. Fix: make the limit data, capped by a check constraint.
Interview tip Say early that search may be slightly wrong but booking may never be wrong. That one sentence justifies the cache, the bitmaps, the stale "rooms left" label and the strict conditional update, and it shows you see the two halves.

1. Problem statement and clarifying questions

We are asked to design a hotel booking platform in the style of Booking.com, Expedia or a hotel chain's own website. Hotels (called properties) list their room types and prices. Guests search by destination and dates, compare results, reserve a room and pay. The system must also let hotels manage their inventory and rates.

At first glance this looks like Case study 15 (ticket booking), and the contention techniques are indeed related. The differences are what make it a separate interview question. A hotel room is not sold once; it is sold per night, so a three-night stay consumes three separate inventory slots that must all be available at the same moment or none must be taken. Rooms of one type are usually interchangeable, so we sell a count of rooms, not a named seat. Hotels expect some guests to cancel or not show up, so they are allowed to overbook on purpose. And the system is search-heavy: guests look at hundreds of results for every booking they make.

1.1 Clarifying questions and assumed answers

QuestionAssumed answer
What exactly is sold?A room type (for example "Standard double, sea view") at a property, for a range of nights. A specific room number is assigned later, at check-in. So inventory is a count per room type per night.
How large is the catalogue?500,000 properties, about 10 room types each, so 5 million room types. The booking horizon is 365 days ahead.
How much traffic?1.5 million bookings a day; a look-to-book ratio of 500 searches per booking.
Do guests pay immediately?Both models exist. Prepaid rates charge at booking; "pay at property" rates only guarantee with a card. We design for prepay (the harder case) and note the difference.
Can the guest hold a room while paying?Yes. When a guest proceeds to checkout the room is held for 15 minutes.
Is overbooking allowed?Yes, within a limit that each property or the platform sets. Airlines do the same. If more guests arrive than rooms, the property "walks" a guest to a similar hotel at its own cost.
Do hotels also sell through other channels?Yes. A property's own system (a property management system, PMS) and other travel sites sell the same rooms. We receive inventory updates from a channel manager, so our counts can be briefly out of date relative to reality.
Is it acceptable for search results to be slightly stale?Yes. A result can show a room that was taken a few seconds ago. The booking step must then refuse politely. Search can be wrong by a little; booking cannot be wrong at all.
Multi-region?Guests are global but each property lives in one region. We write a property's inventory in its home region and serve search from the nearest region.

2. Requirements and scope

2.1 Functional requirements

  • Search for properties by destination (city, map area or point of interest), check-in and check-out dates, number of guests and rooms, with filters (price, star rating, amenities, free cancellation) and ranked results showing the price for the whole stay.
  • View a property with its room types, photos, rates and live availability for the chosen dates.
  • Hold a room for the whole stay while the guest enters details and pays, for a limited time.
  • Pay and confirm: charge the guest, mark the nights as booked and send a confirmation.
  • Modify and cancel a reservation according to the rate's cancellation policy, releasing inventory and refunding when due.
  • Hotel-side management: set total rooms, prices and overbooking limits per room type per date; close dates; receive reservations.

2.2 Non-functional requirements

  • Correctness of inventory: never confirm more reservations than the sellable limit (physical rooms plus the allowed overbooking) for any night. A multi-night stay either gets all its nights or none.
  • Money correctness: a guest is never charged without a confirmed reservation, and never holds a confirmed reservation without paying (for prepaid rates). No double charges.
  • Search latency: results in under 500 ms at the 95th percentile, even though each query touches thousands of properties.
  • Booking latency: hold placed in under 300 ms at the 99th percentile.
  • Availability: search 99.95%, booking 99.9%. If the inventory store is unreachable we refuse bookings rather than guess.
  • Durability: a confirmed reservation is never lost.

2.3 Out of scope

Reviews and ratings, loyalty programmes, the internals of the payment provider (see Case study 14), dynamic pricing algorithms, the channel manager protocols themselves, room-number assignment and housekeeping, and taxes by jurisdiction. We note where each plugs in.

3. Estimation

Read the table the way an interviewer would. The write path that must be perfectly correct is only a few hundred inventory updates per second, which one well-tuned relational primary per shard can handle without trouble. The numbers that need engineering are the 30,000 searches per second at peak, because each search is not a single lookup but a question of the form "which of the 20,000 properties in this city have a free room for these exact three nights, and at what price?". Total inventory data (about 110 GB) is modest, so the work is about access patterns, not size.

Key idea The system has two very different halves. Search is a high-volume, read-only, approximate problem that we solve with caches and denormalised indexes. Booking is a low-volume, exact, transactional problem that we solve with a single authoritative store and atomic conditional updates. The art is keeping them separate and then connecting them with a cheap, one-way flow of change events.

4. API design

REST over HTTPS (see Chapter 5). State-changing calls carry an Idempotency-Key header so a client retry cannot create a second hold or second charge (Chapter 32).

GET  /v1/search?dest=lisbon&checkIn=2026-06-12&checkOut=2026-06-15
                &adults=2&rooms=1&minPrice=..&stars=4&cursor=...
     -> {results:[{propertyId, name, photo, score, fromPrice, currency,
                   roomTypes:[{roomTypeId, rateId, totalPrice, left}]}], nextCursor}

GET  /v1/properties/{propertyId}/offers?checkIn=..&checkOut=..&adults=2
     -> {offers:[{roomTypeId, rateId, nightlyPrices:[..], total, taxes, policy, left}]}

POST /v1/holds                 (Idempotency-Key)
     body: {propertyId, roomTypeId, rateId, checkIn, checkOut, rooms, priceQuoteToken}
     -> 201 {holdId, expiresAt, total} | 409 {reason:"SOLD_OUT"|"PRICE_CHANGED"}

POST /v1/holds/{holdId}/checkout  (Idempotency-Key)
     body: {guest:{...}, paymentMethodToken}
     -> 202 {reservationId, status:"PENDING_PAYMENT"}
GET  /v1/reservations/{reservationId}   -> {status, nights, total, policy}
POST /v1/reservations/{reservationId}/cancel
DELETE /v1/holds/{holdId}                -> release early
POST /webhooks/payments                  (signed provider callback)

PUT  /v1/extranet/properties/{id}/inventory   (hotel side)
     body: {roomTypeId, from, to, total, overbookLimit, closed}

Design choices worth saying aloud. The search response contains a left hint ("only 2 left") but it is advisory. The hold call takes a priceQuoteToken, a signed blob containing the price the guest saw, so that if the hotel changed the rate in between we return PRICE_CHANGED instead of silently charging a different amount. Checkout returns 202 because payment can complete asynchronously through a webhook. Search uses a cursor, not page numbers, because the underlying result set shifts as availability changes. The hotel-side endpoint writes straight to the inventory store; there is no hold concept on that side.

5.2 Why each store

  • Relational database (PostgreSQL or MySQL) for inventory, holds, reservations and payments. A booking changes several rows (one per night) and must be all-or-nothing, and a reservation must tie to a payment. That is exactly what transactions, uniqueness constraints and row-level atomic updates give us (Chapter 18). Write volume is hundreds per second, which does not need a horizontally scaled NoSQL store, and giving up transactions to gain throughput we do not need would be a poor trade.
  • Search engine (Elasticsearch or OpenSearch) for property discovery. Text, geo filters, facets and ranking are what an inverted index and a geo index do well (Chapter 22, Chapter 58). It indexes properties, not nights.
  • Redis for the availability bitmaps, hold fast path, rate limits and short-lived keys (Chapter 15). Single-key operations are atomic and take microseconds.
  • Cache and CDN for property pages, photos and popular search results (Chapter 12, Chapter 13).
  • Kafka for change events ("inventory changed", "reservation confirmed", "hold expired") that feed the search index, the availability cache, email and analytics (Chapter 34).

5.3 Sharding

Shard inventory, holds and reservations by property_id (or by room_type_id, which stays inside one property). Every night of a stay belongs to the same room type, so a multi-night hold touches only one shard and stays a single-shard transaction with no distributed coordination (Chapter 25). Compare this with sharding by date, which would scatter a three-night stay across three shards and turn every booking into a distributed transaction. Properties are spread across shards by a hash of property_id; there is no meaningful "hot" property the way there is a hot concert, though a destination hotspot (a festival week in one city) concentrates reads, not writes, and reads are absorbed by the search tier.

6. A first simple design

Start with one web server and one database. To book a stay for room type 42 from 12 to 15 June, the server runs one transaction:

BEGIN;
SELECT stay_date, total_rooms + overbook_limit - booked - held AS free
  FROM room_inventory
 WHERE room_type_id = 42 AND stay_date >= '2026-06-12' AND stay_date < '2026-06-15'
 ORDER BY stay_date FOR UPDATE;
-- if every row has free >= 1:
UPDATE room_inventory SET booked = booked + 1
 WHERE room_type_id = 42 AND stay_date >= '2026-06-12' AND stay_date < '2026-06-15';
INSERT INTO reservations ...;
COMMIT;

The FOR UPDATE locks all three night rows, so two guests competing for the last room serialise: the second one sees free = 0 and is refused. Ordering by date makes everyone lock in the same order, which avoids deadlocks. Search is a SQL query that joins properties with inventory and filters by city. Payment is a synchronous call from the web thread.

This is correct, and for a small chain it is the right answer. It fails at scale for predictable reasons. A search that must check every night of the stay for every property in a city does a range scan over millions of inventory rows for each of 30,000 queries per second, so the database drowns. A web thread stuck waiting for a slow payment provider holds a connection. A guest who opens a booking page and wanders off, with no hold concept, can lose the room to someone else during payment, or the room is locked for the guest's whole think time. The scaled design splits search from booking, adds holds with a time limit, and moves payment into a saga.

Component responsibilities:

  • API gateway and CDN terminate TLS, authenticate, rate limit (Chapter 16) and cache static content and photos.
  • Search service turns a query into a ranked, priced list in two stages (section 11). It never writes.
  • Property / offers service returns the detail page and the exact per-night price for the chosen dates.
  • Booking service is stateless; it places holds, runs checkout and talks to the inventory database. Stateless means we scale by adding instances behind a load balancer (Chapter 9).
  • Inventory database is the authority on how many rooms exist, how many are booked and how many are held, per room type per night.
  • Payment saga orchestrates charge, confirm and refund against the external payment provider (section 12).
  • Kafka and consumers keep the search index and availability cache fresh, send email and feed analytics.

8. Request-flow walkthroughs

8.1 Search

  1. The guest submits destination and dates. The gateway checks rate limits and forwards to the search service.
  2. The search service asks the search index for candidate properties: matching destination by geo or city, matching filters (stars, amenities), ranked by relevance, popularity and quality score, returning for example the top 300.
  3. For those 300 properties it asks the availability cache whether each has any room type free for every night of the stay, and drops those that do not.
  4. It fetches per-night prices for the surviving room types, sums them for the stay, applies currency and taxes, and re-ranks (price now matters).
  5. It returns the first page with a cursor. The whole path should take under 500 ms; details are in section 11.

8.2 Placing a hold

Walkthrough. Read the four vertical lines as Guest app, Booking svc, Inventory DB and Kafka, and read the messages from top to bottom. 1. The app posts the hold with a quote token and an idempotency key. 2. Booking svc opens a transaction and claims nights in sorted date order. 3. It sends one conditional UPDATE that adds to held only where free is large enough. 4. The database reports how many rows changed, and the service checks that the count equals the three nights. 5. It inserts the hold row with an expiry and commits. 6. It answers 201 with the hold id and expiry time. 7. An outbox event tells Kafka that availability changed. The red 4x line is the failure branch: if any night is short, roll back and return 409 SOLD_OUT. The sorted order is drawn on purpose, because it stops two bookings from waiting on each other. On a whiteboard, circle step 4 and say it is the decision point. Without that check, a guest could hold two of three nights.
  1. The booking service validates the idempotency key (a repeat returns the earlier result) and the quote token (price still valid).
  2. It opens a transaction and updates the inventory rows for each night in ascending date order, with a condition that the free count is at least the number of rooms wanted (section 9).
  3. If the number of rows updated equals the number of nights, it inserts the hold with a 15-minute expiry and commits. Otherwise it rolls back and returns 409 SOLD_OUT.
  4. An outbox row written in the same transaction lets a relay publish "availability changed" to Kafka, so the search cache learns about it (Chapter 36).
  5. The response carries the server-side expiry time. The countdown shown to the guest is derived from it, not from the client clock.

8.3 Checkout and confirmation

  1. The guest submits details and a payment token. The order service creates the reservation in PENDING_PAYMENT and gives the hold a short grace extension while payment is in flight.
  2. It calls the payment provider with an idempotency key derived from the reservation id to authorise (and, for prepaid, capture) the amount.
  3. On success one database transaction converts the hold: it moves the counts from held to booked on each night, marks the hold CONVERTED, and marks the reservation CONFIRMED, together with an outbox event.
  4. Consumers send the confirmation email, notify the hotel's system and update caches.

9. Deep dive 1: inventory by date and atomic multi-night booking

9.1 What is the unit of inventory?

An interviewer will ask early: how do you represent "what is free"? There are three reasonable models and the choice shapes everything else.

Walkthrough. This is a table, so read it row by row. Each row is one night for room type 42, and the columns are total, overbook, booked, held and free. Free is total plus overbook minus booked minus held. Check one row yourself: Jun 12 is 20 + 2 - 20 - 1 = 1. Jun 13 gives 22 - 16 - 0 = 6. Jun 14 gives 22 - 19 - 2 = 1. The three amber rows are the nights of a stay from Jun 12 to Jun 15, and the check-out day Jun 15 is not a night, so it is not amber. The number that matters is the smallest free value in the amber rows, which is 1. That scarcest night decides whether the whole stay can be sold, and once one more guest takes it the stay is sold out even though Jun 13 still has six rooms. 9.2 The atomic multi-night claim

For each night of the stay we need to say: "if there is room, take it; if not, fail", and the result across all nights must be atomic. The conditional update from Case study 15 carries over with a counter instead of a state flag:

UPDATE room_inventory
   SET held = held + :n, version = version + 1
 WHERE room_type_id = :rt
   AND stay_date BETWEEN :checkIn AND :lastNight
   AND closed = false
   AND (total_rooms + overbook_limit - booked - held) >= :n;
-- application checks: rows_updated == number of nights, else ROLLBACK

Walk through what happens. The database evaluates the condition on each night's row and updates only the rows that satisfy it. If a single night has no room, that row is skipped, the count of updated rows is two instead of three, and the application rolls back the whole transaction so the other two nights are released. Because the rows are locked inside the transaction only for the duration of this statement and the insert of the hold (microseconds to milliseconds), contention is cheap. Always updating in ascending date order (the engine may do this itself for a range predicate, but we do not rely on it for multi-statement variants) prevents the deadlock where booking A holds the 12th and waits for the 13th while booking B holds the 13th and waits for the 12th (Chapter 18, Chapter 8).

The reason a version column is a poor fit is worth stating: in a counter model, two guests booking different rooms of the same type on the same night are not really in conflict while free rooms remain. A conditional UPDATE lets both succeed and conflicts only at the last room, while a version check makes them conflict every time. That is the central insight of this deep dive.

9.4 Keeping the counters honest

Counters are fast but can drift from the truth through bugs, partial failures or manual fixes. We defend in three ways. First, the counter changes and the reservation insert happen in one transaction, so they cannot diverge through a crash. Second, a check constraint booked + held <= total_rooms + overbook_limit on the table makes a violation physically impossible to commit even if application code is wrong. Third, a nightly reconciliation job recomputes each night's booked count from reservation_nights and compares it with the counter, alerting and repairing drift. If we ever needed per-room identity we would add model B as a later assignment step without changing the sale path.

Key idea A counter with a conditional update plus a database check constraint gives the strongest possible guard at the cheapest cost. The application proposes, the database disposes.

10. Deep dive 2: overbooking and the hold lifecycle

10.1 Why overbook, and by how much

Some guests cancel, and some never turn up. If the hotel sells exactly its room count, those rooms stay empty and the revenue is lost. Airlines, hotels and restaurants solve this by selling slightly more than capacity, and accepting a small risk that everyone shows up. When that happens the hotel must walk a guest: arrange and pay for a room at another hotel, plus compensation. The decision is therefore an expected-cost trade-off.

A worked example with simplifying assumptions: a room type has 100 rooms and each booked guest independently shows up with probability 0.95. If we sell 100, the expected number of arrivals is 95 and we never overflow, but on average five rooms sit empty. If we sell 102, arrivals have mean 96.9 and standard deviation about 2.2 (the square root of 102 x 0.95 x 0.05); the chance that more than 100 turn up is about 5%. If we sell 105, the mean is 99.75, the deviation about 2.2, and the chance of overflow rises to roughly 37%. Each extra booking adds expected revenue (a room that would have been empty) but also raises the chance of a walk, which costs the hotel perhaps two or three times the room rate. The right limit is where the marginal expected revenue equals the marginal expected walk cost.

Colour tells the risk: the pale bars are tolerable, the red bars at 104 and 105 are not. Read it left to right. At 100 sold there is almost no walk risk but about five rooms sit empty on average. 10.2 How the system implements it

  • Overbook limit is data, not code. The inventory row has overbook_limit; the free formula is total + overbook_limit - booked - held. A revenue-management job sets it per room type per date, from the predicted show rate. Peak nights with a high demand and low cancellation get zero; low-demand weekdays with free-cancellation rates may get 5 to 10%.
  • Prepaid, non-refundable rates overbook less because those guests rarely cancel; flexible rates overbook more.
  • Guard rails. A platform-wide cap bounds any property's limit, and a property can set it to zero. The database check constraint enforces the cap even if the revenue job has a bug.
  • Walk handling. At check-in a property that is full invokes a "relocate guest" workflow (out of scope), which uses our API to find a comparable room elsewhere and records the cost. We track walks per property as a quality metric and reduce its limit if it walks too often.
  • Shared inventory with other channels. Another site selling the same hotel is a more common cause of oversell than our own bugs. Our counts are only as fresh as the channel manager's updates, so we treat the last few rooms conservatively (for example, stop selling when the free count reaches a small buffer on high-risk properties) and we reconcile on every update from the property.

10.3 Holds with a TTL

A hold is a promise to a guest: "this room is yours for 15 minutes while you pay". It exists because the checkout form takes time, because payment may need a bank redirect (3-D Secure), and because without it a guest could pay for a room that someone else had taken meanwhile. In our counter model a hold increments held on each night. The hold is a row in the holds table with an expiry. The hard part is making an abandoned hold give its rooms back, exactly once.

Walkthrough. Each rounded box is a status of one hold row, and each arrow is one allowed change. 1. A hold starts in ACTIVE, where held has been raised by the room count and the 15 minute timer runs. 2. When the guest starts checkout, it moves to PAYING with a short grace period of 3 minutes. 3. If payment is confirmed, it moves to CONVERTED, where held goes down and booked goes up. 4. If payment is declined or times out, it moves to EXPIRED / RELEASED and held goes down. 5. The long red curve from ACTIVE is the abandoned guest: the timer passes and the sweeper releases the rooms. It is drawn as a state machine because each move is one guarded status change in the same transaction as the counter change, so a hold cannot be released twice. On a whiteboard, draw the two exit boxes and put the word "once" on every arrow. Without PAYING, a slow payment loses its room at the 15 minute mark. Without the guard, the sweeper and a payment could both adjust the counters.

There are three ways to implement the expiry. We compare them:

Choice: approach 1 as the source of truth, with a sweeper that runs every few seconds, plus a cheap safety net: the booking path treats expired holds as releasable. Concretely, when a conditional update would fail because free is zero, the service first runs a targeted cleanup for that room type and dates (expire holds past their deadline and retry once). That gives nearly exact behaviour without a hot-path aggregate. The sweeper's SQL is idempotent because the status change is the guard:

-- in one transaction per batch of expired holds
UPDATE holds SET status='EXPIRED'
 WHERE status='ACTIVE' AND expires_at < now()
 RETURNING hold_id, room_type_id, check_in, check_out, rooms;
-- for each returned row, in the same transaction:
UPDATE room_inventory SET held = held - :rooms
 WHERE room_type_id=:rt AND stay_date >= :ci AND stay_date < :co;

If the sweeper crashes halfway, the transaction rolls back and the next run retries; because the status flip is part of the same transaction, no hold is ever released twice. This is the same lazy-plus-sweeper idea as cache expiry (Chapter 13) and the scheduling machinery in Chapter 38.

Watch out The dangerous edge is a payment that succeeds at 15 minutes and 2 seconds, just after the hold expired and another guest took the last room. The grace extension at checkout start reduces this, and the confirm transaction requires the hold to still be ACTIVE or PAYING. If it is not, we refund the payment automatically and apologise. We never confirm a reservation without an inventory claim.

10.4 Cancellations and modifications

Cancelling a reservation reverses its inventory effect, using reservation_nights to decrement booked on exactly the nights it consumed, guarded by the reservation status change (WHERE status='CONFIRMED') so a duplicate cancel is a no-op. A refund follows the rate's policy (for example full refund until 48 hours before check-in). Modifying dates is two actions: claim the new nights and release the old ones in one transaction, so the guest never loses their room if the new dates are unavailable. A change that lengthens a stay is a fresh conditional claim on only the added nights.

11. Deep dive 3: search with availability

Search is where most of the traffic is, and it has a shape that surprises candidates. Finding "hotels in Lisbon" is a classic search-engine problem. Finding "hotels in Lisbon with a free double room on all of 12, 13 and 14 June, priced for the whole stay" combines a text and geo search with a per-night, per-room-type availability and price check that changes constantly. Putting everything in one query is hard to make fast.

11.2 The availability bitmap

For each room type we keep a bit string of 365 bits, one per night starting from a rolling base date, where 1 means "at least one room is sellable that night" (free >= 1). A stay of three nights is available if the AND of its three bits is 1. In practice we build a mask with three bits set and check (bits & mask) == mask, one CPU instruction on a 64-bit slice of the string. With 5 million room types the whole structure is about 228 MB, so each search node can even hold a full copy in memory. Checking 300 properties with 10 room types each is 3,000 checks, which takes microseconds. The bitmap loses the exact count (it knows "at least one" not "five left"), so we keep a second structure of small counters for the "only 2 left" label, or fetch counts from the inventory service just for the displayed page.

The cache is built and kept fresh from the inventory database's change events. Each booking, hold, release or hotel-side update emits an event; a consumer recomputes the affected bit and updates the cache. The lag is typically under a second to a few seconds. A bit can only be wrong in two ways: it says free but the room is gone (the hold step refuses, which is acceptable) or it says sold out but a room was just released (the guest misses a room for a moment, which is also acceptable). We are asymmetric about the first: when free falls to zero, the booking service publishes the event at high priority so sold-out reaches the cache faster than becoming available again. Wrongly showing rooms that are gone wastes the guest's time and erodes trust.

11.3 Prices

Nightly prices change with the date, day of week and demand, and some rates apply only to certain length of stay. We store a price per room type per rate plan per night and cache it with a short TTL. The search service sums the nightly prices for the dates, adds taxes and fees, converts currency, and applies any promotion. Prices shown in results are quotes with a signed token; the hold step re-verifies. A price can also be pre-aggregated for the commonest lengths of stay (1 to 7 nights from popular check-in dates), a classic denormalisation that trades storage for latency.

11.4 Ranking and caching results

Ranking mixes relevance to the destination, distance from the searched point, review score, price, conversion probability and a commercial component. Because ranking needs the price, which needs the dates, the final order cannot be fully precomputed; we compute the static score offline and blend it with the price at query time. Popular queries (city plus dates plus guests) repeat enough that we cache the final first page for 30 to 60 seconds, keyed on a normalised query. Different guests see the same page, so cache hit ratios of 30 to 60% are plausible, which cuts the 30,000 peak searches to something like 12,000 to 21,000 reaching the pipeline. We never cache the hold, checkout or confirmation paths. The cache-failure patterns (stampede, hot keys) are in Chapter 14.

11.5 Index sizing and geo

The property index is 2.5 GB in total, so a handful of shards with replicas serve all queries, replicated per region. The geo part uses a geohash, quadtree or similar structure inside the engine so a "within 5 km of the Eiffel Tower" query visits only nearby cells (Chapter 58, and the same technique appears in Case study 13). The index changes when hotels edit descriptions, photos or amenities, perhaps a few thousand updates per hour, which the near-real-time refresh handles easily. This is exactly why availability does not belong inside it.

12. Deep dive 4: payments and the booking saga

A booking spans our inventory database and an external payment provider, which cannot share a transaction. As in Case study 14 and Case study 15, we use a saga: a series of local transactions with compensating actions (Chapter 48, Chapter 31).

Walkthrough. The four lines are Guest, Order svc, Payment PSP and Inventory DB. Read the messages in order. 1. The guest sends checkout with the hold and a key. 2. Order svc moves the hold to PAYING with 3 extra minutes and creates the reservation as PENDING. 3. It asks the PSP to authorise and capture, using the reservation id as the key. 4. The PSP reports success by response or webhook. 5. Order svc runs one transaction in the Inventory DB: held becomes booked, the reservation becomes CONFIRMED, and an outbox row is written. 6. The guest gets the confirmation by poll or push. The red 5x line is the rare case where step 5 fails, so the service refunds. The note at the bottom covers a decline: release the hold and mark the reservation FAILED. The order matters: the room is reserved at step 2 before money moves at step 3. On a whiteboard, draw the PSP line apart from the others, because it is the only box outside our control. If you skip the key at step 3, a retry can charge twice.

12.2 Rules that keep the saga safe

  • Reserve, then charge. Charging first and then trying to claim the nights can take money for rooms we cannot give. Holding first means the room is ours while money moves.
  • Idempotency everywhere. The charge to the provider carries a key derived from the reservation id, so a retry after a timeout cannot double charge. Our own endpoints take a client key so a double click creates one hold; a unique constraint on the key enforces it (Chapter 32).
  • Unknown outcomes. If the charge call times out we do not know if it succeeded. The reservation stays PENDING_PAYMENT, and a reconciler asks the provider for the status by key and finishes the saga. We never guess.
  • Webhook plus poll. Webhooks are fast but can be lost or repeated. We verify signatures, deduplicate by event id and also poll pending reservations older than a minute.
  • Outbox. The confirmation and the Kafka event are written in one local transaction (Chapter 36), so a crash cannot leave a confirmed room with no email, or a published event for an unconfirmed room.
  • Timeouts, circuit breakers and bulkheads around the provider so a slow payment service cannot starve hold placement (Chapter 51).
  • Currency and price lock. The quote token pins the price and currency shown to the guest; a changed price returns PRICE_CHANGED and the guest must re-confirm.
  • Card data stays out. The browser sends card details straight to the provider and we store only a token, which keeps us out of the heaviest compliance scope.

We use an orchestrated saga: the order service owns the state machine (PENDING_PAYMENT, PAID, CONFIRMED, FAILED, REFUNDED). For a short flow involving money, one place that knows the whole story is easier to audit than choreographed events. Choreography is fine downstream, for email, analytics and search updates, where Kafka consumers act independently.

13.1 Capacity math for the inventory tier

At peak we expect about 500 row updates per second across all properties. Spread by property over, say, 16 shards, that is about 30 updates per second per shard, far below what a single primary can sustain (thousands per second). Storage per shard is about 110 GB divided by 16, around 7 GB of inventory, which sits in memory. We could run on far fewer shards; the reason to shard is isolation and growth, not current load. Even a single hot property selling its last room on a peak night sees tens of conflicting updates per second on one row, which the engine handles. That is a stark contrast to the thousands of buyers per second on a hot concert, which is why this system needs no queue or waiting room for ordinary traffic.

13.2 The one hot spot: destination peaks

Events create demand spikes. A city hosting a large conference or festival sees hundreds of guests chasing the same few hotels on the same nights. Writes remain modest, but searches for that city and date range concentrate on a small set of candidates. We handle it with the result cache (these queries repeat exactly), per-room-type hot caching, and a gentle degrade: when a search is overloaded, serve slightly older cached availability rather than refuse. The booking path for a sold-out property returns fast, since the conditional update fails immediately when free is zero. If a single property still saturates its row, a short per-property queue or a token bucket on holds for that room type spreads the load (Chapter 16).

13.3 Multi-region

Each property has a home region that owns its inventory writes, avoiding cross-region conflicts for a single room type entirely. Guests far from the home region pay an extra 100 to 200 ms on the hold call, which is acceptable for a rare action. Search, property pages and caches are served from the nearest region, built from the global change event stream, accepting that availability in a remote region lags by a second or two. Disaster recovery uses asynchronous replication to a standby region; if the primary region fails, we promote the standby, accepting that the last few seconds of holds and bookings could be lost, and we reconcile against payment provider records so no guest is charged without a reservation (Chapter 41, Chapter 26).

14.1 Consistency choices

Following Chapter 28, the one thing that must be linearizable is the inventory counter transition for a room type, which lives on one primary. When that primary is unreachable we choose consistency over availability and refuse to book. Everything else, such as search results, "rooms left" labels, a guest's reservation list and analytics, is eventually consistent. This split is the core consistency story: strong where a wrong answer costs a double-sold room or a mischarged card, weak where a wrong answer costs one extra click.

14.2 Idempotency summary

  • Place hold: an idempotency key maps to the original hold result for its lifetime (24 hours), enforced by a unique constraint.
  • Checkout: a unique key per reservation attempt; the payment key derives from the reservation id.
  • Confirm: the transition HELD to BOOKED is guarded by WHERE hold_id=:h AND status IN ('ACTIVE','PAYING'), so a duplicate confirm changes zero rows and is treated as success if the reservation is already confirmed.
  • Cancel: guarded by WHERE status='CONFIRMED'; a repeat is a no-op, and the refund call carries its own key.
  • Kafka consumers deduplicate by event id because delivery is at least once; the availability recompute is naturally idempotent because it derives the bit from the current counters.

15. Security, abuse, monitoring and cost

15.1 Security and abuse

  • Scrapers. Competitors and aggregators scrape prices and availability. We rate limit by account, IP and fingerprint, require sessions for deep pagination, and serve results through signed cursors (Chapter 16, Chapter 55).
  • Hold hoarding. A bad actor or bot could place many holds to block rooms. We cap active holds per account and per device, cap the grace extension, and require verified identity beyond a threshold.
  • Fraud. Stolen cards and fake accounts are screened by a risk score before capture; suspicious reservations are held for review and cancelled with their rooms returned.
  • Hotel-side integrity. Inventory and price updates from extranet and channel managers are authenticated per property, validated (no negative counts, bounded overbook limits) and audit logged, because a bad bulk update could oversell or underprice thousands of nights.
  • PII. Guest names, contact details and payment tokens are encrypted at rest and access controlled; card numbers never touch our servers.

15.2 Monitoring and SLOs

  • Correctness SLO: zero nights where booked + held exceeds total plus allowed overbook. A continuous audit recomputes counters from reservation rows and alerts on drift or violation.
  • Latency: search p95 under 500 ms; hold p99 under 300 ms; offers p95 under 200 ms.
  • Availability: search 99.95%; hold and checkout 99.9%.
  • Freshness: lag from inventory commit to availability cache update, with a target p99 under 5 seconds.
  • Business signals: search-to-hold and hold-to-booking conversion, hold expiry rate, 409 SOLD_OUT rate (a high rate means the cache is too stale), walk rate per property, payment decline rate, reconciler backlog.
  • Runbook dials: degrade search to cached availability, disable a property's overbooking, shorten or lengthen hold TTL, switch the payment provider.

Observability practices are in Chapter 54.

15.3 Cost notes

Search dominates compute because of the 500:1 look-to-book ratio, so the biggest savings come from result caching, in-memory bitmaps and avoiding heavy per-query work. Storage cost is small: about 110 GB of inventory and half a terabyte of reservations per year. The CDN carries photos, which are typically the largest bandwidth line item. Payment provider fees are a percentage of each booking and are a business cost to be negotiated, not engineered away. Reservation data older than a few years moves to cold storage.

16. Evolution, phasing and alternatives

16.1 What to build first

  1. Phase 1: one relational database, inventory counters per night, conditional-update booking, synchronous payment with idempotency keys. Search as simple SQL on a small catalogue. Correct and enough for the first few thousand properties.
  2. Phase 2: a search engine for properties, with an availability filter read from replicas; holds with TTL and a sweeper; the order saga with webhook plus reconciliation; Kafka outbox for emails.
  3. Phase 3: availability bitmaps in a cache fed by change events; result caching; denormalised price aggregates; hotel extranet and channel-manager sync.
  4. Phase 4: overbooking with revenue management, regional deployments with home-region ownership, fraud scoring, fallback payment provider.

16.2 At 10x scale

With 15 million bookings a day and 300,000 searches per second at peak, the inventory write path is still modest (a few thousand updates per second), so we add shards rather than redesign. The pressure is in search: partition the candidate index by region, precompute availability and price aggregates for the most common (destination, length-of-stay) combinations, push personalisation to a separate ranking service, and move the cache to a sharded cluster with per-region copies. At that scale the model also needs an experimentation platform for ranking, and a dedicated team for supply connectivity.

16.3 Alternatives not chosen

  • Distributed locks per room type (Chapter 30, Case study 21). A separate lock service adds a failure mode and lease expiry hazards without more correctness than the database's own conditional update.
  • Room-level interval model with exclusion constraints. Beautiful for correctness and for assigning rooms, but it forbids overbooking and makes search harder. We would adopt it for a boutique chain that assigns rooms at booking time.
  • Availability inside the search index. Simpler architecture but expensive reindexing on every booking and slow near-real-time visibility.
  • Event sourcing the inventory. Good audit trail but read-model complexity; a counter table plus an audit log gives most of the benefit.
  • Choreographed payment saga. Valid, but harder to audit and debug for a flow involving money.
  • NoSQL with eventual consistency for inventory. Higher write scale than we need, and weaker guarantees for the one thing that must be exact.

17. Interview Q&A

How do you represent inventory so that a three-night stay is booked atomically?

I keep one row per room type per night with the total, an overbook limit, and booked and held counters. A stay touches one row for each night, all inside one property, which means one shard and one local transaction. I run a conditional update that increments the counter only where the free count is at least the rooms requested, then check that the number of rows updated equals the number of nights. If any night fails, I roll back the transaction so the guest gets all nights or none. A check constraint on the table makes it impossible to commit counters above the allowed limit, even if the application has a bug.

Why use a conditional UPDATE rather than SELECT FOR UPDATE or an optimistic version column?

All three are correct if used properly, but they behave differently under contention. SELECT FOR UPDATE makes waiters queue while holding connections. A version column forces a retry whenever any other booking touched the row, even when free rooms remain, which is wasteful for a counter. A conditional update puts the check in the WHERE clause, so concurrent bookings of different rooms of the same type both succeed and only the last room causes a conflict. Losers fail instantly with zero updated rows, with no waiting on client think time.

What is overbooking, and how would you implement it safely?

Overbooking means selling more rooms than exist because some guests cancel or do not show up. I implement it as data: an overbook limit per room type per night, so the free formula is total plus limit minus booked minus held. A revenue-management job sets the limit from predicted show rates, higher on flexible low-demand nights and zero on peak nights. A platform cap and a database check constraint bound it, so a bad job cannot oversell wildly. When guests do overflow, a walk workflow relocates one to a comparable hotel at the property's cost, and I track the walk rate so a property that walks too often gets its limit reduced.

How do holds expire without leaking rooms?

A hold is a row with a status and an expiry time, and the held counter on each night reflects its rooms. A sweeper runs every few seconds, flips ACTIVE holds past their expiry to EXPIRED and decrements the counters, all in one transaction, so a hold can never be released twice even if the sweeper crashes and retries. The booking path adds a safety net: before reporting sold out it runs a targeted expiry for that room type and dates and retries once. Checkout also extends the hold briefly so a slow payment does not lose the room, and a payment that succeeds for a lost hold is refunded automatically.

Why not put availability in Elasticsearch together with the property data?

Because the two data sets change at very different rates. Property descriptions change a few thousand times an hour; availability changes with every hold, booking and release, and each change would rewrite a document and wait for the next refresh before becoming visible. Filtering nested nightly data over a year is also slow. I use the search index for candidate retrieval by geo, filters and quality, then check the exact dates against compact in-memory availability bitmaps and attach prices. Each store then does what it is good at, and the cost is a small amount of staleness plus a loop to fetch more candidates when many are sold out.

How do you answer "is this property free for these nights" in microseconds?

For each room type I keep a 365-bit mask, one bit per night, set when at least one room is sellable. For a stay I build a mask with the stay's nights set and test with a bitwise AND, so each check is a single instruction on a machine word. With 5 million room types the whole structure is around 228 MB, so each search node can hold it in memory. A consumer updates the bit from inventory change events within a second or two. The bitmap cannot say how many rooms remain, so I show the count only for the displayed page by asking the inventory service.

Payment succeeded but our service crashed before confirming the booking. What happens?

The reservation is PENDING_PAYMENT with a stored idempotency key, and the provider has a record of the charge. On restart, or via the webhook, the order service learns of the success and runs the confirm transaction, which converts the hold into booked counts and marks the reservation confirmed, together with an outbox event. A reconciler also sweeps pending reservations older than a minute and asks the provider for their status by key, so we do not depend on a lost webhook. If the hold was lost in the meantime, the confirm fails its condition and we refund. Every ambiguous state is resolved by asking the source of truth, not guessing.

How do you avoid showing availability that is already gone?

You cannot avoid it completely, and trying to is not worth the cost. The cache is a hint built from change events and lags by a second or so, so occasionally a guest selects a room that was just taken. The hold step is authoritative and returns a specific sold-out response, and we then show similar rooms or nearby dates. I do reduce the harm in two ways: sold-out events are published at higher priority than released-room events so the cache learns bad news first, and for the last few rooms I can verify against the inventory service before showing the "only 1 left" label.

What changes if another website also sells the same rooms?

Then our counters are only as accurate as the property's updates, and the real risk is a room sold twice by two channels. The property or its channel manager pushes inventory to all channels, so we treat their updates as authoritative and apply them immediately, and we reconcile on every update. For properties known to be slow to sync, we keep a safety buffer and stop selling when free rooms fall to a small number. The overbooking limit absorbs small errors, and the walk workflow handles the rest. It is a business problem with technical mitigations, not one we can eliminate on our own side.

How would you shard this system and why by property?

I shard inventory, holds and reservations by property id. A stay covers several nights of one room type in one property, so every booking transaction touches one shard and needs no distributed coordination. Sharding by date would put each night of a stay on a different shard and turn every booking into a distributed transaction. The cost is that guest-centric queries such as "my trips" cross shards, which I serve from a secondary table maintained from Kafka events. Properties spread evenly by hash, and since writes are only hundreds per second the shard count is about isolation and growth rather than current load.

How does this differ from the ticket-booking design?

Ticket booking is dominated by a spike where a million people fight over a few thousand named seats, so it needs a waiting room, a Redis gate and hot-event cells. Hotel booking has much calmer write contention, since a property is rarely fought over by thousands in one second, but it has multi-night atomicity, interchangeable rooms sold by count, deliberate overbooking, and a far larger read problem in search with date-dependent availability and price. The shared ideas are the conditional update as the authority, holds with a TTL, a payment saga with idempotency, and stale-tolerant read caches in front of a strict write path.

18. Recap

  • Two halves: search is high-volume and approximate (30,000 queries per second at peak, 500 searches per booking); booking is low-volume and exact (about 90 per second at peak, 300 to 500 inventory row updates per second).
  • Inventory is a counter per room type per night: free = total + overbook limit - booked - held. A stay touches one row per night in one property, so shard by property and keep each booking a single-shard transaction.
  • Book with a conditional UPDATE and check that rows updated equals nights; back it with a check constraint and a nightly reconciliation against reservation rows.
  • Overbooking is a data-driven limit set by expected show rates, bounded by a platform cap, with a walk workflow as the cost of losing the bet.
  • Holds are rows with a TTL, a held counter, a sweeper that releases each hold exactly once, and a grace extension during payment.
  • Search is two-stage: a search index for candidates, then in-memory availability bitmaps and cached prices for the exact dates. Stale availability is acceptable because the hold step is authoritative.
  • Checkout is an orchestrated saga: reserve, charge with an idempotency key, confirm in one transaction with an outbox, refund on a lost race; unknown outcomes are reconciled, never guessed.
  • Strong consistency only for the inventory counters on each property's primary; everything else is eventually consistent. Each property has one home region that owns writes.
  • Build first the correct simple core, then add the search engine, the cache and bitmaps, and finally overbooking and multi-region.
