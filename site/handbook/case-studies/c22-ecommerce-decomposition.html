Decompose an E-commerce Monolith

A microservices capstone: take a twelve-module e-commerce monolith with one shared database, find the real service boundaries, decide what to extract first and what never to extract, move the data without losing an order, and keep Black Friday alive at every step of the migration.

How to approach this problem (read this first)

What the interviewer is really testing. This is not a "draw the end state" question. The hard parts are: first, judgment about whether and how much to split (many pains do not need a network boundary); second, the order of work and how to keep the business running and reversible at every step; third, moving data and the money path (checkout, stock, payment) without losing or duplicating anything. A candidate who jumps straight to twelve boxes has missed the point.

The thinking order, step by step

  1. Ask what hurts and what must not change. Say: "What is the actual pain: deploys, outages, database load, or team conflict? And is a rewrite allowed?" Ask first because each pain has a different cure, and the answer (no rewrite, 11 months, Black Friday deadline) sets every later constraint.
  2. Estimate to find the real pressure. Say: "Reads outnumber orders about 320 to 1 at peak, and 70 percent of database time is replicable reads." Do the traffic split before any design because it shows that the cheapest win is moving reads off the primary, not touching checkout.
  3. Name the pains and match each to a cure. Say: "Reviews taking down checkout needs process isolation. Database CPU needs read offloading. Slow releases need independent pipelines." This is where you separate problems needing microservices from problems needing discipline.
  4. Find boundaries by domain, not by table. Say: "I split by bounded context and team, and I will also list what I will not split." The word "product" meaning four different things is the example to use. Boundaries come before the plan so the plan has units to order.
  5. Put a gateway in front, then choose the order. Say: "Step zero is a gateway with all traffic still on the monolith. Then I extract low risk, high read, low coupling contexts first and the money path last." The gateway comes first because it makes every later step a routing rule with instant rollback.
  6. Explain how data moves. Say: "Break foreign keys, start CDC, backfill in chunks, verify, switch reads, then switch writes. No long lived dual writes." Data comes after order because each context needs this recipe.
  7. Deep dive the checkout saga. Say: "One transaction becomes a saga with compensations and a pivot point, with atomic stock reservation and idempotency keys." This is the likely probe, so reserve real time for it.
  8. Cover reads, resilience and operations. Say: "Product pages come from a projected read model; only price and stock are live. Every call has a timeout from a propagated budget." Keep it to the failure each pattern prevents.
  9. Finish with phases, rollback and honest cost. Say: "Four phases, risk ascending, with an exit gate and a rollback each. It costs more infrastructure and needs a platform team." End on the safe state at Black Friday.

The decision points

  • Split at all, or modular monolith? Ask how many of these are true: different scaling, change cadence, failure tolerance, owning team. Two or more justify a boundary; one does not.
  • What goes first? Ask what is the value, risk and coupling. High value, low risk, low coupling goes first, so Reviews and Notifications beat Checkout.
  • Dual write or CDC? Ask whether the two writes can be atomic. They cannot, so make one store the source of truth and derive the other from its log.
  • Saga orchestration or choreography? Ask whether there is a business transaction with compensations. Yes for checkout, so orchestrate; the fan out after confirmation is choreographed.
  • Compose live or project a read model? Ask how stale the data may be. Stars and descriptions can lag; price and stock at purchase cannot.

Common mistakes on this problem

  • Proposing a big bang rewrite. Fix: strangler pattern behind a gateway.
  • Splitting by database table or noun. It builds a distributed monolith. Fix: split by bounded context and team.
  • Starting with checkout because it hurts most. Fix: build the tooling on safe contexts first.
  • Relying on dual writes. They diverge silently. Fix: CDC or an outbox with one source of truth.
  • Using two phase commit across the PSP. The PSP cannot join. Fix: a saga with compensations and idempotency keys.
  • Forgetting rollback after writes move. Fix: reverse replication or cohort based cut over, planned in advance.
Interview tip Open with: "First I will ask whether we should split at all, and which pains need a network boundary." Then spend your depth on the path (order, data, rollback), because that is what the question is about.

1. Problem statement and clarifying questions

The interviewer says: "We run an online retailer on a single Java application backed by one large PostgreSQL database. It has grown for nine years. Deploys are painful, Black Friday is frightening, and the CTO wants microservices. You are the architect. Tell me what you would do, in what order, and what could go wrong."

This is not a "design a new system" question. The hard part is that the business keeps running while you change it. A good answer therefore spends less time drawing the end state and more time on the path: the order of extraction, how data moves, how each step is undone if it fails. Before drawing anything, ask questions that fix the scope.

QuestionAssumed answer
What does the monolith contain?Twelve modules in one deployable: catalog, search, cart, checkout, orders, payments, inventory, shipping, users, promotions, notifications, reviews.
How is it deployed and by whom?One artifact, 12 app instances behind a load balancer, released every two weeks after a 45-minute build and a 3-hour regression suite. About 60 engineers commit to the one repository.
What is the data situation?One PostgreSQL primary (64 vCPU) with 4 read replicas, about 1.4 TB, roughly 380 tables. Every module reads and writes any table it likes, and there are foreign keys across module boundaries.

Interview tip Say the opening position out loud: "My first question is whether we should do this at all, and how much of it. Microservices are an organizational scaling tool as much as a technical one. I will extract the parts where independent scaling or independent deployment pays for the added complexity, and I will deliberately leave the rest." That frames the whole answer and shows maturity.

2. Requirements and scope

2.1 Functional

  • All existing customer behavior continues unchanged: browse, search, view product, add to cart, apply promotion, pay, track order, review.
  • Every capability has exactly one owning team and one owning data store at the end state (with explicit exceptions in section 6).
  • At every migration step, traffic can be shifted back to the monolith within minutes.

2.2 Non-functional

  • Correctness: never oversell beyond an agreed tolerance, never charge a customer without an order, never create an order without payment or an explicit "pay later" state. Money and stock are the sacred data.
  • Availability: browsing 99.95%, checkout 99.9% (about 43 minutes of error budget per month). Reviews and recommendations may degrade without affecting checkout.
  • Latency: product page p99 under 400 ms server side; checkout submit p99 under 3 s including the PSP call.
  • Independent deployability: a team ships its service several times a week without a company-wide freeze.
  • Migration safety: shadow traffic, reconciliation, and a documented rollback per phase.

2.3 Out of scope

Building a new recommendation engine, replacing the PSP, re-platforming the front end, a multi-region active-active design (we mention it under evolution), and the warehouse management system internals beyond the interface we call.

3. Estimation: the numbers that drive decisions

Estimation here is not for sizing a new system. It tells us which parts of the monolith are under real pressure and which are fine, and therefore which extractions pay for themselves. (Technique: Chapter 2.)

Notice the shape. Reads outnumber orders by about 8,000 / 25 = 320 to 1 at peak. Checkout is small in volume but enormous in value; browsing is huge in volume but cheap and cacheable. These two shapes want different infrastructure, and in the monolith they are glued together.

3.2 Storage

  • Order: about 3 KB with lines, addresses and payment references. Normal day 150,000 orders x 3 KB = 450 MB/day, about 165 GB per year. Black Friday 600,000 x 3 KB = 1.8 GB that day.
  • Catalog: 2M SKUs x 10 KB of attributes = 20 GB. A rendered product-page fragment is about 30 KB; the hot 200,000 products x 30 KB = 6 GB, which fits in a small cache cluster. (Cache design: Chapter 13.)
  • Reviews: 40M reviews x 1 KB = 40 GB, almost entirely read.
  • Event log for CDC: every order touches about 6 state changes, each event 1 KB, so 600k x 6 x 1 KB = 3.6 GB per Black Friday. Trivial for Kafka (Chapter 34).

3.3 What the monolith DB feels today

On a normal Friday evening the primary runs at 85% CPU. About 70% of its query time is reads for the catalog, search facets, reviews and order history, which are replicable and cacheable. Only about 8% is checkout and inventory writes. If we move reads off the primary, the database is no longer the first thing to break, and that is the cheapest scaling win in the whole project. This is the single most important estimation insight: the first extractions should be the read-heavy, low-risk contexts, not the money path.

4. The monolith and its pain points

The starting architecture is a classic "modular in name only" monolith. The modules are Java packages, but any package can call any other and any can run SQL against any table.

5. Finding the boundaries: bounded contexts and subdomains

The wrong way to split is by technical layer or by noun ("a ProductService, an OrderService, a UserService, one per database table"). That produces a distributed monolith: services that must all be deployed and called together. The right way, from domain-driven design (Chapter 45), is to find bounded contexts: areas where a word has one precise meaning and one team can own the model end to end.

A telling example: the word "product" means different things. In catalog it is rich descriptive content (title, images, attributes, SEO). In inventory it is a SKU with a count per warehouse. In cart and orders it is a line item with a price frozen at purchase time. In shipping it is a weight and dimensions. If one shared "Product" class serves all four, every team edits it and nobody can change it safely. Separate contexts each keep their own small model of a product, linked by a stable SKU id.

5.3 What NOT to split

Equally important is the negative list.

  • Order and payment record keeping stay in one transactional boundary at first. Separating them early forces a distributed transaction on the most valuable write. We split them last, and only when payment methods (wallets, buy-now-pay-later) change faster than order logic.
  • Pricing, tax and currency rounding are one library or one service. If cart, promotions and checkout each compute totals, a one-cent rounding difference between them produces a "price changed at payment" support ticket. Keep a single pricing function.
  • Order lines and order headers are never separate services. Entities that always change together in one transaction are one aggregate.
  • Tiny CRUD concerns (wish lists, store locator, gift-card balance lookup) stay in the residual monolith. A "nano service" costs a pipeline, dashboards and an on-call rotation, and yields nothing.
  • Admin back office is read-mostly and low traffic. It is a client of the services, not a service itself.
Key idea A boundary is justified when at least two of these are true: it has a different scaling profile, a different change cadence, a different failure-tolerance requirement, or a different owning team. If only one is true, a module inside the monolith with a clean interface is cheaper and almost as good.

7. Migration strategy: the strangler fig

The strangler fig pattern (Chapter 44) grows new services around the monolith, routes one slice of functionality at a time to them, and lets the monolith shrink until it can be deleted or left as a small core. It works because nothing changes for callers: a routing layer decides, per request, who answers.

7.1 The routing layer is the gateway

We put an API gateway (Chapter 11) in front of the monolith first, with 100% of traffic still going to the monolith. That is step zero, it is nearly risk free, and from then on every extraction is a routing rule rather than a client change. The gateway supports routing by path, header, cookie or percentage, so we can do canary releases (1%, 5%, 25%, 100%) and instant rollback by flipping a rule.

Walkthrough. Purple is the clients, amber is the gateway and the translator, blue is the new services, red is the monolith, green is the legacy database. 1. Clients call the gateway as before and never learn about the migration. 2. The gateway applies a routing rule per path, percentage or user. 3. Paths that were extracted go to Reviews, Catalog or Inventory services. 4. Everything else takes the dashed default route to the shrinking monolith. 5. When a new service needs an old fact, its call goes through the anti-corruption layer on the right, which translates and reads the legacy database or the monolith. The red box is deliberately drawn smallest at the end of the migration. The design avoids changing clients at each step. Draw the gateway first and add one service arrow at a time. If you remove the gateway, every extraction needs client releases and rollback is slow. If you remove the translator, old status codes leak into new models.

7.2 The anti-corruption layer

The monolith has quirks: status codes like S7, prices in integer cents in one table and decimals in another, a "customer" that is sometimes a company. If a new service adopts those quirks, the mess leaks into every new model. An anti-corruption layer (ACL) is an adapter between the old model and the new one. Calls into the monolith go through a small client in the new service (or a dedicated translator service) that converts S7 into SHIPPED, converts cents to a Money type, and maps old ids to new ids. When the old module is finally deleted, only the ACL is removed. The new domain code never changes.

7.3 Choosing the order: what to extract first

We score each candidate by three things: value (how much pain removed), risk (what happens if it breaks), and coupling (how many tables and calls cross its boundary). We want high value, low risk, low coupling first, in order to build the muscles (pipelines, observability, CDC) on safe ground before touching money.

8. Splitting the data

The code split is the easy part. The shared database is where migrations stall, because one table may be written by four modules, and foreign keys and joins cross every boundary. The rule of the end state (Chapter 46): each service owns its data, and nobody else touches it. The path there has five steps for each context.

  1. Make the boundary visible in the monolith. Move the module's code behind an interface; find every SQL statement from other modules that touches its tables (static analysis plus query logs). Replace them with calls to the interface. This alone is a safe, shippable refactor.
  2. Break cross-boundary foreign keys. Replace order.product_id REFERENCES product(id) with a plain id column and an application-level check. Replace joins with two queries or a denormalized copy (for example the order line stores product title and price at purchase time, which is correct domain behavior anyway because the order must not change when the catalog changes).
  3. Replicate the data to the new store with CDC and a backfill (below).
  4. Switch reads to the new store, compare against the old one in shadow mode.
  5. Switch writes (the new service becomes the source of truth), keep the old table readable for a rollback window, then retire it.

8.1 Why not dual writes

The naive way to keep two stores in sync is "dual write": the application writes to the old DB, then to the new store. It fails because the two writes are not atomic. If the process crashes between them, or the second one times out but actually succeeded, the stores diverge, silently. There is no transaction across a PostgreSQL table and a different store. Two concurrent writers can also apply the two updates in different orders to the two stores. For migration, dual writing is acceptable only as a short, closely monitored phase with a reconciliation job, never as the long-term sync mechanism. The long-term answer is to make one store the source of truth and derive the other from its change log.

8.2 CDC and the transactional outbox

Change data capture (CDC) reads the database's write-ahead log (in PostgreSQL, logical decoding, commonly through a tool such as Debezium) and emits every committed row change as an event to Kafka, in commit order, with no change to application code. It is ideal for the early phases because it works on the untouched monolith. (Chapter 36, Chapter 34.)

For events the business wants to publish deliberately (for example OrderPlaced with a clean contract rather than a raw table diff), use the transactional outbox: in the same database transaction that writes the order row, insert a row into an outbox table. A relay (often CDC reading the outbox table) publishes it to Kafka. Because the order and the event commit together or not at all, you never publish an event for a rolled-back order, and never lose an event for a committed one. Delivery is at least once, so consumers must be idempotent (Chapter 32): they record processed event ids, or apply changes that are naturally repeatable ("set status to SHIPPED").

Walkthrough. Red is the monolith, green is the stores and the verifier, amber is the CDC reader and Kafka, purple is the backfill job, blue is the new service. 1. The monolith keeps writing to the legacy PostgreSQL, including to its outbox table. 2. The CDC reader tails the database log and sends each committed change, in commit order, to Kafka. 3. Kafka keeps the events keyed by entity id. 4. The new service consumes them idempotently and writes the new store. 5. The dashed top path is the backfill job copying old rows in chunks. 6. The verifier compares old and new continuously. The note at the top gives the order that matters: start CDC first so nothing is missed, backfill second, apply buffered events, then verify. Removing CDC makes the backfill stale the moment it ends. Removing the verifier leaves you hoping the copy is right when you switch writes.

8.3 Backfill without losing updates

The classic race: you copy a row, someone updates it on the old side, your copy is stale. The safe sequence is: (1) record the CDC log position and start capturing changes into Kafka, which retains them; (2) run the backfill in chunks by primary key range, throttled so the primary is not harmed (for example 5,000 rows per batch, pause when replica lag exceeds 5 seconds), reading from a replica; (3) apply the backfilled rows to the new store using "upsert only if the version is newer" so that a replayed change can never be overwritten by an older backfill row; (4) consume the buffered CDC events to catch up; (5) verify. Each row needs a version or last-updated timestamp for step 3, and if the old tables lack one, the CDC log sequence number serves.

8.5 Cross-service reporting

Finance used to run a SQL join across orders, payments and shipping. After the split that join is gone. Provide a read-only analytical copy (events streamed into a warehouse or lake, Chapter 37) and tell the business early. This is a common surprise on the day the shared database is switched off.

9. Deep dive 1: the checkout saga and inventory reservation

In the monolith, checkout is one database transaction: decrement stock, create the order, record the payment row, commit. Either everything happens or nothing does. After the split, stock lives in Inventory, the order in Orders, and the charge in Payments (and at an external PSP that cannot join our transaction). Two-phase commit across them is a poor fit: the PSP does not participate, locks are held across slow network calls, and a coordinator failure blocks everyone (Chapter 31). We use a saga instead (Chapter 48): a sequence of local transactions, each with a compensating action that semantically undoes it if a later step fails.

9.1 Orchestration versus choreography

OptionHow it worksProsCons
ChoreographyEach service reacts to the previous one's event: OrderCreated, then InventoryReserved, then PaymentCaptured...No central component; loose couplingThe flow is spread across five codebases; hard to see state, add a step, or debug a stuck order; cyclic dependencies appear
OrchestrationA Checkout orchestrator holds the state machine and tells each service what to doOne place shows the flow; explicit timeouts and retries; easy to observeOrchestrator is a central dependency and must be highly available; risk of putting business logic in it

Decision: orchestration for checkout (five participants, money involved, must be explainable to support staff), and choreography for the fan-out after the order is confirmed (notifications, shipping, analytics subscribe to OrderConfirmed). The orchestrator persists its state in its own database after every step, so a crashed instance can resume (Chapter 38 covers workflow engines).

9.3 Inventory reservation: the hard part

Overselling is the failure the business cares most about. The reservation protocol: Inventory keeps on_hand and reserved per SKU and warehouse. reserve executes one conditional, atomic statement: UPDATE stock SET reserved = reserved + :q WHERE sku = :s AND on_hand - reserved >= :q. If zero rows change, the stock is not there, and the caller gets "insufficient stock" immediately. The reservation row stores an expiry time (10 minutes), and a sweeper releases expired ones, because a customer who abandons the payment page must not hold stock forever. commit converts reserved into a permanent decrement of on_hand; release decrements reserved. All three are idempotent through the reservation id.

A doorbuster SKU with 1,000 attempts per second would serialize on one row. Options:

OptionHowTradeoff
Row-level conditional update (default)One atomic SQL statement; about 1,000 to 3,000 updates/s per hot row is achievable if the transaction is tiny and holds the lock for under 1 msSimple and exact; ceiling on a single hot row
Redis counter in frontAtomic decrement in Redis admits only as many requests as there is stock; DB updated asynchronouslyVery fast; adds a second source to reconcile; Redis loss means re-seed from DB
Split the stock into buckets1,000 units become 10 buckets of 100; a request picks a random bucket, falls back to the othersScales hot rows 10x; small chance of false "sold out" near zero, mitigated by fallback scan
Queue the flash saleA waiting room admits shoppers at the rate stock can be processedBest experience at extreme demand; product work needed

Choice: row-level conditional update for 99.9% of SKUs; bucketed stock plus a waiting room for flagged flash-sale SKUs. We accept a small "oversell tolerance" only for deliberately flagged low-value items with backorder, and never for flagged limited-edition ones.

9.4 Idempotency and the user-visible contract

The browser may double-click, the mobile app may retry on a dropped connection, and a gateway may retry on timeout. The client sends an Idempotency-Key (a UUID created when the checkout page opens). The orchestrator stores key to saga id, so a replay returns the existing saga status instead of starting a second one (Chapter 32). The PSP call uses the order id as its own idempotency key, so a retried authorization cannot double-charge. The response to the client is "order accepted" with a status URL, and the final confirmation arrives through polling or a push, so a slow step 6 never blocks the customer.

10. Deep dive 2: reads with API composition and CQRS

After splitting, a product page needs data from five owners: catalog (title, images), pricing and promotions (price and badge), inventory (in stock?), reviews (rating), and recommendations. Calling five services from the browser or from one fat handler per page view, at 8,000 pages per second at peak, means 40,000 internal calls per second, and the page is as slow as the slowest dependency. There are two techniques, and a mature design uses both.

10.1 API composition

A composer (a backend-for-frontend, BFF, or a gateway plugin) fans out calls in parallel, applies a per-call timeout, merges the results, and degrades gracefully: if reviews time out, render the page without stars. The page latency is the maximum of the parallel calls rather than their sum. Composition is simple, always fresh, and requires no extra data store, but it multiplies load on the owners and the tail latency (the p99 of a fan-out of five is worse than any single p99) grows with the number of calls. Use it for data that must be fresh and is cheap to fetch: live price, stock status for the buy button, and the cart.

10.2 CQRS read models

Command Query Responsibility Segregation (Chapter 36) keeps the write model (normalized, owned by each service) separate from one or more denormalized read models built by consuming the services' events. For the product page we maintain a "product view" document in a key-value store or search index: title, images, attributes, rating summary, price band, coarse availability, all in one record, rebuilt on any upstream event. A page view then reads one key (about 1 ms from cache) instead of calling five services. For order history, an Orders read model keeps one document per customer order with the denormalized status, shipment tracking number, and item titles, so "My orders" is one query and does not call Shipping 20 times.

Walkthrough. Left column: the write side services (blue), each owning its data. Middle: Kafka topics (amber), one keyed by SKU and one keyed by customer. Right: read models (green) and the consumers of them. 1. Catalog, Promotions, Inventory and Reviews publish change events, drawn as dashed arrows into the SKU topic. 2. A projector reads that topic and builds one Product view document per SKU. 3. The document goes into the cache and CDN with a TTL of 30 to 60 seconds. 4. 10.3 The cost of CQRS: eventual consistency

The read model lags the write model, typically under a second but unbounded during an incident. The case that hurts is read-your-writes: a customer places an order and lands on "My orders", which does not show it yet. Fixes: the confirmation page is rendered from the saga's response rather than from the read model; or the BFF merges the last few orders from the Orders service with the read model for the first minute; or the client carries a version token and the read waits until the projection reaches it. For the product page, minutes-old reviews are fine, but a price or stock status must never come from a stale read model for the purchase decision: the add-to-cart and checkout steps re-validate against the owners. Always rebuildable: because the read model is derived, a bug is fixed by replaying the event log from the start (keep at least 7 days in Kafka plus a snapshot).

Interview tip Say which data is allowed to be stale and which must not be. "Stars and descriptions: 60 seconds is fine. Price and stock on the checkout button: authoritative." That one sentence shows you understand why composition and CQRS coexist.

11. Sync versus async communication

The rule from Chapter 47: use a synchronous call when the caller cannot proceed without the answer, and asynchronous events when the caller only needs to announce a fact. Every synchronous edge couples availability: if A calls B synchronously and B is 99.9% available, A is capped at 99.9% for that path, and a chain of five such calls caps at about 99.5% (0.999 to the fifth power).

InteractionStyleReason

Avoid "asynchronous in disguise": a service that publishes a request message and then blocks waiting for a reply has all the coupling of a synchronous call plus extra latency. Also avoid event-carried chains where one event triggers a cascade of events nobody can trace; that is the failure mode of pure choreography.

12. Service discovery, mesh, resilience and timeout budgets

12.1 Discovery and mesh

With about 12 services and perhaps 150 instances, hardcoded addresses are not feasible. On Kubernetes, DNS-based discovery gives each service a stable name and the platform's service layer load-balances across healthy pods (Chapter 49). A service mesh (Chapter 50) adds sidecar or node proxies that provide mutual TLS between services, retries, timeouts, circuit breaking and uniform metrics with no application code. The tradeoff is real: extra hop latency (typically around 1 ms), more moving parts, and about 10 to 20% more CPU. We adopt the mesh at the second wave, when there are more than five services and a security need for mTLS, not on day one; before that, a shared client library and the gateway are sufficient.

12.2 Timeout budgets

The most common cascading failure is a slow dependency that nobody timed out. The principle (Chapter 51, Chapter 40): the caller's total deadline is passed downstream, and each hop's timeout must be smaller than the time remaining. The user is promised an answer in 3 seconds. The gateway cuts the request at 3,000 ms. The orchestrator budget is 2,500 ms, leaving room for the network and the response.

Walkthrough. Read it as nested bars: the longer the bar, the more time it may use. 1. The top purple bar is the gateway deadline of 3,000 ms, the promise to the user. 2. Inside it, the amber bar is the orchestrator's 2,500 ms, leaving 500 ms for network and the response. 3. Below are the sequential step timeouts: price 200, reserve 300, order 200, PSP authorize 1,500 (red, the long pole) and confirm 200. 4. The sum is 2,400 ms, which fits inside 2,500. The number that matters is that sum: if you add a step you must take time from another or raise the budget. The notes explain that a retry is allowed only when budget remains, and past the budget the client gets "processing" while the saga finishes in the background. Drawing it this way avoids every hop using its own default of 30 seconds. Remove the budget and one slow PSP call stalls threads upstream and spreads the outage.

12.3 Resilience patterns we apply, and where

  • Timeouts everywhere, derived from the budget above, never the default of "infinite" or 30 seconds.
  • Retries with exponential backoff and jitter, only for idempotent calls and with a retry budget (for example no more than 10% extra load), to avoid retry storms during incidents.
  • Circuit breakers on the PSP, reviews, recommendations and the ACL calls into the monolith: after a failure threshold, fail fast and use a fallback (hide stars, show cached recommendations).
  • Bulkheads: separate connection pools and thread pools per dependency; this is precisely what the monolith lacked and what caused the reviews-kills-checkout outage.
  • Load shedding and rate limiting at the gateway (Chapter 16): prefer rejecting bots and low-priority traffic (reviews, recommendations) so checkout keeps capacity. Priority classes: checkout over cart over browse over everything else.
  • Graceful degradation switches (feature flags): "serve product page from static cache", "disable recommendations", "disable review submission". Practiced before Black Friday, not invented during it.

13. Observability, testing and deployment

13.1 Observability

The first day after splitting, a slow checkout has twenty possible causes across six services. Without good telemetry (Chapter 52, Chapter 54), the migration makes things worse, so the platform team ships these before the first extraction: a correlation id (and W3C trace context) generated at the gateway and carried through HTTP, gRPC and Kafka headers; structured logs with that id; RED metrics (rate, errors, duration) for every service and every outbound dependency; distributed traces sampled at 1% plus all errors and all requests over 2 seconds; and business metrics such as orders per minute, checkout success rate and reservation-to-order conversion. Alert on SLO burn rate rather than on raw error counts: for a 99.9% checkout SLO, page when the burn rate would exhaust the monthly budget within about 2 hours. During migration there is an additional class: parity dashboards (old versus new result diff rate, replication lag, shadow-traffic mismatches).

13.2 Testing

The 3-hour end-to-end regression suite is the first thing to retire; it cannot scale to a dozen services. Replace it with the test pyramid (Chapter 53): fast unit tests per service; consumer-driven contract tests, where each consumer publishes what it expects from a provider's API or event and the provider's pipeline fails if a change breaks it; a few end-to-end smoke journeys (browse, cart, pay in a sandbox) run after each deploy; and production verification through canaries and synthetic transactions. For the migration itself, the highest-value technique is shadow traffic (dark launching): the gateway sends each read to both the monolith and the new service, returns the monolith's answer, and compares the two asynchronously. A mismatch rate under 0.01% for a week is the exit criterion for switching reads.

13.3 Deployment

Each service has its own pipeline building a container image, running tests, and rolling out through canary (Chapter 43): 1% of traffic for 10 minutes, then 10%, then 50%, then 100%, with automatic rollback when error rate or latency crosses thresholds. Database schema changes follow the expand-and-contract pattern: first add the new column or table (compatible with old code), deploy code that writes both and reads new, backfill, then remove the old, in separate releases. Feature flags decouple deploying from releasing. Autoscaling uses request rate and queue lag as signals; the catalog read model and search scale out horizontally and are pre-scaled before Black Friday, because autoscaling takes minutes, and a flash-sale ramp takes seconds.

14. Security

Splitting a monolith widens the attack surface: in-process calls become network calls that anyone inside the cluster could make (Chapter 55).

  • Edge authentication: users authenticate once at the gateway (OAuth 2.0 / OIDC); the gateway validates the token and forwards a short-lived signed internal JWT containing user id and scopes. Services authorize against that, never trust a user id passed in a body.
  • Service-to-service identity: mutual TLS from the mesh with per-service certificates rotated automatically (hours, not years), plus network policies so that only Checkout can call Payments' authorization endpoint. Reviews has no route to Payments at all.
  • Least privilege on data: each service has database credentials only for its own schema. This is also the enforcement mechanism for "nobody reads my tables".
  • PCI scope: card numbers go from the browser directly to the PSP's hosted fields and return a token; no service but Payments ever sees even the token. Keeping that boundary narrow keeps the other eleven services out of audit scope.
  • PII and GDPR: user data now lives in several stores (orders keep addresses, reviews keep names). Deleting a user is a saga too: Identity publishes UserErased, each service anonymizes its copy, and a ledger shows completion. Orders needed for tax law are anonymized, not deleted.
  • Abuse: bots hoarding flash-sale stock. Rate limit per user and per IP at the gateway, cap quantity per order, and shorten reservation TTL for flagged sessions.
  • Secrets: a secret manager with per-service access, never in images or config files.

17. Cost, team size and honest reality

Microservices are not free, and an interviewer will value a candidate who says so. Rough, defensible numbers for this scenario:

  • Infrastructure: more small deployments, sidecars, per-service databases, Kafka and observability storage. Expect about 30 to 60% higher infrastructure spend than the monolith at the same traffic. Part of this is offset by scaling only the hot parts (catalog reads, search) instead of all 12 monolith instances, and by shrinking the 64-vCPU primary once reads leave it.
  • People: a platform team of about 6 (10% of engineers) is the real price; below roughly 30 engineers, that overhead often outweighs the benefit, and a modular monolith is the better answer.
  • Cognitive load: each team owns about 1 to 2 services. A rule of thumb is that an on-call engineer can hold about 3 to 5 services in their head; more means shared rotations and weaker ownership.
  • Time: 11 months to extract the first nine contexts is aggressive; many real programs take 2 to 3 years. Scope the first year to the value-bearing contexts and consciously stop.
  • Data cost: duplicated read models multiply storage, but at 20 GB catalog and 40 GB reviews this is negligible. The real cost is the engineering time to keep projections correct.

The honest end state is not twelve services plus nothing. It is about 9 to 11 services and a residual "core commerce" monolith that still holds the back office, admin, and low-change utilities. That is a good outcome, not a failure.

18. Evolution and alternatives not chosen

  • At 10x scale (six million orders on a peak day, 250 orders per second sustained): shard Orders and Inventory by customer or SKU (Chapter 25), move to a cell-based layout where each cell serves a slice of customers end to end (Chapter 42), and go multi-region with the cart and catalog active-active but money paths pinned to a home region per customer (Chapter 26).
  • Alternative: modular monolith only. Enforce boundaries inside one deployable with architecture tests and per-module schemas. It would solve ownership and half the coupling at much lower cost; we choose to go further only because failure isolation and flash-sale scaling need separate processes.
  • Alternative: big-bang rewrite. Rejected: no incremental value, hidden requirements lost, and long periods of parallel maintenance.
  • Alternative: shared database across services. Rejected as an end state: it keeps schema coupling. It is, however, a legitimate short-lived intermediate step ("shared DB, separate code") before the data split.
  • Alternative: two-phase commit instead of a saga. Rejected because the PSP cannot participate and locks held during a 1.5 s external call limit throughput.
  • Alternative: buy. Identity, search, and payments orchestration are candidates for managed products; "buy for generic subdomains, build for differentiating ones" is a legitimate answer.

19. Interview Q&A

How do you decide the service boundaries, and how do you know you got them wrong?

I start with domain analysis: event storming with the business to find where terminology changes (a "product" in catalog versus inventory versus orders), then classify subdomains as core, supporting or generic, and align them with teams. A boundary is good when most changes touch one service, when it has a clear owner, and when its API is small and stable. Symptoms of a wrong boundary: two services that always change and deploy together, a chatty pair making dozens of calls per request, or a service that needs another's tables. The fix is usually to merge them back, which is cheap early and expensive later, which is exactly why we extract low-coupling contexts first and learn.

Why start with Reviews and Notifications rather than Checkout, which is where the pain is?

Because the first extraction teaches you how to extract, and the cost of a mistake should be small while you learn. Reviews and Notifications have low coupling, tolerate degradation, and are not on the money path, so a bug costs some stars rather than orders. They also deliver real value: reviews was the source of two checkout outages through shared pools, and about 70% of primary query time is read traffic we can move. By the time we reach checkout we have a gateway, CDC, tracing, contract tests and a practiced rollback, all proven on harmless traffic.

How do you move the data without downtime or lost writes?

For every context: make the boundary explicit in code, cut cross-table foreign keys and joins, start CDC to capture changes in commit order, backfill history in throttled chunks with version-checked upserts, consume the buffered changes to catch up, and then run a continuous verifier comparing both sides. Reads switch first, in shadow mode, then writes switch behind a flag, with reverse replication kept for a rollback window. I avoid long-lived dual writes because the two writes are not atomic and will silently diverge. Where the business needs a deliberate event, I use the transactional outbox so the event commits atomically with the state change.

Walk me through what happens if the payment is authorized but the order service is down.

In my ordering, the order is created as PENDING before payment authorization, so that case does not arise in that form. If Orders fails at step 3, the orchestrator releases the stock reservation and returns an error without ever contacting the PSP. If Orders fails after authorization at the confirm step, the orchestrator retries the confirmation, since it is idempotent, for as long as the budget allows, and then reports "processing" to the client. If it cannot complete, a compensation voids the authorization and releases stock. A reconciliation job comparing PSP authorizations to confirmed orders catches anything that slips through, and also voids orphan holds.

How do you prevent overselling during a flash sale?

Reserving is a single atomic conditional update that only succeeds if available stock covers the quantity, so concurrent buyers cannot both win the last unit. Reservations expire after about 10 minutes so abandoned carts release stock. For one extremely hot SKU, a single row caps throughput at roughly a few thousand updates a second, so I bucket the stock across several rows or put a waiting room in front to admit buyers at a rate the system can process. Nightly audits compare on-hand, reserved and committed counts with orders, and the business sets an explicit oversell tolerance only for backorderable items.

Why use orchestration for checkout but choreography afterward?

Checkout is a short, ordered process with money, compensations and strict timeouts, and people must be able to ask "where is this order stuck?" A central state machine answers that directly and makes timeouts explicit. After confirmation, many consumers independently react (email, shipping, analytics, loyalty points) and none affects the others; adding one should not require changing the checkout code. So events decouple them naturally. The rule of thumb: orchestrate when there is a business transaction with compensations, and choreograph when it is a fan-out of independent reactions.

How does a product page stay fast when its data lives in five services?

Most of it does not need to be fetched live. I project catalog content, rating summary, promotion badge and coarse availability into one denormalized product-view document updated by events, cache it with a short TTL behind the CDN and gateway, and read one key per page. Only volatile and decision-critical fields, such as current price and buy-button stock status, are composed in parallel at request time with tight timeouts and fallbacks. This keeps 8,000 page views per second from becoming 40,000 internal calls per second, and a reviews outage just hides the stars.

What is the biggest risk of this migration, and how do you control it?

The biggest risks are organizational and about data, not technical. Technically, it is moving the write path of orders, stock and payments without losing or duplicating anything. I control it by ordering the work from low to high risk, shadow-testing before switching, running reconciliation against the PSP, canarying by cohort, rate-limiting to a maximum of two concurrent extractions, and freezing in October so Black Friday runs on a state we have already proven. Organizationally, the risk is building a distributed monolith, which I counter with team-aligned boundaries, contract tests, and a rule that every new synchronous dependency needs review.

How do you handle a query that used to be a SQL join, such as "all orders with product titles and shipping status"?

Three options in order of preference. First, denormalize at write time: the order line stores the title and price at purchase time, which is also semantically correct. Second, build a CQRS read model fed by order and shipping events, so the history page is one lookup. Third, for rare or internal queries, compose in the BFF or use the analytics warehouse. I would not reintroduce a cross-service join through a shared read replica because that recreates the schema coupling we are removing.

When would you advise a company not to do this migration?

If the team is under about 30 engineers, the pain is mainly code organization, there is no platform capability for CI/CD, tracing and containers, or the scale is modest. A modular monolith with enforced boundaries, a separate read replica for reporting, and a clear ownership map delivers most of the benefit at a fraction of the cost. Microservices pay off when independent scaling, independent release cadence and failure isolation are real, measured needs. I would say that to the CTO in plain terms, with the numbers from this estimate.

How do you keep the new services from calling the monolith in a way that recreates coupling?

All access goes through an anti-corruption layer with a narrow, versioned interface, and calls to it are counted on a dashboard with a target of falling to zero. The monolith's tables are made read-only to the new service's credentials, so a direct query fails in test. Each extraction ends by deleting the corresponding code in the monolith, not just disabling it, because dead but reachable code is how the old path quietly returns.

20. Connections and recap

Recap

  • Do not start with "split everything". Name the pains, separate those needing a network boundary from those needing discipline, and keep a small residual monolith.
  • Boundaries come from bounded contexts and team ownership. Extract low-coupling, low-risk, read-heavy contexts first (Notifications, Reviews, Search, Catalog reads), and the money path last.
  • Put a gateway in front first. Routing rules give canary, shadow and instant rollback, and an anti-corruption layer keeps the legacy model from leaking.
  • Data: break foreign keys, CDC plus chunked backfill plus verification, outbox for deliberate events, no long-lived dual writes, one source of truth per entity.
  • Checkout is an orchestrated saga with compensations and a pivot, atomic conditional inventory reservations with TTL, and idempotency keys end to end.
  • Reads: CQRS projections for slow-changing shared content, API composition only for the volatile fields, and an explicit list of what may be stale.
  • Resilience comes from a propagated timeout budget (3 s at the client, 2.5 s orchestrator, 1.5 s PSP), bulkheads, breakers, retry budgets, shedding and rehearsed degradation switches.
  • Operate it: tracing and SLO burn alerts before extraction, contract tests instead of a regression suite, canary deploys, mTLS and per-service data credentials.
  • Be honest about cost: roughly 30 to 60% more infrastructure and a platform team of about 10% of engineers; end state is 9 to 11 services plus a residual core.
