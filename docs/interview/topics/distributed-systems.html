Mental model: the 8 ideas everything hangs on

One line: a distributed system is many computers that can fail separately, talking over a network that can lose, delay, duplicate and reorder messages, with no shared clock. Every hard question is about what you do when you cannot tell "slow" from "dead".
  1. Partial failure. One machine either works or crashes. In a distributed system, part of it can be broken while the rest runs, and you often cannot tell which part.
  2. Unreliable network. A message can be lost, delayed, delivered twice, or arrive out of order. If you sent a request and got no reply, you do not know whether it ran.
  3. No shared clock. Each machine has its own clock, and they drift. You cannot trust "timestamp A is before timestamp B" across machines.
  4. Timeouts are guesses. A timeout cannot prove a node is dead. It only says "I stopped waiting".
  5. Replicate for availability, partition for scale. Copies of data survive failures but must be kept in step. Splitting data across nodes gives size and throughput but makes cross-node operations hard.
  6. Agreement needs a majority. To decide one value safely (leader, commit, lock owner) nodes use a quorum: a majority. Two majorities always overlap, so two conflicting decisions cannot both win.
  7. At-least-once plus idempotency beats "exactly once". Retry until you hear back, and make repeats harmless.
  8. Everything is a trade-off. Consistency, availability, latency, cost. A strong answer names what you give up.

1. Failure models and the eight fallacies

What is a distributed system, and why is it harder than a single-machine program? Basic

What the interviewer is testing: do you understand that the difficulty is failure and uncertainty, not just "more servers".

A distributed system is a set of independent computers that cooperate over a network to look like one system. The reason it is harder is not the number of machines. It is that the parts fail independently and the network between them is unreliable. On one machine, a function call either returns or the process dies, and everyone dies together. Across machines you get partial failure: the call may have worked, may have failed, or may still be running, and you cannot know. There is also no global clock and no shared memory, so "what happened first" and "what is the current value" are hard questions. We accept this pain because we need things one machine cannot give: more capacity than one box, survival when a box dies, and low latency by being close to users.

Likely follow-ups
  • Name two reasons you would still choose one big machine. Simplicity and strong consistency for free. Many systems should stay on one database until they truly cannot.
  • Is a microservice system distributed? Yes. Two services and a database already have network calls that can fail halfway.
What are the eight fallacies of distributed computing? Which ones have hurt you most? Basic

What the interviewer is testing: whether you have real scars, not just the list.

The fallacies, from Peter Deutsch and colleagues at Sun, are false beliefs people hold. (1) The network is reliable. (2) Latency is zero. (3) Bandwidth is infinite. (4) The network is secure. (5) Topology does not change. (6) There is one administrator. (7) Transport cost is zero. (8) The network is homogeneous. In my experience the first two cause most bugs. Believing the network is reliable gives you code with no timeouts and no retries, and then one stuck call hangs a thread pool. Believing latency is zero gives you chatty designs: a page that makes 40 sequential service calls works on a laptop and is slow in production, because 40 calls at 5 ms is already 200 ms. Number 5 bites during deployments and autoscaling, when IP addresses change and cached DNS or long-lived connections go stale. Number 7 shows up as cloud cost: cross-zone and cross-region traffic is billed.

Likely follow-ups
  • How do you design against fallacy 2? Batch calls, fetch in parallel, cache, and move data close to the code that uses it.
  • Fallacy 6 example? A dependency owned by another team changes its rate limit or deploys at a different time, and you cannot coordinate it.
What failure models do we design for: crash-stop, crash-recovery, omission, Byzantine? Intermediate

What the interviewer is testing: do you know that an algorithm's guarantees depend on which failures it assumes.

A failure model is the list of bad things we assume can happen. Crash-stop: a node works correctly, then stops forever. Easy to reason about, rare in real life. Crash-recovery: a node can crash and come back, with its disk data but not its memory. This is the model real databases use, and it is why they write to a log before replying. Omission: a node or the network drops some messages. Timing or performance failures: the node is correct but very slow, for example a long garbage collection pause. Byzantine: a node can behave arbitrarily, even lie, because of bugs or attackers. Most business systems assume crash-recovery plus omission and do not defend against Byzantine behaviour. Byzantine tolerance is used where participants do not trust each other, like blockchains, and it costs more: you need 3f+1 nodes to survive f liars, versus 2f+1 for crashes.

Likely follow-ups
  • Why is a slow node worse than a dead one? A dead node is detected and replaced. A slow node still answers sometimes, holds locks and connections, and drags everyone down.
  • Is Raft Byzantine tolerant? No. It assumes nodes follow the protocol and only crash or lose messages.
What is the Two Generals problem, and what does it tell us about exactly-once delivery? Intermediate

What the interviewer is testing: the theoretical root of "you cannot be sure the other side got it".

Two generals on two hills must attack at the same time. They can only send messengers across a valley where messengers may be caught. General A sends "attack at dawn". A cannot attack unless B confirms. B sends a confirmation, but now B does not know if A got it, so B wants a confirmation of the confirmation, and so on forever. No finite number of messages gives both sides certainty over a lossy link. The lesson for engineers: when a message or its acknowledgement can be lost, the sender can never be fully sure the receiver acted. So you cannot get a guarantee of "exactly one execution" from the network alone. You choose: retry (at-least-once, risk of duplicates) or do not retry (at-most-once, risk of loss) and fix duplicates at the receiver with idempotency.

Likely follow-ups
  • How is this different from FLP? Two Generals is about lossy links. FLP is about agreement among nodes when even one may crash and timing is unbounded.
What does the FLP impossibility result say, and how do real systems get around it? Senior

What the interviewer is testing: whether you can state the theorem correctly and not overclaim.

FLP (Fischer, Lynch, Paterson, 1985) says: in a fully asynchronous system, where message delays have no upper bound, no deterministic algorithm can guarantee that nodes reach agreement if even one node may crash. The reason is that you cannot tell a crashed node from a very slow one, so you can wait forever. It does not say consensus is impossible in practice. It says you cannot have both safety (never decide two different values) and guaranteed liveness (always eventually decide) in that model. Real protocols like Paxos and Raft keep safety always, and give up guaranteed liveness: they make progress only when the network is "well-behaved enough" for a while. They use timeouts and randomisation, for example random election timeouts in Raft, to make livelock very unlikely. So the honest statement is: these systems never give a wrong answer, but may pause under bad conditions.

Likely follow-ups
  • Which property do you drop in practice? Liveness under bad timing. Safety is never traded.
Common mistakes: saying "FLP proves consensus is impossible". It is impossible to guarantee termination in the pure asynchronous model with deterministic algorithms.
How can you tell whether a remote node is dead or just slow? Intermediate

What the interviewer is testing: the central honesty of distributed systems.

You cannot tell for sure. From outside, a crashed node, a node in a long GC pause, a congested network link and a node that is overloaded all look the same: no reply. What you can do is decide using a rule. The simplest rule is a timeout: if no heartbeat in N seconds, treat the node as failed. A short timeout detects crashes fast but gives false alarms during hiccups. A long timeout is stable but slow to react. Better detectors, such as phi accrual, turn silence into a suspicion level that adapts to normal network jitter. Then, because you might be wrong, the design must stay safe when a "dead" node comes back and acts. That is why leaders use terms and fencing tokens: the old leader can wake up and must be rejected. Failure detection gives you a hint for liveness. Safety must never depend on it being correct.

Likely follow-ups
  • What is a false positive here? Declaring a healthy node dead. It triggers failover and may cause two leaders if safety is not protected.
If each of three services has 99.9% availability and a request needs all three, what is the availability? How do you improve it? Intermediate

What the interviewer is testing: simple reliability arithmetic and a feel for dependency chains.

When all are required (in series) you multiply: 0.999 × 0.999 × 0.999 is about 0.997, so 99.7%. That is about 26 hours of downtime a year instead of 8.8 hours. The more hard dependencies on the request path, the worse it gets. When you have redundant copies and any one is enough (in parallel), failure needs all to fail: two independent 99.9% replicas give 1 − 0.001 × 0.001 = 99.9999%. The catch is "independent": two replicas in one rack, behind one bug, or on one deploy pipeline fail together. To improve a chain: remove dependencies from the critical path, make some optional with fallbacks (cached data, default values), add redundant copies in separate failure domains, and use timeouts so a slow dependency does not turn into an outage for all callers.

Likely follow-ups
  • Why is a 99.99% claim from a vendor still risky? It is measured by the vendor, often monthly, and excludes planned maintenance and "partial" brownouts.

2. CAP, PACELC and consistency models

Explain the CAP theorem correctly. Why is "pick two of three" misleading? Basic

What the interviewer is testing: the precise statement. Many candidates only recite a triangle.

CAP (Brewer's conjecture, proven by Gilbert and Lynch in 2002) says: when a network partition happens, a system must choose between consistency and availability. Here "consistency" means linearizability: every read sees the latest completed write, as if there were one copy. "Availability" means every request to a working node gets a non-error answer. "Partition" means nodes cannot talk to each other. The triangle "pick two" is misleading because partitions are not a choice. Networks do fail, so you cannot give up P. The real choice is only during a partition: refuse some requests (keep consistency, lose availability) or answer with possibly stale data (keep availability, lose consistency). When there is no partition, CAP says nothing, and you can have both. Also the words are narrow: CAP "consistency" is not the C of ACID, and CAP "availability" does not mean 99.99% uptime.

Likely follow-ups
  • Is "CA" a real category? Not for a system spread over a network. A single-node database is "CA" only because it has no partitions to survive.
  • Does CAP mean a CP system is down all the time? No. It is only unavailable on the minority side of a partition. The majority side keeps serving.
Common mistakes: saying "MongoDB is CP, Cassandra is AP" as a fixed label. Both depend on configuration (write concern, consistency level).
What does PACELC add to CAP, and where do common databases fall? Intermediate

What the interviewer is testing: whether you think about the normal case, not only the failure case.

Daniel Abadi's PACELC says: if there is a Partition, choose Availability or Consistency (that is CAP); Else, when running normally, choose between Latency and Consistency. The second half matters more day to day, because partitions are rare but every request pays latency. Keeping replicas strongly consistent means waiting for them: a synchronous write to a replica in another region adds tens of milliseconds. Answering from the nearest replica is fast but may be stale. Typical placements: Dynamo-style stores such as Cassandra and DynamoDB (eventually consistent reads) are PA/EL: available in a partition, low latency otherwise. Google Spanner and etcd are PC/EC: consistent in both cases, and they pay in latency. MongoDB with default settings is often described as PA/EC. Yahoo PNUTS is PC/EL. Treat these as defaults only, since most systems can be tuned per request.

Likely follow-ups
  • Which half of PACELC do you meet most in production? The ELC half: how many replicas do I wait for on each read and write.
Walk me through the consistency models from strongest to weakest. Intermediate

What the interviewer is testing: a clear mental ladder, with an example of each rung.

Linearizable (strong): every operation appears to happen at one instant between its start and finish, and all clients agree on the order. Once a write returns, everyone sees it. It behaves like one copy. Sequential: all clients see the same single order of operations and each client's own order is kept, but that order need not match real time, so you can read slightly old data. Causal: operations that are causally related (a reply to a comment) are seen in order by everyone, while unrelated operations may appear in different orders on different nodes. Session guarantees (read-your-writes, monotonic reads) give a single user a sensible view. Eventual: if writes stop, all replicas converge to the same value, with no promise about what you read meanwhile. Stronger models are easier to program against and cost more latency and availability. Many systems let you pick per request.

Likely follow-ups
  • Which is the strongest model that stays available during a partition? Causal consistency (with sticky sessions) is the strongest known model achievable with high availability.
What exactly is linearizability, and how is it different from serializability? Senior

What the interviewer is testing: two words people mix up. A senior engineer separates them cleanly.

Linearizability is about single objects and real time. Each read or write seems to take effect at one point between its call and its return, and once a write has returned, every later read sees it. It is a "recency" guarantee. Serializability is about transactions touching many objects. The result must equal some serial order of the transactions, but that order need not match real time. So a serializable database can show you an old snapshot and still be correct as a serial order. A database that gives both is called strict serializable (Spanner calls it external consistency). A good example: a bank transfer needs serializability so two transactions do not interleave badly. A leader lock needs linearizability so two clients never both believe they own it. Linearizability costs coordination for each operation: you need a quorum or a single leader, so it is slower and unavailable on the minority side of a partition.

Likely follow-ups
  • Is snapshot isolation serializable? No. It allows write skew. It is also not about real time.
  • Where do you really need linearizability? Locks, leader election, unique constraints (username taken), and anything where "I just wrote it, now you read it" must be exact.
What does "eventually consistent" really promise, and what does it not? Basic

What the interviewer is testing: whether you understand it is a weak but real guarantee.

It promises only this: if no new writes arrive, all copies will in the end hold the same value. It does not say how long that takes, and it says nothing about what you read in the meantime. You may read old data, you may read new data then old data again (going back in time), and two users may see different values. Real systems add detail: typical convergence is milliseconds to seconds, and mechanisms like read repair and anti-entropy make it happen. It is a good fit for like counts, feeds, product catalogue views and shopping-cart merges. It is a bad fit for money balances, stock counts that must not oversell, and unique usernames, unless you add a stronger mechanism such as conditional writes or quorums. Conflicts need a rule: last-write-wins, version vectors, or merge functions.

Likely follow-ups
  • Does eventual consistency mean data loss? Not if conflicts are resolved by merging. Last-write-wins can silently drop an update, though.
What are read-your-writes, monotonic reads and consistent prefix? How do you provide them? Intermediate

What the interviewer is testing: the user-facing symptoms of replication lag, and practical fixes.

These are session guarantees, which protect one user from confusing behaviour on an asynchronously replicated store. Read-your-writes: after I post a comment, I see it. Without it, my write goes to the leader and my next read hits a lagging follower, so my comment "vanishes". Monotonic reads: I never see time go backwards. Without it, one read hits a fresh replica and the next a stale one, so a comment appears then disappears. Consistent prefix: I see writes in an order that could have happened: a question before its answer, never the answer first. Practical fixes: read from the leader for data the user just changed, or for a short window after a write; pin a user's session to one replica; or pass a version token (a log position) and only read from a replica that has caught up to it. The token approach is common: write returns a position, the client sends it with the next read.

Likely follow-ups
  • Cross-device read-your-writes? Sticky sessions break. You need the token stored server-side per user, or route by user id to the leader.
What is causal consistency and why is it useful? Intermediate

What the interviewer is testing: do you know the practical middle ground between strong and eventual.

Causal consistency promises that if one operation could have influenced another, every node shows them in that order. Alice posts a photo. Bob comments on it. Nobody should see Bob's comment without the photo, because the comment "depends on" the photo. But two unrelated posts by different users may show up in different orders on different nodes, and that is fine. The reason it is useful: it matches human expectation of "cause and effect" and, unlike linearizability, it can stay available during a partition, because it needs no global ordering, only tracking of dependencies. It is implemented by attaching dependency metadata (a vector clock or a causal token) to writes, and by delaying the visibility of a write until the things it depends on are visible. MongoDB offers causally consistent sessions. Research systems such as COPS popularised it.

Likely follow-ups
  • What is the cost? Metadata per write, and delayed visibility while waiting for dependencies.
Does reading and writing at a quorum give you linearizability? Senior

What the interviewer is testing: a well-known subtle trap in Dynamo-style stores.

Not by itself. With N replicas, if write quorum W plus read quorum R is bigger than N, any read set overlaps any write set in at least one node. So a read contacts at least one node that has the latest acknowledged write. That gives "usually fresh" reads, but not linearizability. Reasons: (1) with sloppy quorums, writes can land on nodes outside the usual replica set, so the overlap guarantee disappears. (2) Concurrent writes with last-write-wins and skewed clocks may pick the wrong winner. (3) A write that fails halfway may be on some replicas but not acknowledged; readers may see it, then not see it. (4) Two readers can disagree during a write in progress: reader A sees the new value at one node, reader B then reads a different set and sees the old one, which breaks the "once seen, always seen" rule. Fixing this takes read repair that blocks before returning, or a consensus-based approach, such as Cassandra lightweight transactions.

Likely follow-ups
  • What does Cassandra do for compare-and-set? Lightweight transactions use Paxos to agree on the order for one partition.
Cassandra, DynamoDB and MongoDB let you tune consistency per request. How do you choose? Intermediate

What the interviewer is testing: practical judgement, not memorised labels.

I ask: what is the cost of a stale or lost answer for this specific operation? For a product page view, an old price for a second is fine, so read from one replica (Cassandra ONE, DynamoDB eventually consistent read). For checking inventory before a purchase or reading a just-written order, I need fresh data: Cassandra QUORUM or LOCAL_QUORUM (a majority in the local datacenter, which avoids cross-region latency), DynamoDB a strongly consistent read, MongoDB readConcern: majority with w: majority writes. For safety-critical writes such as payments, I use majority writes so an acknowledged write survives a primary failover. For multi-region, I prefer local quorum plus asynchronous cross-region replication, and I accept that a regional disaster may lose the last moments. I also write down the choice per operation, because defaults differ by driver and version.

Likely follow-ups
  • What does MongoDB w:1 risk? An acknowledged write that only the old primary had may be rolled back after a failover.
What is the difference between the C in ACID and the C in CAP? Basic

What the interviewer is testing: a quick check for sloppy vocabulary.

They are unrelated. The C in ACID means a transaction moves the database from one valid state to another valid state: your rules, such as foreign keys, unique constraints and "balance is never negative", are not broken by a committed transaction. It is partly the application's job, since the database only enforces constraints you declared. The C in CAP means linearizability: replicas behave like a single copy and every read sees the latest write. One is about data rules inside a transaction, the other about agreement between copies over a network. Also separate "isolation" (the I in ACID, how concurrent transactions see each other) from both. If someone says "this database is consistent", ask which one they mean. BASE ("basically available, soft state, eventually consistent") is a marketing contrast to ACID. It is not a precise technical term.

3. Time, clocks and ordering

Why can't you trust wall-clock time in a distributed system? Basic

What the interviewer is testing: the root cause behind many subtle bugs.

Each machine has its own quartz clock, and they run at slightly different speeds (drift), so two machines slowly disagree. NTP (Network Time Protocol) corrects them, but only to about a few milliseconds on a good LAN and tens of milliseconds, sometimes more, over the internet. NTP can also step a clock backwards or forwards when it corrects a big error, so "time" can jump. Leap seconds, virtual machine pauses, and a laptop sleeping add more trouble. The result: if node A stamps an event 10:00:00.005 and node B stamps another 10:00:00.003, you cannot say B happened first. Two rules follow. Never use wall-clock timestamps from different machines to decide ordering or "who wins". And for measuring durations (timeouts, elapsed time) use a monotonic clock, which only moves forward, such as System.nanoTime() in Java, never currentTimeMillis().

Likely follow-ups
  • What can wall-clock time still be used for? Logs for humans, rough TTLs with slack, and business timestamps ("order placed at").
How does NTP work, and what can go wrong with it? Intermediate

What the interviewer is testing: do you know the clock sync mechanism and its failure modes.

A client asks a time server for the time and records four timestamps: when it sent the request, when the server received it, when the server replied, and when the client got the reply. From these it estimates network round-trip delay and the clock offset, assuming the delay is the same each way. It then slews the clock (speeds it up or slows it down slightly) for small errors, or steps it for big ones. Servers form layers called strata, with the most accurate (atomic or GPS clocks) at stratum 0. What can go wrong: asymmetric network paths make the offset estimate wrong; a misconfigured or malicious server gives bad time; a firewall blocks NTP and the clock slowly drifts for weeks; a VM is paused and resumes with a stale clock; and leap seconds cause a repeated or missing second, which has crashed software. Cloud providers offer better sources, and some "smear" a leap second over many hours to avoid a jump.

Likely follow-ups
  • How big can the error get? Without monitoring, a drifting clock can be off by seconds or more. Alert on offset.
What is the happens-before relation? Intermediate

What the interviewer is testing: the idea behind logical clocks.

Leslie Lamport defined it in 1978 as a way to order events without a clock. Event A happens-before event B if: (1) A and B are on the same process and A comes first; or (2) A is the sending of a message and B is its receipt; or (3) there is a chain of such steps (it is transitive). If neither A happens-before B nor B before A, the two events are concurrent: they could not have influenced each other, so no order between them is meaningful. This is "causal order": a partial order, not a total one. It is the right concept for questions like "did this write see that write?" Logical clocks are tools to capture it: Lamport clocks give a number consistent with it, and vector clocks capture it exactly, so you can tell "before" apart from "concurrent".

Likely follow-ups
  • Are concurrent events a problem? They are a conflict if they touch the same data. You must merge or pick a winner by a rule.
How do Lamport clocks work, and what can't they tell you? Intermediate

What the interviewer is testing: can you run the algorithm by hand.

Each process keeps a counter. Before each local event or send, it increments the counter. A message carries the sender's counter. On receive, the receiver sets its counter to the maximum of its own and the message's, then adds one. This guarantees: if A happens-before B, then L(A) is less than L(B). But the reverse is false: L(A) less than L(B) does not mean A caused B; they might be concurrent. So Lamport timestamps give a total order consistent with causality (break ties by process id), which is enough for things like a fair ordering of lock requests, but they cannot detect concurrency. For that you need vector clocks.

Likely follow-ups
  • Where are Lamport clocks used? Ordering in distributed mutual exclusion, and as the logical part of other schemes such as hybrid logical clocks.
How do vector clocks work, and how are they used to detect conflicts? Senior

What the interviewer is testing: the comparison rule and the practical cost.

A vector clock is an array with one counter per node. A node increments its own slot when it does an event. A message carries the whole vector, and the receiver takes the element-wise maximum with its own, then increments its own slot. To compare two vectors A and B: if every slot of A is less than or equal to B's, and at least one is less, then A happened-before B. If the reverse holds, B before A. If neither holds (A is bigger in some slot, B in another), the events are concurrent: a conflict. Dynamo used this to detect concurrent writes to a key: the store keeps both versions ("siblings") and returns them, and the client or application merges them. The cost: the vector grows with the number of writers, so systems prune it or use per-replica "dots" (dotted version vectors, used in Riak) to keep size bounded. Many popular systems avoid the complexity and use last-write-wins instead, accepting lost updates.

Likely follow-ups
  • Vector clock versus version vector? A version vector tracks versions of data items across replicas. A vector clock orders events across processes. The mechanics are the same.
What is a hybrid logical clock (HLC), and why do CockroachDB and MongoDB use it? Senior

What the interviewer is testing: modern practice. HLC is how real databases get "mostly real time" ordering without special hardware.

An HLC timestamp is a pair: a physical part, close to wall-clock time, and a small logical counter. On a local event, the physical part becomes the maximum of the node's clock and the last HLC value; if the physical part did not move forward, the counter increases. On receiving a message, the node takes the maximum of its own physical part, the message's physical part and the clock, and adjusts the counter. This gives you timestamps that (1) respect causality like a Lamport clock, so effect is always bigger than cause, and (2) stay within the clock-skew bound of real time, so they are human-readable and usable for "show me data as of 10:00" queries. And they are compact: one 64 bit-ish value, unlike vector clocks. CockroachDB uses HLC for transaction timestamps (with a configured maximum clock offset, 500 ms by default, beyond which a node shuts itself down), and MongoDB uses a hybrid cluster time for causal sessions.

Likely follow-ups
  • Does HLC give linearizability? Not alone. You also need to handle the uncertainty window, which is what TrueTime does with explicit waiting.
How does Google Spanner use TrueTime to get external consistency? Senior

What the interviewer is testing: can you explain "commit wait" in simple words.

TrueTime is an API that does not return one time. It returns an interval, [earliest, latest], that is guaranteed to contain the true current time. Google keeps the interval narrow, typically a few milliseconds, using GPS receivers and atomic clocks in every datacentre. When Spanner commits a transaction, it picks a commit timestamp at the latest end of the interval. Then it waits until that timestamp is definitely in the past on every machine (commit wait) before it makes the commit visible and tells the client. So any transaction that starts after this commit finishes will get a later timestamp. That ordering matches real time, which is strict serializability. The price is the wait, a few milliseconds per commit, and the need for good clock hardware. The idea to remember: instead of pretending clocks are exact, expose the uncertainty and wait it out.

Likely follow-ups
  • Can I do this without atomic clocks? Some systems (YugabyteDB, CockroachDB) use HLC with an assumed maximum skew and restart or wait on reads in the uncertainty window. Cloud time services (such as AWS Time Sync) give tight bounds too.
Why is last-write-wins dangerous? Intermediate

What the interviewer is testing: link between clocks and data loss.

Last-write-wins (LWW) resolves a conflict by keeping the value with the highest timestamp and dropping the others. It is simple and always converges, which is why Cassandra uses it by default. The danger has two parts. First, concurrent updates are silently destroyed: if two users each edit a different field of the same row and the store keeps the whole row of the "latest" one, one edit is lost. Second, "latest" depends on clocks. If node A's clock is 200 ms ahead, its older write can beat a newer write made on node B, so a real later update is thrown away, and nobody gets an error. It is acceptable for data where losing a concurrent update is harmless: sensor readings, cache entries, "last seen" times. It is wrong for counters, balances, carts and collaborative documents. Safer options: write each column separately (as Cassandra does per cell), use a conditional write or version number, or use a CRDT or a merge function.

Likely follow-ups
  • How do you reduce skew risk? Use server-side timestamps from well-synced nodes, monitor NTP offset, or use a logical or hybrid clock.
How do you generate unique, roughly time-ordered IDs across many machines? Intermediate

What the interviewer is testing: practical application of clocks and coordination avoidance.

Options. A database auto-increment is simple and strictly ordered but is a single bottleneck and a single point of failure. UUID v4 is random and needs no coordination, but is not sortable and spreads inserts randomly across a B-tree index, which hurts write performance. UUID v7 (a 2024 standard) puts a millisecond timestamp first, so it is sortable and index-friendly. The Snowflake design (Twitter) builds a 64-bit number: about 41 bits of millisecond timestamp, 10 bits of machine id, and 12 bits of per-millisecond sequence, so one node can make about 4,096 ids per millisecond. IDs are roughly time-ordered, unique without talking to anyone. Pitfalls: machine ids must be unique (assign through a coordinator at start-up), and if the clock goes backwards, the generator must wait or refuse, otherwise it may repeat an id.

Likely follow-ups
  • Are Snowflake ids strictly ordered globally? No. Only roughly, within clock skew.

4. Replication

Why replicate data, and what are the three main replication architectures? Basic

What the interviewer is testing: the vocabulary and the trade-off between them.

We replicate for three reasons: survive failures (a copy remains), serve reads from more machines, and keep data close to users. The hard part is keeping copies in step when data changes. Single-leader (leader-follower): all writes go to one leader, which streams changes to followers. Simple, no write conflicts, and reads can scale. The leader is a bottleneck and needs failover. Multi-leader: several nodes accept writes, usually one per datacentre, and sync with each other. Good for multi-region writes and offline clients, but concurrent writes to the same data conflict and must be resolved. Leaderless: any replica accepts writes; the client or a coordinator writes to several replicas and reads from several, using quorums (Dynamo, Cassandra, Riak). Highly available, tunable, but needs repair mechanisms and conflict rules. Most business systems start with single-leader, which is the least surprising.

Likely follow-ups
  • When would you pick multi-leader? Active writes in several regions, where a cross-ocean write to one leader is too slow, and the data allows conflict resolution.
Synchronous versus asynchronous replication: what are the trade-offs? Basic

What the interviewer is testing: durability versus latency, with concrete failure behaviour.

With synchronous replication the leader waits until the follower has the write before it tells the client "done". If the leader then dies, the follower has the data, so nothing acknowledged is lost. The cost: higher write latency, and if the follower is down or slow, writes stall. With asynchronous replication the leader acknowledges as soon as it has written locally and ships the change in the background. It is fast and keeps working when a follower is down, but if the leader crashes, the latest acknowledged writes may exist nowhere else, and are lost on failover. The common compromise is semi-synchronous: one follower is synchronous and the rest are async. If the sync follower fails, another one is promoted into that role. PostgreSQL offers this via synchronous_standby_names, and MySQL via semi-sync plugin or group replication.

Likely follow-ups
  • If all replicas are synchronous, what happens when one dies? Writes block. So "fully synchronous" is rarely used with many replicas.
What problems does replication lag cause, and how do you handle them? Intermediate

What the interviewer is testing: real-world symptoms and practical mitigations.

Lag is the delay between a write on the leader and its appearance on a follower. It is usually milliseconds but can grow to minutes during a heavy batch write, a long-running query on the follower, or network trouble. Symptoms: a user saves a profile and then sees the old one (read-your-writes break); a report shows a total that does not match another page; a user sees a comment, refreshes, and it is gone (monotonic reads break). Mitigations: read from the leader for recently written data; route a user's reads to the same replica; carry a position token from the write to the read; monitor lag in seconds and take a lagging replica out of rotation; and avoid read-after-write patterns that need fresh data, for example, return the saved object in the write response instead of re-reading it.

Likely follow-ups
  • How do you measure lag? Compare log positions, or write a heartbeat row and read its age on the replica.
What happens during a leader failover, and what can go wrong? Intermediate

What the interviewer is testing: awareness that failover is where data is lost and split brain is born.

Steps: detect that the leader is gone (heartbeat timeout), choose a new leader (usually the most up-to-date follower, by election or by an operator or tool), reconfigure clients and followers to use it, and make sure the old leader steps down if it comes back. What can go wrong: (1) Lost writes. With async replication, the new leader may miss recent writes. When the old leader returns, its extra writes conflict and are usually discarded, which can break outside promises, such as an id handed out to a customer. (2) Split brain: the old leader is not dead but isolated, and two nodes accept writes. (3) Wrong timeout. Too short causes needless failovers during a load spike, which adds more load. Too long extends downtime. (4) Stale clients that keep writing to the old address. The defences: majority-based election, fencing the old leader, replicas that must acknowledge writes before ack, and rehearsal in a game day.

Likely follow-ups
  • How does a client find the new leader? A proxy, DNS with a low TTL, a service registry, or a driver that tracks cluster topology.
What are the problems with multi-leader replication, and how are write conflicts resolved? Senior

What the interviewer is testing: you know why multi-leader is avoided unless needed.

When two leaders accept writes at once to the same record, neither knows about the other's write. Both succeed locally, and the conflict is found later during sync. Strategies: avoid it by routing all writes for one record to one region (home region per user), which is the best option when possible. Last-write-wins by timestamp: simple but loses data. Merge: combine values, for example union of set items, or a CRDT. Keep both versions as siblings and let the application or user choose, like a Git merge. Custom resolution code run on a conflict, such as in CouchDB or a database trigger. Other pain points: conflicts in constraints (two regions each hand out the same unique username, and each looked fine locally), auto-increment keys clashing, and tricky topologies (circular or star) where updates can arrive out of order. Use multi-leader only with a clear conflict plan.

Likely follow-ups
  • A product example? A shared document editor with offline clients, or a calendar syncing across devices.
How does leaderless replication (Dynamo style) work? Explain quorums with N, W and R. Intermediate

What the interviewer is testing: can you do the quorum math and explain what it buys.

There is no leader. A key lives on N replicas, say 3. A client or coordinator sends each write to all N and waits for W acknowledgements. A read asks R replicas and takes the newest version it sees. If W + R > N, the set of nodes that took the write and the set you read from must share at least one node, so the read sees the latest acknowledged write. With N=3, W=2, R=2 you tolerate one node down for both reads and writes. W=1, R=3 makes writes fast and reads strict. W=3, R=1 is the opposite. Choosing W + R less than or equal to N favours speed and availability but allows stale reads. A node that was down misses writes, and is brought up to date by read repair and background anti-entropy, described in the next questions.

Likely follow-ups
  • What if two of three nodes are down? With W=2 or R=2, you cannot reach a quorum, and the operation fails. That is the consistency-for-availability trade.
What are read repair, hinted handoff and anti-entropy? Intermediate

What the interviewer is testing: how leaderless stores heal.

These are three ways a replica that missed a write catches up. Read repair: when a read contacts several replicas and sees that one has an older version, the coordinator writes the newest version back to it. It heals data that is read often, for free. Hinted handoff: if a replica is down during a write, another node stores a "hint" (the write, plus a note of who it was meant for) and delivers it when the replica returns. It makes short outages painless, but hints expire, so long outages need more. Anti-entropy: a background process compares replicas and copies missing data. To compare fast without sending everything, replicas build Merkle trees (hash trees), compare roots, and descend only into the branches whose hashes differ. This heals data nobody reads. In Cassandra you run this by nodetool repair, regularly, within the tombstone grace period (gc_grace_seconds, default 10 days), otherwise deleted data can come back.

Likely follow-ups
  • Why can deleted data reappear? A replica missed the delete, the tombstone was garbage-collected on the others, and then repair copies the old value back as new.
What is a sloppy quorum, and what does it give up? Senior

What the interviewer is testing: deeper Dynamo knowledge and the exact meaning of "available".

In a strict quorum, W and R must come from the N nodes that "own" the key. If some of those are unreachable, the write fails. In a sloppy quorum, the coordinator accepts acknowledgements from any healthy nodes, even ones outside the owning set, and keeps the data there as a hint until the owners return (hinted handoff). The write succeeds more often, which gives high write availability, for example during a partition that isolates the owner nodes. The cost is that the usual overlap guarantee no longer holds: a read of the owner nodes may not include the node holding the latest write until the hint is delivered. So even with W + R greater than N you can read stale data. Dynamo, Riak and (optionally) Cassandra work this way. Choose it when writes must never be refused, such as a shopping cart, and you can merge later.

What is a CRDT? Give examples and say when you would use one. Senior

What the interviewer is testing: can you explain conflict-free merging from first principles.

A CRDT (conflict-free replicated data type) is a data structure designed so that replicas can be updated independently, with no coordination, and always converge when they exchange updates. This works because the merge operation is commutative (order does not matter), associative and idempotent (applying twice is harmless). Examples. A G-counter (grow-only) keeps one count per replica; the value is the sum, and merge takes the maximum per slot. A PN-counter uses two G-counters, one for increments, one for decrements. An OR-Set (observed-remove set) tags each added element with a unique id, so a remove only removes the tags it saw, and a concurrent add wins, which avoids the classic "lost add". Text CRDTs (used by Yjs and Automerge) support collaborative editing. Use them for offline-first apps, collaboration, counters like "likes" across regions, and multi-region caches. Limits: they cannot enforce invariants such as "balance never below zero", they can grow metadata (tombstones), and thinking in them is harder.

Likely follow-ups
  • CRDT versus operational transformation? OT (Google Docs) transforms operations through a central server. CRDTs need no central server.
  • State-based versus op-based? State-based send the full state or deltas and merge. Op-based send operations and need reliable, exactly-once, causal delivery.
How do followers actually receive changes: statement, WAL shipping or logical log? Senior

What the interviewer is testing: a detail that matters in migrations and in change data capture.

Statement-based replication sends the SQL text. It is compact but breaks on non-deterministic functions (NOW(), RAND()), auto-increment ordering and side effects. Physical (WAL) shipping sends the low-level disk-block changes of the write-ahead log. It is exact and fast, but tied to the storage format, so the leader and followers must run the same major version, which complicates rolling upgrades (PostgreSQL streaming replication). Logical (row-based) replication sends row-level change events: "row 7 in table orders changed from X to Y". It is independent of storage format, allows cross-version upgrades and selective replication, and is the basis for change data capture tools like Debezium, which read the PostgreSQL logical decoding stream or the MySQL binlog and publish to Kafka. MySQL defaults to row-based binlog format since version 5.7.7 on.

Likely follow-ups
  • Why do we care about CDC? It is the reliable way to publish database changes to other systems, which is how the outbox pattern is usually implemented.

5. Partitioning and consistent hashing

What is partitioning (sharding), and how is it different from replication? Basic

What the interviewer is testing: the two orthogonal tools, often confused.

Replication keeps copies of the same data on several nodes: it helps availability and read throughput. Partitioning splits the data so that each node holds only a slice: it helps data size and write throughput, because each node handles a share. Real systems do both: split into partitions, and replicate each partition on, say, three nodes. Main ways to split: range partitioning (keys A to F on node 1, and so on), good for range scans but prone to hot spots (all new timestamps go to the last range); and hash partitioning (hash the key to choose a node), which spreads load evenly but destroys key order, so range queries must hit all partitions. The cost of partitioning: queries that span partitions, joins, and transactions across partitions become slow or hard.

Likely follow-ups
  • How does a query find the right partition? The client, a routing tier, or any node that forwards the request, using a partition map from a coordination service or gossip.
Explain consistent hashing and why it beats hash(key) mod N. Intermediate

What the interviewer is testing: one of the most asked distributed systems questions.

With hash(key) mod N, going from 4 to 5 nodes changes the result for most keys, so almost all data must move, and a cache cluster loses nearly everything at once. Consistent hashing places both nodes and keys on a circle (a hash ring, say positions 0 to 2^32). A key belongs to the first node you meet going clockwise from its position. When a node is added, it takes over only the keys between it and its predecessor, about 1/N of the data. When a node dies, only its keys move to its clockwise neighbour. To spread load evenly and avoid a lucky or unlucky position, each physical node is placed at many points, called virtual nodes (for example 100 to 256 each). A bigger machine can take more virtual nodes. Replication on the ring: store the key on the next few distinct nodes clockwise. Used in Dynamo, Cassandra (token ring), memcached client libraries and many load balancers.

Likely follow-ups
  • Alternatives? Rendezvous (highest random weight) hashing, jump consistent hash (no memory, but nodes must be numbered 0..N-1) and fixed hash slots like Redis Cluster's 16,384 slots.
One partition is getting far more traffic than the rest (hot key). What do you do? Intermediate

What the interviewer is testing: diagnosing skew, with several levels of fix.

First confirm: look at per-partition request rate and latency, and find the key. Causes: a celebrity user, a viral item, a monotonically increasing key (timestamp) under range partitioning, or a low-cardinality key such as country. Fixes, from cheap to hard: (1) Cache the hot reads in front, including a short-TTL local cache in the app. (2) Split the key: for hot writes, add a random suffix (post:42#0 to post:42#9), write to a random one and read all ten and combine. This is useful for counters. (3) Replicate hot data more widely and spread reads over the replicas. (4) Change the partition key so the load spreads, for example use (user_id, day) instead of day. (5) Let the store split the hot range automatically, as Bigtable, DynamoDB adaptive capacity and CockroachDB do. The trade-off with key splitting is that reads become more expensive and you need to remember which suffixes are hot.

Likely follow-ups
  • Is hashing the key enough? It spreads many keys but cannot split one hot key. A single key lives on one partition.
How do secondary indexes work in a partitioned database? Senior

What the interviewer is testing: the scatter-gather cost.

The primary key tells you which partition to ask. A query on another field, such as WHERE color = 'red', has no such hint. There are two designs. Local (document-partitioned) index: each partition indexes only its own rows. Writes are cheap (one partition), but a read must ask every partition and merge the results (scatter-gather), so latency is set by the slowest and a lot of load multiplies. Elasticsearch, MongoDB and Cassandra's local indexes work this way. Global (term-partitioned) index: one index covering all data, itself partitioned by the indexed value. A read goes to one index partition, which is fast, but a write to one row may update index entries on other partitions, often asynchronously, so the index may lag. DynamoDB global secondary indexes work like this and are eventually consistent. Rule: if a query pattern matters, model the data so that the query uses the partition key, for example keep a second table keyed by that field.

How do you rebalance data when nodes are added or removed? Intermediate

What the interviewer is testing: operational realism: rebalancing is expensive and can cause an outage.

Good schemes move as little data as possible. Fixed number of partitions: create many more partitions than nodes at the start (for example 1,000 for 10 nodes). When a node joins, it takes a few whole partitions from the others. Only entire partitions move, and the key to partition mapping never changes. Used by Elasticsearch, Riak, Couchbase and Redis Cluster (16,384 slots). Dynamic partitioning: a partition splits when it grows too big and merges when small (HBase, MongoDB, CockroachDB). Token or ring based: new nodes claim ranges (Cassandra, with vnodes). Operational cautions: throttle the data movement so it does not starve live traffic, because rebalancing adds load right when a node is already overloaded or just failed; decide whether to rebalance automatically or manually (automatic plus failure detection can cause a cascade, where a slow node looks dead, its data moves, which slows others); and keep the routing map consistent so clients do not hit the old owner.

How do you handle a transaction or query that spans partitions? Senior

What the interviewer is testing: design judgement: avoid it first, pay for it second.

First, try to avoid it by choosing the partition key so that data that is used together lives together. For a multi-tenant app, partition by tenant, so most transactions stay in one partition. For an e-commerce order, keep the order and its items together. Second, if it is unavoidable, choose a mechanism. For queries: scatter-gather, accept the latency of the slowest partition, set a timeout and allow partial results where it is acceptable. For writes: a distributed transaction with two-phase commit (Spanner, CockroachDB, and Vitess offer it, with a cost in latency and in blocking on failure) or a saga, which is a series of local transactions with compensations. Third, denormalise: store a copy of data where the query needs it, and update it by events. Each option adds failure modes, so a senior answer states the invariant to protect and picks the cheapest mechanism that protects it.

Likely follow-ups
  • What does DynamoDB offer? Transactions across items and tables, up to a limit (100 items), at about twice the cost of normal writes.

6. Consensus, leader election and split brain

What is consensus, and where does it show up in the systems you use? Basic

What the interviewer is testing: do you know why it is needed, not only the algorithm names.

Consensus means getting several nodes to agree on one value, even if some of them crash or messages are delayed. Once agreed, the decision must never change (safety) and the system should eventually decide (liveness). It is the foundation for "everyone agrees who the leader is", "everyone agrees on the order of operations", and "everyone agrees whether this transaction committed". You use it every day without noticing: etcd (under Kubernetes), ZooKeeper (Kafka before KRaft, Hadoop, HBase), Consul, Google Spanner and CockroachDB (Raft or Paxos per data range), and the Kafka controller since KRaft. The rule of thumb: do not write your own. Use a system built on a proven implementation for the small amount of data that needs strong agreement (configuration, locks, leader identity, metadata) and keep bulk data elsewhere.

Likely follow-ups
  • How many failures can it survive? A majority quorum of 2f+1 nodes tolerates f crashes. Five nodes survive two.
Why do consensus systems use odd cluster sizes and a majority? Basic

What the interviewer is testing: the key idea: overlapping majorities.

Any two majorities of the same set must share at least one node. If a value is committed by a majority, then any future majority includes a node that knows about it. So two competing leaders or two different decisions cannot both get a majority at once. That is how a partition is handled safely: only the side with a majority can make progress. Odd sizes are used because an even size adds cost without adding tolerance. With 3 nodes you need 2, and survive 1 failure. With 4 nodes you need 3, so you still survive only 1. With 5 you need 3 and survive 2. So 4 is worse than 3 (more nodes, same tolerance, a bit more latency). Typical production sizes are 3 or 5, rarely 7: each extra node adds replication work for every write. Bigger clusters do not mean faster writes.

Likely follow-ups
  • How do you spread nodes? Across three failure domains (three zones) so a single zone loss leaves a majority. With two zones, losing the bigger zone loses the quorum.
Explain Paxos intuitively. Senior

What the interviewer is testing: whether you can describe it without drowning in details. Nobody expects the proof.

Basic Paxos decides a single value. There are proposers, acceptors and learners. A proposer picks a unique, increasing proposal number n. Phase 1 (prepare): it asks a majority of acceptors, "promise not to accept anything numbered below n, and tell me what you already accepted". Phase 2 (accept): if a majority promised, the proposer asks them to accept a value. The key rule: if any acceptor reported a value it had already accepted, the proposer must propose the value with the highest number it was told about, not its own. This is what keeps a value chosen once from ever changing. When a majority accepts, the value is chosen. Two competing proposers can keep invalidating each other (livelock), which is solved with a stable leader. Multi-Paxos runs many rounds with one stable leader, skipping phase 1 after the first. It is famously hard to implement in full, which is why Raft was created to be understandable.

Likely follow-ups
  • Where is Paxos used? Google Chubby and Spanner, Cassandra lightweight transactions, and many internal systems.
How does Raft work? Explain leader election and log replication. Intermediate

What the interviewer is testing: the most asked consensus question. Be clear on terms, votes and commit.

Raft splits the problem in three: leader election, log replication, safety. Time is divided into terms (numbered epochs). Each node is a follower, candidate or leader. A follower that hears no heartbeat from a leader for a randomised timeout (for example 150 to 300 ms) becomes a candidate, increments the term, votes for itself and asks the others for votes. Each node votes once per term, and only for a candidate whose log is at least as up to date as its own. A candidate with votes from a majority becomes leader and starts sending heartbeats. Random timeouts make split votes rare. Log replication: clients send commands to the leader, which appends them to its log and sends them to followers. When a majority has stored an entry, it is committed; the leader applies it and replies to the client. Followers learn the commit index from later messages and apply it too. If a follower's log differs, the leader overwrites it with its own, starting from the point where they match.

Likely follow-ups
  • Why random election timeouts? So that nodes rarely become candidates at the same moment and split the vote.
  • Can a leader commit an entry from a previous term by counting replicas? Not directly. Raft commits it indirectly by committing a new entry from its own term after it.
In Raft, how is it guaranteed that a committed entry is never lost when the leader changes? Senior

What the interviewer is testing: the safety argument in plain words.

Two rules work together. First, an entry is committed only after a majority of nodes stored it. Second, a node grants its vote only to a candidate whose log is at least as up to date as its own (compared by last term, then by length). To win, a candidate needs a majority of votes. A majority that stored the committed entry and a majority that voted for the new leader must overlap in at least one node. That node has the committed entry and would refuse to vote for any candidate that lacks it. So only a candidate that contains all committed entries can win. This is called leader completeness. Therefore a new leader never has to ask "what was committed?", it already has it, and followers only ever get their logs overwritten for entries that were never committed. A practical consequence: an entry acknowledged by the leader to a client was committed, so it survives; an entry the leader wrote but did not get a majority for may be lost, and the client must retry.

Likely follow-ups
  • What if the client timed out on a write? The write may or may not be committed. The client must retry with an id so the state machine can deduplicate.
How do you serve a linearizable read from a Raft cluster? Is reading from the leader enough? Senior

What the interviewer is testing: a classic subtle trap about stale leaders.

Reading from the leader's memory is not enough, because the node that thinks it is the leader might have been replaced without knowing, for example after a network partition or a long pause. It could return stale data. Options: (1) Log read: put the read through the log like a write. Always correct, slow. (2) ReadIndex: the leader notes the current commit index, confirms it is still leader by getting heartbeat acknowledgements from a majority, waits until it has applied up to that index, then reads. One round of heartbeats, no log write. etcd does this by default for linearizable reads. (3) Leader leases: the leader assumes it is leader until a lease time passes, and reads locally with no round trip. Faster, but it depends on bounded clock drift, so a bad clock can break correctness. (4) Follower reads: serve possibly stale data from followers when the client accepts it (etcd "serializable" reads, ZooKeeper default reads).

How would you implement leader election in an application using ZooKeeper or etcd? Intermediate

What the interviewer is testing: do you use the coordination service rather than invent election.

Let the coordination service decide, since it already has consensus. With ZooKeeper: each candidate creates an ephemeral sequential node under /election. Ephemeral means it vanishes if the client's session dies. The node with the lowest sequence number is the leader. Every other candidate watches only the node just before it (not the leader), so that when the leader dies only one candidate wakes up, which avoids a thundering herd. With etcd: create a key with a lease in a transaction "if key does not exist", and keep the lease alive; whoever creates it is the leader; if the leader stops renewing, the lease expires, the key disappears and others compete. Etcd's concurrency.Election and Kubernetes' lease-based leader election do this. Important: the leader must stop acting as soon as it may have lost the lease, and downstream resources should check a fencing token, because the leader can pause and not know the lease expired.

Likely follow-ups
  • What if the leader's session times out due to a GC pause? Others take over, and the old leader must notice and step down. Fencing protects the shared resource.
What is split brain, and how do you prevent it? Intermediate

What the interviewer is testing: one of the highest-value questions: cause, damage, prevention.

Split brain means two parts of a cluster each believe they are the only legitimate leader and both accept writes. It happens when the network splits, or a node is only slow, and a failure detector wrongly declares the leader dead. The damage is two diverging versions of data, duplicated work, and double-spent resources (two nodes writing to the same disk or sending the same emails). Prevention layers: (1) Majority quorum. Only a side holding a majority may elect a leader or accept writes. With three nodes, at most one side has two. The minority side stops. (2) Fencing. Every leadership change has an increasing number (term or fencing token). The storage or downstream service rejects requests with an older number, so a zombie old leader is harmless. (3) STONITH (shoot the other node in the head): the new leader forcibly powers off or isolates the old one before taking over, used in traditional HA clusters. (4) Avoid two-node clusters without a tie-breaker (a witness or arbiter node). In a two-datacentre setup, put the third voter in a third location.

Likely follow-ups
  • Can a 2-node cluster be safe? Only with a third tie-breaker (witness), or by accepting that it stops whenever the nodes cannot see each other.
What is a fencing token, and why is a lock without one unsafe? Senior

What the interviewer is testing: the classic distributed lock bug (Martin Kleppmann's argument).

A lock service hands out a lock with a lease (an expiry time). Client 1 takes the lock and gets token 33. Then client 1 pauses for 30 seconds (a long GC pause, a stalled VM, a swap storm). The lease expires. Client 2 takes the lock and gets token 34 and writes to the storage. Client 1 wakes up, still believes it has the lock, and writes. Its write overwrites client 2's, or corrupts the data. Client 1 cannot detect the problem by checking the clock, because the pause may happen between the check and the write. The fix: the lock service gives a monotonically increasing number with each grant, the client includes it in every write, and the storage remembers the highest token seen and rejects any lower token. So the stale write with token 33 is refused after 34 has been used. In practice, ZooKeeper's zxid or node version, an etcd revision, or a database sequence can serve as the token. The storage must be able to check it: this is why a lock over a plain, non-cooperating resource is only an efficiency hint, not a safety guarantee.

Likely follow-ups
  • Does Redis Redlock provide fencing tokens? No, it has no monotonic token, which is the main criticism in the Kleppmann and Redis author debate.
When would you use ZooKeeper, etcd or Consul, and what should you not store in them? Intermediate

What the interviewer is testing: do you treat them as small coordination stores, not databases.

All three are strongly consistent, replicated key-value stores built on consensus (ZooKeeper uses its own Zab protocol; etcd and Consul use Raft). Use them for: leader election, distributed locks, service discovery, configuration that changes rarely, and cluster membership. They give you watches (notifications on change), ephemeral keys or leases tied to a client session, and ordered, atomic updates. They are small: etcd recommends a data size of a few gigabytes at most (2 GB default limit, 8 GB suggested maximum) and every write goes through a majority, so throughput is thousands of writes per second, not hundreds of thousands. Do not store large blobs, high-rate counters, application data or session data in them, and do not let thousands of clients poll them. Run 3 or 5 nodes on separate machines with fast disks, because the write-ahead log fsync latency sets your write latency.

Likely follow-ups
  • What happens to Kubernetes if etcd is slow? The control plane (API server) slows or stops. Running pods continue, but nothing new can be scheduled.
What is Byzantine fault tolerance, and do you need it in a typical backend? Senior

What the interviewer is testing: knowing when a heavy tool is not needed.

A Byzantine fault is when a node behaves arbitrarily: sends contradictory messages to different peers, forges data, or acts maliciously. Algorithms such as PBFT tolerate up to f Byzantine nodes out of 3f+1, and need more message rounds than Raft. In a normal backend, all nodes run your own code in your own network under your control, so the assumption is crash faults only, and Raft or Paxos is enough. Real bugs like silent memory corruption or a bad deploy can look Byzantine, and are handled by checksums, validation and canary deploys, not by BFT protocols. BFT matters when the participants do not trust each other: public blockchains, consortium ledgers, and some aircraft and spacecraft control systems. In an interview, say that you assume crash-recovery plus network faults, and you protect against corruption by end-to-end checksums and signed data.

7. Distributed transactions and messaging

How does two-phase commit (2PC) work, and what is its main weakness? Intermediate

What the interviewer is testing: the protocol and the blocking problem.

2PC makes several nodes (participants) either all commit or all abort, under a coordinator. Phase 1, prepare: the coordinator asks every participant "can you commit?". Each one does the work, writes it durably to its log, takes its locks, and replies yes or no. A "yes" is a promise: it can no longer change its mind. Phase 2, commit or abort: if all said yes, the coordinator logs the decision and tells everyone to commit. If any said no, or timed out, it tells everyone to abort. The weakness is blocking. If the coordinator crashes after participants voted yes but before they heard the decision, they are "in doubt": they cannot commit or abort alone, and they keep holding locks until the coordinator recovers. That can freeze parts of the database. Other costs: several network round trips and disk writes per transaction, and low availability, because any participant being down aborts the transaction. This is why 2PC is used inside one database engine or between a few XA resources, and avoided across independent microservices.

Likely follow-ups
  • How do you reduce the blocking? Replicate the coordinator's decision log with consensus (as Spanner does: each participant and the coordinator are Paxos groups).
What is three-phase commit, and why is it rarely used? Senior

What the interviewer is testing: understanding the idea without pretending it is common.

Three-phase commit adds a "pre-commit" step between voting and committing. After everyone votes yes, the coordinator tells all "pre-commit" and waits for acknowledgements, then sends commit. If the coordinator dies, the participants can use timeouts and the pre-commit state to decide on their own: if anyone saw pre-commit, everyone can safely commit, otherwise abort. This removes the blocking, but only under assumptions: the network must not partition and delays must be bounded. In a real network with partitions or long pauses, different nodes can reach different decisions, so it is not safe. It also costs an extra round trip. In practice, systems either use 2PC with a replicated coordinator (a consensus group), or avoid distributed atomic commit by using sagas. So 3PC is mostly textbook knowledge. If asked, say what it tries to fix and why consensus-based commit replaced it.

What is a saga, and how do orchestration and choreography differ? Intermediate

What the interviewer is testing: the main alternative to distributed transactions across services.

A saga is a business transaction made of a sequence of local transactions, each in one service with its own database. If a step fails, the saga runs compensating actions for the steps that already succeeded, to undo their effect in a business sense: refund the payment, release the reserved stock. Example, placing an order: create order (pending), reserve stock, charge card, confirm order. If the charge fails, release stock, cancel order. In choreography, services react to each other's events with no central controller: simple for short flows, but the flow is spread over many services and is hard to see and debug. In orchestration, a central orchestrator (a service or a workflow engine such as Temporal, Camunda or AWS Step Functions) tells each service what to do and tracks state: easier to understand, retry and monitor, but one more component. Key rules: every step and every compensation must be idempotent and retryable, and compensations must not be able to fail permanently.

Likely follow-ups
  • A saga is not isolated. What does that mean? Other requests can see the intermediate state (stock reserved, payment not yet taken). You design for it with "pending" states and semantic locks.
What can you not compensate in a saga, and how do you design around it? Senior

What the interviewer is testing: practical limits. Sagas are not magic undo.

Some actions cannot be reversed: an email already sent, a physical parcel shipped, a payout to an external bank, a third-party API call without a cancel endpoint. For those, order the saga so that the hard-to-undo step comes last, after all steps that can fail, and call it the pivot: steps before it are compensable, steps after it must be retryable until they succeed. Reserve first, charge, then ship. Other tools: use "pending" or "reserved" states instead of final ones (authorise a card, capture it later), so the cancel is cheap (void instead of refund); keep a deadline after which a pending state expires on its own; and make a compensation a new business fact (a credit note) rather than deleting the earlier one, so audit history stays. Also think about a compensation arriving before the original action (out-of-order messages): the service should record "cancelled" and then ignore the late "reserve".

What is the transactional outbox pattern, and what problem does it solve? Intermediate

What the interviewer is testing: the dual-write problem. A very common senior question.

The problem: a service must update its database and publish an event ("OrderPlaced") to a broker. These are two different systems, and you cannot make them atomic. If you save, then publish, a crash in between loses the event. If you publish, then save, a crash gives an event for an order that does not exist. The outbox fixes it by using only one transaction. In the same local database transaction that changes the order, insert a row in an outbox table with the event. Both commit or neither. A separate relay reads unpublished outbox rows and publishes them to the broker, then marks them sent. The relay can be a poller, or change data capture (Debezium reading the database log), which is cleaner and has lower lag. If the relay crashes after publishing but before marking, it will publish again, so delivery is at-least-once, and consumers must be idempotent. The mirror image on the consumer side is an "inbox" table that records processed message ids.

Likely follow-ups
  • Ordering? Publish in outbox id order and use the entity id as the broker partition key to keep per-entity order.
  • Cleanup? Delete or archive sent rows, or the table grows without limit.
What is idempotency, and how do you make a payment API idempotent? Intermediate

What the interviewer is testing: the single most useful tool against duplicates. Expect detailed follow-ups on races.

An operation is idempotent if doing it twice has the same effect as doing it once. Setting status = SHIPPED is idempotent; balance = balance - 10 is not. Networks force retries, so write operations that matter must be made idempotent. The standard design: the client generates a unique idempotency key for each logical operation (a UUID) and sends it in a header. The server, in the same database transaction as the business change, inserts a row with the key under a unique constraint, and stores the response. If the same key arrives again, the server returns the stored response and does nothing else. If the key arrives while the first request is still running, return a "conflict, in progress" answer or wait. Keys are kept for a window (Stripe keeps them for at least 24 hours) and must be tied to the request body, so the same key with a different body is rejected. The unique constraint is what makes it safe under races, not an "if exists" check.

BEGIN;
INSERT INTO idempotency(key, request_hash, status)
  VALUES (:key, :hash, 'IN_PROGRESS');   -- unique violation means: duplicate
INSERT INTO payments(id, amount, ...) VALUES (...);
UPDATE idempotency SET status='DONE', response=:resp WHERE key=:key;
COMMIT;
Likely follow-ups
  • Which HTTP methods are idempotent by definition? GET, PUT, DELETE, HEAD and OPTIONS. POST and PATCH are not.
  • What if the call goes to an external payment provider? Pass your key on to the provider too, so a retry from you is deduplicated there as well.
Common mistakes: checking "does a record exist" and then inserting (a race), or storing the key in a cache with no durable write so a restart forgets it.
Explain at-most-once, at-least-once and exactly-once delivery. Basic

What the interviewer is testing: the basic vocabulary, and the right instinct about which to choose.

At-most-once: send and do not retry. A message is delivered zero or one time. Fast and simple, but messages can be lost. Fine for metrics samples or log lines you can lose. At-least-once: retry until acknowledged. A message is delivered one or more times, so duplicates are possible. This is the default for most brokers (Kafka consumers, SQS standard, RabbitMQ with acks). Exactly-once: each message has its effect exactly one time. Over an unreliable network, true exactly-once delivery is impossible, as in the Two Generals problem. What systems offer is exactly-once processing effect: at-least-once delivery plus deduplication or transactions, so duplicates are harmless. So, the practical recipe is at-least-once delivery combined with idempotent consumers.

Likely follow-ups
  • How does the consumer commit order change things? Commit the offset before processing gives at-most-once. Commit after processing gives at-least-once.
Kafka says it supports "exactly-once". Is that real? What exactly does it cover? Senior

What the interviewer is testing: can you explain the boundary of the guarantee, not just say "it's a myth".

It is real within a boundary. Kafka (since version 0.11) has two features. The idempotent producer gives each producer an id and each message a sequence number, so the broker drops duplicates created by producer retries; the result is exactly one copy per partition. Transactions let a producer write to several partitions and commit consumer offsets atomically, so a "read from topic A, process, write to topic B" pipeline either does everything or nothing; consumers with isolation.level=read_committed do not see aborted data. Kafka Streams turns this on with processing.guarantee=exactly_once_v2 (Kafka 2.5 and later). The boundary: it only covers Kafka to Kafka. If your consumer also sends an email, calls a payment API, or writes to a different database, Kafka cannot undo or deduplicate that. For those side effects you still need idempotent operations, or write the result and the offset in the same database transaction. So "exactly-once" is a property of a closed pipeline, not of the whole world.

Likely follow-ups
  • How do you get effectively-once into a database sink? Store the offset (or message id) in the same transaction as the data, and skip messages you have already seen.
How do you keep message ordering in a distributed system? Intermediate

What the interviewer is testing: knowing that global order is expensive and per-key order is cheap.

Total order across everything needs a single sequencer or consensus, so it limits throughput to one node. Most systems only need order per entity: all events for one order id, in order. Kafka gives order within a partition, so you choose the partition key as the entity id, and all events of that entity land in one partition and are processed one at a time in order. Pitfalls: more partitions than keys does not help; a retry of a failed message can overtake later ones unless you limit in-flight requests (max.in.flight.requests.per.connection up to 5 is safe with the idempotent producer); parallel consumers inside one partition break order; and changing partition count changes the key mapping. SQS FIFO uses a message group id the same way. If a consumer must tolerate out-of-order arrival anyway, put a version or sequence number in each event and ignore events older than what you have applied.

Likely follow-ups
  • How do you handle a poison message that blocks a partition? Retry a few times, then send it to a dead-letter topic and move on, with an alert.
How do you prevent lost updates in read-modify-write sequences across services? Intermediate

What the interviewer is testing: concurrency control without distributed locks.

A lost update happens when two clients read the same value, each computes a new one, and both write: the second write erases the first. Tools, from lightest: (1) Atomic operation in the store: UPDATE stock SET qty = qty - 1 WHERE id = 7 AND qty > 0, or Redis INCR. The read and the write are one step. (2) Optimistic concurrency: keep a version number on the row. Update with WHERE id = 7 AND version = 12. If zero rows change, someone else wrote first: re-read and retry. In REST this maps to ETag and If-Match (returning 412 on mismatch). (3) Compare-and-set in DynamoDB (condition expressions), etcd transactions or Cassandra lightweight transactions. (4) Pessimistic lock (SELECT ... FOR UPDATE) inside one database. (5) Route all updates for one entity to one worker through a partitioned queue. Avoid a distributed lock if a version check works: it is simpler and safer.

Likely follow-ups
  • Does snapshot isolation prevent lost updates? Some databases detect them (PostgreSQL repeatable read aborts), others do not. Check your engine.
Why is "write to the database and then to the cache or search index" a bug waiting to happen, and what do you do instead? Intermediate

What the interviewer is testing: spotting dual writes in everyday designs.

Two writes to two systems are not atomic. If the second fails, the systems disagree, and they stay that way. If two requests race, the writes can land in opposite order in each system, so the database has A last and the index has B last, permanently. Retrying does not fix ordering. Instead, make one system the source of truth and derive the others from its ordered change log. Use change data capture: read the database's transaction log and feed a stream that updates the index, cache or other service. The derived store is then eventually consistent but always converges to the source's order. If you cannot use CDC, use the outbox pattern. Add a periodic reconciliation job that compares the two and repairs drift, because you will have bugs. The principle: one writer path, everything else is a downstream copy.

8. Distributed locks, leases and exactly-once myths

When do you actually need a distributed lock, and what are the options? Intermediate

What the interviewer is testing: judgement: a lock is the last tool, not the first.

A distributed lock is for mutual exclusion across processes: only one worker runs a nightly job, or only one instance updates a resource. First ask whether you can avoid it: use a database unique constraint, an atomic update, a version check, or a partitioned queue where one consumer owns each key. If you do need one, two purposes differ. Efficiency locks avoid doing the same work twice (two nodes building the same report). If the lock fails now and then, you waste some work, and a simple Redis SET key value NX PX 30000 is fine. Correctness locks protect data from corruption. These need a consensus-based service (ZooKeeper, etcd, Consul) and fencing tokens checked by the resource, because a lock alone cannot stop a paused client from writing late. Database advisory locks (PostgreSQL pg_advisory_lock) work well if all contenders use the same database. Always set a lease time so a crashed holder does not block everyone forever.

Likely follow-ups
  • How do you release a Redis lock safely? Only delete it if the value is still yours (compare the unique value in a Lua script), or you may release someone else's lock.
What is Redlock, and why is it controversial? Senior

What the interviewer is testing: awareness of the well-known debate, and a balanced view.

Redlock is an algorithm by the Redis author to get a safer lock from several independent Redis nodes (usually five). The client tries to set the lock on all, measures how long it took, and considers the lock held if it got a majority within a small fraction of the lock's lifetime. Martin Kleppmann criticised it: Redlock's safety depends on timing assumptions (bounded network delay, process pauses and clock drift), which real systems break. Also, it gives no fencing token, so a client that paused beyond its lease can still write after another client took over. The Redis side answered that you can add a unique value and make operations check it, and that clock assumptions are reasonable for many uses. A balanced answer: for efficiency locks, a single Redis or Redlock is acceptable. For correctness, use a consensus system plus fencing tokens, or make the operation idempotent and version-checked so the lock is unnecessary.

What is a lease, and what clock assumptions does it make? Intermediate

What the interviewer is testing: understanding of time-bounded ownership.

A lease is a lock with an expiry. A node is granted ownership of something (leadership, a lock, a cached copy) for a fixed time, for example 10 seconds, and must renew it before it ends. If the node crashes, nobody needs to detect it: the lease just runs out and ownership can move on. This makes the system self-healing, and it avoids deadlock from a dead lock holder. The catch is time. The holder must stop using the resource before the lease ends by its own clock, and the granter must wait until the lease has expired by its own clock before giving it to someone else. If clocks run at different speeds, or the holder is paused, both may think they own it. So leases need: a safety margin (the holder stops a bit early), a bound on clock drift (rate, not absolute time, which is easier to promise), and fencing tokens for protecting shared data. Used in: Kubernetes leader election, Chubby, GFS chunk leases, HDFS, DNS and HTTP cache TTLs.

Likely follow-ups
  • How long should a lease be? Long enough to ride out a normal hiccup and a few missed renewals, short enough that failover is quick. Often 10 to 30 seconds, with renewal at one third of that.
A client calls POST /transfer and gets a timeout. What should it do, and what should the server guarantee? Intermediate

What the interviewer is testing: applying the timeout dilemma to a real API.

The client does not know whether the transfer happened. Blindly retrying could move money twice, and giving up could lose it. The right design: the client generates an idempotency key when the user presses "send", and retries with the same key using exponential backoff with jitter. The server stores the key with the result under a unique constraint, so the second attempt either gets the saved result or is told the first is still in progress. If the client still has no answer after several tries, it should not create a new key; it asks the status endpoint (GET /transfers?key=...) or shows "pending, we will confirm" to the user and reconciles later. The server also should return a stable result for a repeated key, even if the first attempt failed with a business error, so the client sees the same answer. This design turns an unknown outcome into a safe repeat.

You run 5 instances of a service. How do you make sure a scheduled job runs only once per tick? Intermediate

What the interviewer is testing: practical choice among simple mechanisms.

Options, simplest first. (1) Run the scheduler as a single dedicated instance (one replica) or use the platform scheduler (Kubernetes CronJob with concurrencyPolicy: Forbid). Simple, but a single point of failure, so make the job restartable. (2) Elect a leader (lease in etcd or a database row) and only the leader runs jobs. (3) A database lock row: each instance tries UPDATE jobs SET locked_until = now() + 5min WHERE name='report' AND locked_until < now(), and the one that updates one row wins (this is what ShedLock does). (4) Make the job idempotent and partitioned, so two runs do no harm: a unique constraint on (job_name, tick_time). Whatever you pick, assume it can still run twice sometimes (a pause during lock expiry), so make the work itself safe to repeat, and log the run id.

Why is a database unique constraint often a better "distributed lock" than a lock service? Senior

What the interviewer is testing: preference for simple, strongly consistent primitives.

A unique index is checked atomically by the database as part of the same write that changes the data. There is no separate lock to acquire, no lease to expire, no pause problem: the check and the write are one operation inside a single linearizable system. So "only one booking per seat", "only one payment per idempotency key", "only one job per tick" are all done by inserting a row with a unique key and treating a violation as "already done". It survives crashes (the row is durable), needs no cleanup logic beyond optional expiry, and is easy to reason about. The limit is that it works only within one database or one partition. Across several databases you need a coordinator or a saga. And hot unique keys can create contention. But many designs that reach for ZooKeeper locks could be replaced by one constraint, which is simpler and more robust.

9. Failure detection, retries, timeouts and load control

How do heartbeats and timeouts work for failure detection, and how do you choose the timeout? Basic

What the interviewer is testing: the trade-off between speed and false alarms.

Each node sends a small "I am alive" message to a monitor or to its peers at a fixed interval, for example every second. If nothing arrives within a timeout, say 3 to 10 intervals, the node is suspected failed. The timeout is a trade-off. Short: you detect real crashes quickly, but you also declare healthy nodes dead during a GC pause, network blip or busy period, and failover for a healthy node can cause worse trouble, such as a storm of rebalancing. Long: fewer false alarms, but a real failure takes longer to notice, so users see errors for longer. A good timeout is based on measured behaviour: for example, a few times the 99.9th percentile of heartbeat gaps. Also use several signals and require more than one missed heartbeat before acting. Keep health-check threads separate from busy worker threads, otherwise a loaded but alive node may stop responding to checks.

How does the phi accrual failure detector differ from a fixed timeout? Senior

What the interviewer is testing: a clear explanation of an adaptive detector, used in Cassandra and Akka.

A fixed timeout answers "dead or alive" with a yes or no. The phi accrual detector (Hayashibara et al., 2004) instead produces a continuous suspicion level, phi. It keeps a sliding window of recent heartbeat arrival gaps and fits a distribution to them. When a heartbeat is late, it computes how unlikely it is that a healthy node would be this late: phi is about the negative base-10 logarithm of that probability. Phi of 1 means a 10% chance the node is still alive and just late, 2 means 1%, 8 means one in a hundred million. The application picks a threshold (Cassandra's phi_convict_threshold defaults to 8; cloud networks often use 10 to 12). The benefit: it adapts to the actual network. On a jittery network the window shows wide gaps, so a late heartbeat is less surprising, and phi rises slowly. On a steady network, a small delay raises phi quickly. The detector does not need to be redesigned for each environment.

What is a gossip protocol, and what is it used for? Intermediate

What the interviewer is testing: decentralised membership and failure detection.

In gossip, every node periodically (say every second) picks a few random peers and exchanges what it knows: the list of members, their status, and sometimes data versions. Information spreads like a rumour or an epidemic. Each round the number of nodes that know a fact roughly doubles, so it reaches all N nodes in about log N rounds, even with some message loss. There is no central server, so no single point of failure, and the load per node stays constant as the cluster grows. Uses: cluster membership and failure detection (Cassandra, Consul via SWIM, Redis Cluster), spreading configuration and schema versions, and anti-entropy for data repair. SWIM improves the detection: instead of everyone pinging everyone, each node pings one random node; if no answer, it asks a few others to ping it indirectly before suspecting it, which cuts false positives from a single bad link. The cost: information is eventually consistent, so two nodes may briefly disagree about who is alive.

Likely follow-ups
  • Why not have a central health monitor? It is a single point of failure and a scaling limit. Gossip trades that for slightly slower, eventually consistent views.
How do you choose timeouts, and what is a deadline? Intermediate

What the interviewer is testing: practical resilience engineering.

Every network call needs a timeout. Without one, a hung dependency ties up a thread or connection forever, and the stuck calls pile up until the caller falls over too. Choose a timeout from data, not a guess: look at the latency of the dependency, for example p99 or p99.9, and set the timeout a bit above it. Too short causes retries and false failures. Too long delays error handling and wastes resources. Use separate connect timeout (short, like 1 second) and read timeout. For a chain of calls, A calls B calls C, use a deadline: an absolute "must be done by" time passed down with the request (gRPC deadline propagation). Each service subtracts what is already used and stops work when it is out of time. Otherwise A may give up after 2 seconds while B and C still work for 10 seconds on a request nobody waits for. And make the timeouts decrease as you go deeper, so the inner call times out before the outer one.

Likely follow-ups
  • What is a good default for HTTP clients? There is no good default: many libraries have none, or infinite. Always set it explicitly.
How should retries be done? What is exponential backoff with jitter, and what is a retry storm? Intermediate

What the interviewer is testing: safe retry design, the most common source of self-inflicted outages.

Retry only operations that are safe to repeat (idempotent) and only for errors that may be temporary: timeouts, connection resets, 503, 429. Do not retry 400 or 404. Wait between attempts, and increase the wait each time (exponential backoff: 100 ms, 200 ms, 400 ms and so on, capped). Add jitter, a random amount, so that a thousand clients that failed together do not all retry at the same instant. The "full jitter" variant picks a random time between zero and the backoff cap. Limit the number of attempts and the total time. A retry storm happens when a dependency is slow, every caller retries, the load multiplies, and a service that could have recovered is pushed over. It gets worse in layers: if each of three layers retries 3 times, one user request can become 4 × 4 × 4 = 64 calls to the bottom service. Defences: retry in one layer only, use a retry budget (for example allow retries up to 10% of traffic), honour Retry-After, and add circuit breakers.

Likely follow-ups
  • What are hedged requests? Send a second copy of a slow request to another replica after, say, the p95 latency, and take the first reply. It cuts tail latency, at about 5% extra load, and needs idempotent reads.
What is a circuit breaker, and how do you set it up? Intermediate

What the interviewer is testing: the states, and that you can explain the point: fail fast and give the dependency room to recover.

A circuit breaker wraps calls to a dependency and watches the results. Closed: normal, calls go through, failures are counted. When the failure rate in a window passes a threshold (for example 50% of the last 20 calls, or too many slow calls), it trips to open: calls fail immediately, without touching the dependency, for a cool-down period, for example 30 seconds. This saves your threads from waiting and takes load off the sick service. After the cool-down it goes half-open: it allows a few trial calls. If they succeed, it closes; if not, it opens again. Pair it with a fallback: cached data, a default value, a degraded feature, or a clear error. Practical points: one breaker per dependency (not one for everything); count timeouts as failures; do not trip on business errors like 404; alert when a breaker opens. Libraries: Resilience4j in Java (Hystrix is in maintenance and no longer developed), Polly in .NET, or a service mesh like Envoy or Istio outlier detection.

What are bulkheads, and how do they limit the blast radius of one slow dependency? Intermediate

What the interviewer is testing: isolation thinking.

The name comes from the watertight walls in a ship: a hole floods one compartment, not the whole ship. In software you give each dependency or each kind of work its own limited resources, so one slow part cannot use them all. Examples: a separate thread pool or connection pool for each downstream service, so if the recommendation service hangs, the 20 threads for it are stuck, but the payment pool is unaffected. Semaphore limits on concurrent calls. Separate queues for different tenants or priorities, so a noisy tenant does not starve others. Separate deployments for critical and non-critical traffic, and cell-based architecture where each cell serves a subset of customers. The cost is lower resource sharing: reserved capacity may sit idle. The pairing is bulkhead plus timeout plus circuit breaker: the timeout frees the resource, the breaker stops new calls, the bulkhead caps the damage in between.

What is backpressure, and how do you apply it? Intermediate

What the interviewer is testing: flow control: do you let fast producers crush slow consumers?

Backpressure is the consumer telling the producer to slow down, so that the system's load follows its capacity. Without it, an unbounded queue keeps growing, memory fills, latency climbs (everything waits in line) and the process eventually dies. Ways to apply it: bounded queues that block or reject when full; pull-based consumption (the consumer asks for work when ready, as in Kafka polling and reactive streams request(n)); TCP flow control, which does this at the byte level; explicit signals such as HTTP 429 or 503 with Retry-After; and credit-based protocols. When pushing cannot be slowed (user traffic), shed load instead. A key insight from queueing theory (Little's law: items in system = arrival rate × time in system): if arrivals exceed service rate, any queue only delays the pain and adds latency. An unbounded queue hides overload until it is a disaster.

Likely follow-ups
  • What should a bounded queue do when full? Reject fast (with a clear error), drop the oldest or lowest priority item, or block the producer, depending on whether data loss, latency or blocking is least harmful.
What is load shedding, and how do you decide what to drop? Senior

What the interviewer is testing: deliberately failing some requests to keep the rest healthy.

When demand exceeds capacity, a service that tries to serve everything gets slow for everyone, then collapses. Load shedding means rejecting some requests early and cheaply, so the rest are served well. Detect overload by a signal such as concurrent in-flight requests above a limit, queue wait time, or CPU. Then reject with a fast 503 or 429 before doing expensive work. Choose what to drop by value: shed low-priority traffic first (background jobs, prefetching, analytics), keep checkout and login; shed requests whose deadline has already expired, since nobody is waiting; drop new requests rather than old ones in a queue (or the opposite, last-in-first-out, since the newest are the ones whose callers are still waiting). Adaptive concurrency limits (Netflix's concurrency-limits, TCP-like additive increase and multiplicative decrease) find the limit automatically. Clients should treat 503 as a signal to back off, not to retry harder.

Likely follow-ups
  • Load shedding versus rate limiting? Rate limiting enforces a per-client fairness policy at all times. Shedding protects the server when overall demand is too high, regardless of who is asking.
Compare token bucket, leaky bucket, fixed window and sliding window rate limiting. How do you do it across many servers? Intermediate

What the interviewer is testing: algorithms, trade-offs and the distributed part.

Fixed window: count requests per minute; simple and cheap, but a client can send 100 at 11:59:59 and 100 at 12:00:00, which is double the rate in two seconds. Sliding window log: store every timestamp; exact but memory heavy. Sliding window counter: weight the previous and current window counts; a good approximation with small state. Token bucket: a bucket refills at a steady rate (say 10 tokens per second) up to a maximum size (burst, say 50). Each request takes a token; none left means reject. It allows bursts and enforces an average. Most used (API gateways, AWS). Leaky bucket: requests enter a queue that drains at a fixed rate; smooths output, good when the backend needs a steady flow. For many servers: keep the counters in a shared store like Redis with an atomic Lua script or INCR with expiry; or split the limit locally (each of 10 nodes gets one tenth) and sync occasionally, trading accuracy for speed. If the store is down, decide fail-open (allow) or fail-closed (reject), usually fail-open for general APIs and fail-closed for security-sensitive limits.

Likely follow-ups
  • What do you return when limited? HTTP 429 with a Retry-After header, and rate limit headers so clients can adapt.
What is graceful degradation? Give an example design. Intermediate

What the interviewer is testing: designing for partial failure, not only for success.

Graceful degradation means that when a part fails, the product keeps working with fewer features, instead of failing entirely. Design by splitting features into critical and optional. Take a product page. The critical parts are title, price and the buy button. Optional parts are reviews, recommendations, "people also bought", and live stock count. When the recommendation service times out, the page renders without that panel, or shows a cached or generic list. When the price service is down, the page may show the last cached price flagged "may be outdated", but the buy button re-checks the price at checkout. Tools: timeouts and circuit breakers on optional calls, cached fallbacks, default values, feature flags to switch off heavy features during an incident, and static versions of pages. Test it by killing dependencies in staging (chaos testing), because untested fallbacks usually fail on their own.

10. Caching consistency and observability

How do you keep a cache consistent with the database? What races exist in cache-aside? Intermediate

What the interviewer is testing: there is no perfect answer, but you should know the race and the mitigations.

In cache-aside, the app reads the cache, on a miss reads the database and fills the cache. On write, it updates the database and then deletes the cache entry (preferred over updating it, since an update can race and write a stale value). A race still exists. Reader R misses and reads the old value from the database. Writer W updates the database and deletes the cache. Reader R then writes its old value into the cache. Now the cache has stale data until the TTL. It is rare, because it needs a slow read straddling a write, but it happens at scale. Mitigations: a TTL as a safety net (so staleness is bounded); delete again after a short delay ("delayed double delete"); version numbers in cache values so older data cannot overwrite newer; leases or tokens on misses (Facebook's memcache paper); or update the cache from the database's change stream (CDC), so the order matches the database. The honest framing: cache gives bounded staleness, not strong consistency. Decide how stale is acceptable for each data type.

Likely follow-ups
  • Why delete, not update, on write? Two concurrent updates can reach the cache in the opposite order from the database. Delete is idempotent and order-safe, and the next read reloads.
What is a cache stampede (thundering herd), and how do you prevent it? Intermediate

What the interviewer is testing: a classic production failure with several known remedies.

A stampede happens when a popular cache entry expires, or the whole cache restarts empty, and thousands of requests miss at the same time. All of them go to the database to rebuild the same value, and the database, which was sized for cache hits, is overloaded and slow. Slow rebuilds mean even more misses. Prevention: (1) Request coalescing (single flight): only one request per key rebuilds, and others wait for its result; in one process use a per-key lock or a future, across processes use a short lock key in the cache. (2) Jittered TTLs: add a random amount to expiry so that keys loaded together do not expire together. (3) Stale-while-revalidate: serve the slightly expired value and refresh in the background. (4) Early refresh: probabilistically refresh a key before it expires. (5) Warm the cache before sending traffic to a new or restarted cache. (6) A load-limiter in front of the database so that the worst case is slow, not dead.

Likely follow-ups
  • Cache penetration? Requests for keys that do not exist miss every time. Cache the "not found" result with a short TTL, or use a Bloom filter.
How do you debug one request that crosses ten services? What are traces, correlation ids and context propagation? Intermediate

What the interviewer is testing: the basic toolkit of distributed observability.

You need a single id that follows the request everywhere. At the edge, create a trace id (or accept one from the caller) and pass it in a header on every outgoing call and message. The standard is W3C Trace Context: the traceparent header carries the trace id and the parent span id. Each service records a span: the operation name, start time, duration, status and attributes, linked to its parent span. Together the spans form a trace, a tree that shows the path and where time went. Every log line includes the trace id, so searching logs for one id gives the story across services. For this to work: propagate context through thread pools, async callbacks and message headers (the usual place it breaks); use a common library (OpenTelemetry); and sample sensibly. Head sampling decides at the start (cheap, may miss rare errors). Tail sampling decides after the trace completes, keeping all errors and slow traces (needs buffering).

Likely follow-ups
  • Where does context get lost? Async executors, message queues without headers, and calls through third-party code that does not forward headers.
Metrics, logs and traces: what is each good for, and what should you alert on? Basic

What the interviewer is testing: a clear workflow for finding out what is broken.

Metrics are numbers over time, cheap to store and fast to query. They tell you that something is wrong: error rate, latency percentiles, saturation. Traces show where in the chain the time or error is. Logs show why: the details and the error message. Typical flow: an alert on a metric fires, a dashboard narrows the service, a trace finds the slow or failing hop, and logs for that trace id explain it. Use the RED method for services (Rate, Errors, Duration) and USE for resources (Utilisation, Saturation, Errors). Alert on symptoms users feel (error ratio, latency against the SLO), not on every cause such as CPU at 80%. Use percentiles (p50, p95, p99), never averages: an average of 100 ms can hide 1% of requests taking 5 seconds, and in a fan-out of 100 calls, that 1% hits most users.

Likely follow-ups
  • Why do percentiles not add up across services? You cannot average p99 values. Aggregate histograms instead.
Why does tail latency matter more in a distributed system, and what can you do about it? Senior

What the interviewer is testing: understanding fan-out and the "tail at scale" effect.

If a request needs responses from 100 backends in parallel, and each is slow (above its p99) 1% of the time, then the chance that at least one is slow is 1 − 0.99^100, about 63%. So most user requests see the slow case, and your p99 per service becomes the typical latency of the whole. Tail latency comes from garbage collection, noisy neighbours, queueing, background jobs, cache misses and retries. Techniques from Google's "The Tail at Scale": hedged requests (send a duplicate to another replica after waiting for roughly the p95, use the first answer, and cancel the rest); tied requests with cancellation; reduce fan-out; split big work into small pieces; cap queue depth; keep timeouts tight; avoid synchronised background jobs; and measure per-hop percentiles so you know where the tail starts.

What are SLI, SLO and error budget, and how do they change engineering decisions? Intermediate

What the interviewer is testing: a reliability vocabulary that connects to day-to-day work.

An SLI (service level indicator) is a measurement of user experience: for example, "the fraction of requests answered successfully in under 300 ms". An SLO (objective) is the target for it: 99.9% over 30 days. An SLA is a contract with consequences (credits) and is looser than the internal SLO. The error budget is the allowed failure: 99.9% allows 0.1%, which is about 43 minutes of full outage per 30 days. The budget turns reliability into a shared number. While the budget is healthy, the team ships features faster and takes risks. When it is spent, the team slows releases and works on reliability. It also shows that 100% is the wrong target: it is extremely costly, and users cannot tell the difference beyond the reliability of their own network and device. Alert on burn rate: how fast you are spending the budget, so that a fast burn pages someone, and a slow burn creates a ticket.

How would you test a distributed system for failure behaviour before production does it for you? Senior

What the interviewer is testing: maturity: failures are tested, not assumed.

I test at three levels. First, in unit and integration tests, inject faults: use a test double that times out, returns 503, returns duplicates and delivers messages out of order, and assert the code retries safely, stays idempotent and does not corrupt state. Tools like Toxiproxy add latency, drops and resets between real components in a test environment. Second, run game days in staging or a controlled production slice: kill a node, kill the leader, fill a disk, cut a network link between zones, expire a certificate, and watch the dashboards, alerts and failover as the on-call would. Third, chaos engineering in production (Chaos Monkey style) with a clear hypothesis ("if one instance dies, p99 stays under 400 ms"), a limited blast radius and an abort switch. For stateful systems, use model-based or Jepsen-style testing that checks recorded histories against a consistency model, to find lost writes and stale reads. Always write down what was learned and fix the gaps.

11. Real-life scenarios

Every answer follows the same shape: situation, what you check first, steps, and how you prevent it next time. Say this shape aloud in the interview, it shows you work calmly under pressure and think about causes, not only symptoms.

After a network blip, two database nodes both think they are primary and both accepted writes. What do you do? Senior

What the interviewer is testing: split brain handling: stop the damage first, then reconcile, then prevent.

Situation: an automatic failover promoted a replica while the old primary was only isolated, not dead. The blip ended, and now there are two writers with diverging data.

Check first: which node is the legitimate one (the one the failover tool and the majority recognise, with the higher term or timeline)? How long was the overlap, and which clients wrote to which node? Did any client use the old node by a cached address?

Steps:

  1. Stop the bleeding. Fence the loser: shut down or block the old primary (security group, firewall, stop the service), and make traffic go only to the legitimate one.
  2. Measure the divergence. Export writes made on the old primary since the split point (its log or binlog after the last position the new primary has).
  3. Reconcile. For each lost write, decide with the business owners: replay it on the new primary (if it does not conflict), or contact the customer, or drop it. Never just "rejoin" the old node: PostgreSQL needs pg_rewind or a fresh base backup, and a rewind discards the extra writes.
  4. Rebuild the old node as a follower.

Prevent: a majority-based failover (a third witness or consensus-managed tool such as Patroni with etcd), fencing on promotion (STONITH or a token), synchronous replication for data you cannot lose, and short client connection caching so clients re-resolve the primary.

Likely follow-ups
  • Why did automatic failover not prevent this? It decided with a local view only. A decision needs a quorum that can tell the isolated node to step down.
A customer was charged twice for one order. Where do you look, and how do you fix it for good? Senior

What the interviewer is testing: the timeout dilemma applied to money.

Situation: two successful payment captures with the same order id, a few seconds apart.

Check first: the logs and trace for both attempts. Are they the same request retried (client, gateway, or service retry after a timeout), a double click, or a message consumed twice (broker redelivery after a consumer timeout)? Did the payment provider receive one idempotency key or two? Was the first call really a failure, or did it succeed and only the response was lost?

Steps:

  1. Fix the customer now: refund the duplicate and note the cause.
  2. Find the retry layer. Typical: gateway timeout is 5 s, the payment call takes 6 s, so the gateway retried while the first call was still running.
  3. Add an idempotency key per order attempt, generated once (client or order service), stored in the database with a unique constraint, and passed to the provider (Stripe-style Idempotency-Key). A retry then returns the earlier result.
  4. Make the consumer idempotent: unique constraint on (order_id, payment_type).
  5. Align timeouts: the inner call must time out before the outer retry begins, and a retry must reuse the same key.

Prevent: a daily reconciliation job comparing the provider's settlements with the ledger, an alert on two captures for one order, and a test that injects a lost response.

Common mistakes: fixing it by removing retries (then you get lost payments), or a "check if already paid" read before the write, which races.
Stock goes negative: two services each read the quantity, subtract, and write back. How do you fix it? Intermediate

What the interviewer is testing: read-modify-write races across services.

Situation: inventory shows -3 for a popular item during a sale. Both the order service and the cart-reservation service do "read qty, compute, update qty".

Check first: where do the writes come from, and is the read-modify-write in application code? Are there two writers to the same table (a bad boundary: two services own one piece of data)? Is isolation level read committed?

Steps:

  1. Replace the read-modify-write with one atomic statement: UPDATE stock SET qty = qty - :n WHERE sku = :s AND qty >= :n, and check that one row was updated. If zero rows, the item is sold out.
  2. If the service needs to read first, add a version column and update with WHERE version = :v, retrying on failure.
  3. Give the data one owner: only the inventory service writes quantities. Others call it or send commands.
  4. For high contention on one hot SKU, shard the counter or put a queue in front so one worker serialises updates.

Prevent: a database check constraint qty >= 0 as a last guard, a concurrency test with many parallel buyers, and a monitor for negative values.

Users report that their latest profile edit was overwritten by an older one. Logs show correct request order. What happened? Senior

What the interviewer is testing: clock skew with last-write-wins.

Situation: the store resolves conflicts by timestamp, and each application server assigns the timestamp from its own clock. One server is about 400 ms ahead.

Check first: how timestamps are produced (client, app server, database node), and the NTP offset on all of them. Compare the write timestamps in the data with the real request order in the access logs. Look for a single host whose writes always win.

Steps:

  1. Confirm with chronyc tracking or ntpq -p on the suspect host: a big offset, or a stopped sync service.
  2. Fix the clock and restart the sync service. Alert on offset above, say, 50 ms.
  3. Stop depending on app-server time: use a timestamp assigned by the database (or a hybrid logical clock), or use version numbers with compare-and-set so the loser gets an error, not silent loss.
  4. Repair data: the overwritten edits are in request logs or an audit table; replay them if you have them.

Prevent: monitor clock offset as a standard metric; avoid LWW for fields people edit; use conditional writes with ETag or version.

Likely follow-ups
  • Other bugs of the same family? Tokens or certificates that look expired or not yet valid, caches that expire at the wrong time, and scheduled jobs firing twice around a clock step.
A job leader paused for 40 seconds (long GC), a new leader took over, and then the old one resumed and wrote bad data. How do you stop this class of bug? Senior

What the interviewer is testing: the zombie leader problem and fencing.

Situation: the leader held a 15 second lease. It paused 40 seconds, so the lease expired and another instance became leader. The paused one woke up, did not check again, and finished its write.

Check first: GC logs and pause times at that moment; lease and renewal settings; whether the resource accepts a token; what exactly was written, and in which order versus the new leader's writes.

Steps:

  1. Repair the data from the audit trail: identify writes made after the lease expiry by the old holder.
  2. Add fencing: the lock or election service returns a monotonic token (etcd revision, ZooKeeper zxid, a database sequence). Every write carries it, and the store rejects a token lower than the highest it has seen. A conditional write such as UPDATE ... WHERE owner_epoch = :epoch does the same.
  3. Make the leader re-check its leadership before each externally visible action, and step down on any renewal failure.
  4. Reduce the pauses: tune the GC (heap size, collector), find big allocation bursts.

Prevent: treat a lease as a hint for efficiency and fencing as the correctness guard; test by deliberately suspending the leader process (kill -STOP) in staging.

At 9:00 every morning the database spikes to 100% CPU for a few minutes. What is going on? Intermediate

What the interviewer is testing: thundering herd diagnosis from a periodic pattern.

Situation: a time-based pattern points to a synchronised event: many cache entries expiring together (all written with the same TTL at the previous 9:00), or clients that start work at exactly 9:00 (cron jobs, mobile app syncs, scheduled reports).

Check first: cache hit rate graph (does it drop to near zero at 9:00?), top queries during the spike, and the expiry times of hot keys. Check for cron schedules set to 0 9 * * * across many instances.

Steps:

  1. Add random jitter to TTLs (for example 60 minutes plus or minus 10%) so they spread out.
  2. Coalesce rebuilds: one request per key rebuilds while the others wait or get the stale value (stale-while-revalidate).
  3. Spread clients: add random delay to scheduled jobs and client sync timers.
  4. Pre-warm important keys shortly before the peak and protect the database with a concurrency limit so a spike makes requests slower, not the database fall over.

Prevent: a load test that simulates a cold cache; a rule that scheduled work includes jitter.

Checkout calls payment, then inventory, then shipping. Shipping times out after payment succeeded. What is the correct behaviour? Senior

What the interviewer is testing: partial failure in a multi-step business flow, with an unknown outcome.

Situation: the customer has paid, stock may be reserved, and we do not know if shipping created the shipment.

Check first: the state of each step in each service (payment captured, stock reserved, shipment exists?), asking by idempotency key rather than assuming. Is the shipping call safe to repeat?

Steps:

  1. Do not tell the user "failed" and do not refund yet. The right customer state is "order received, being processed".
  2. Retry shipping with the same idempotency key, with backoff. A saga or workflow engine tracks the state, so the retry survives restarts.
  3. If shipping stays down past a deadline, compensate in reverse: release stock, refund or void the payment, notify the customer.
  4. If shipping later reveals it did create the shipment, the reconciliation should find the duplicate and cancel it.

Prevent: model checkout as a saga with explicit states (PENDING, PAID, RESERVED, SHIPPED, CANCELLED), authorise first and capture later (cheap void), keep the hardest-to-undo step last, and have a reconciliation job for stuck orders.

A network partition separates one availability zone. What does each of PostgreSQL, MongoDB, Cassandra and etcd do? Senior

What the interviewer is testing: knowing real products, not only CAP slogans. Say that exact behaviour depends on version and configuration.

Situation: a three-zone cluster, with zone C cut off from A and B. The answers below are typical defaults.

PostgreSQL (primary with streaming replicas): core PostgreSQL has no built-in election. If the primary is in the cut-off zone, it keeps accepting writes (with async replication) while a tool like Patroni may promote a replica in the majority side: that risks split brain unless the old primary is fenced. With synchronous replication, the old primary blocks commits until it hears from the sync standby, so no divergence but no availability on that side. MongoDB (replica set): the side without a majority cannot elect a primary; a primary in the minority steps down and becomes read-only. Writes with w:1 acknowledged only by the old primary may be rolled back later; w:majority writes are safe. Cassandra: every node stays up. Whether an operation succeeds depends on the consistency level: LOCAL_QUORUM in the majority zones works, ALL fails, ONE works even in the cut-off zone but may read stale data. After healing, repair and read repair merge data, with last-write-wins. etcd/ZooKeeper: the minority side cannot serve linearizable reads or writes; the majority carries on. Kubernetes in the minority zone keeps running existing pods but cannot change cluster state.

Check and prevent: know your write concern or consistency level per operation, run an odd number of voters across three zones, test a zone cut in staging, and make clients retry with backoff and tolerate leader changes.

A downstream service had a 30-second slowdown, but the outage lasted 40 minutes, long after it recovered. Why? Senior

What the interviewer is testing: metastable failure: a trigger passes but a feedback loop keeps the system down.

Situation: the slowdown made callers time out and retry. The retries doubled the load, so the service stayed slow even after the original cause vanished. Queues grew full of work that nobody was waiting for any more.

Check first: request rate versus the normal rate (is it far higher than user traffic?), retry counts per layer, queue depth and age of the oldest item, and the share of requests whose deadline had already passed.

Steps (during the incident):

  1. Reduce load fast: turn off retries or lower the limit via config, shed low priority traffic, and reject at the edge with 503.
  2. Drain or purge stale queues, and drop requests that are past their deadline.
  3. Bring capacity back gradually (circuit breakers half-open, ramp-up) instead of all at once.

Prevent: retries at one layer with exponential backoff, jitter and a retry budget; deadline propagation so expired work is dropped; bounded queues; adaptive concurrency limits; circuit breakers; and a load test that includes a slowdown, not only a crash.

Users say "I saved my settings, but the page shows the old values". Only some users, only sometimes. What is it? Intermediate

What the interviewer is testing: recognising read-your-writes violation from replication lag.

Situation: writes go to the primary and reads are load-balanced to replicas. Occasionally the read arrives before the replica applied the change.

Check first: replica lag metrics at the time of reports; whether the app uses a read-write split; whether the save returns a redirect that triggers an immediate read; whether lag spikes during batch jobs.

Steps:

  1. Short term: after a write, read from the primary for that user for a few seconds (a flag in the session), or return the saved object in the write response and render it directly.
  2. Better: pass a replication position token (for example the log sequence number) from the write, and let the read go only to a replica that has caught up to it. Some proxies and drivers support this.
  3. Find the lag cause: large transactions, a long-running query on the replica blocking replay, undersized replica, or a network link.
  4. Alert if lag exceeds a threshold, and take lagging replicas out of rotation.

Prevent: decide per endpoint which reads need freshness; do not send "read what I just wrote" flows to replicas by default.

One Kafka partition has huge lag while the others are idle. How do you investigate and fix it? Intermediate

What the interviewer is testing: hot key or stuck message, and ordering trade-offs.

Situation: consumer lag on partition 7 grows, others are near zero.

Check first: is the consumer of partition 7 alive and moving its offset? If stuck, look for a poison message (failing and retrying forever) or a very slow handler. If moving but not fast enough, check message rate per partition: one key (a large customer, a null key mapped to one partition) may produce most traffic. Check for a rebalance loop (consumer repeatedly kicked out, often because processing exceeds max.poll.interval.ms).

Steps:

  1. Poison message: after limited retries, send it to a dead-letter topic with context, commit the offset, alert, and fix the bug.
  2. Slow handler: reduce work per message, process in batches, or raise max.poll.interval.ms only if processing time is known.
  3. Hot key: if order only matters per sub-entity, use a finer key (customer plus order id). Otherwise split the hot key with a suffix and merge in a later stage.
  4. Adding consumers does not help: one partition is read by at most one consumer in a group.

Prevent: per-partition lag alerts, dead-letter topics, and key design that considers skew.

A consumer processed the same Kafka message three times and sent three emails. How do you fix it properly? Intermediate

What the interviewer is testing: at-least-once delivery and idempotent consumers.

Situation: the consumer handles a message, sends an email, then commits the offset. It crashed (or was rebalanced out) between the email and the commit, so the message was redelivered, twice.

Check first: consumer logs for rebalances and crashes at that time; whether the handler takes longer than the poll interval so the group thinks the consumer is dead; commit mode (auto commit can also duplicate or lose).

Steps:

  1. Accept that duplicates will occur. Make the handler idempotent: record a processed key (message id, or business id such as order_id + email_type) in a table with a unique constraint, inside the same transaction as the other effects, and skip if it already exists.
  2. For the email provider, send a deduplication key if it supports one.
  3. Fix the cause of the redelivery: shorten processing, tune max.poll.records and poll interval, handle shutdown by committing before leaving.
  4. Choose the order consciously: mark first, then send, gives at-most-once for the email (a crash loses it). Send, then mark, gives at-least-once.

Prevent: a test that kills the consumer between processing and commit.

The database says an order exists, but no "OrderPlaced" event was ever published, so downstream never shipped it. Why, and how do you fix it? Senior

What the interviewer is testing: dual-write failure and the outbox as the fix.

Situation: the service commits the order, then publishes to Kafka. A crash, a broker timeout or a deployment restart between the two steps loses the event. It is rare, so it showed up only at scale.

Check first: find all orders without a matching event (reconcile order table against the topic or downstream); check for broker errors and restarts at those times; check that the publish call result was handled, not ignored.

Steps:

  1. Repair now: a script that republishes events for orders with no downstream record, with idempotent consumers so duplicates are harmless.
  2. Fix the design with the transactional outbox: write the event to an outbox table in the same transaction as the order, and let a relay or CDC publish it.
  3. Consumers dedupe by event id.
  4. Add a reconciliation job and an alert on "orders older than 5 minutes without a shipment".

Prevent: a design review rule: no code path writes to two systems in one request without an outbox or a saga.

A non-critical recommendations service got slow, and then the whole website went down. Explain and fix. Senior

What the interviewer is testing: cascading failure through shared resources.

Situation: the product page calls recommendations synchronously. Its calls hang for 30 s (no timeout). Each hung call holds a request thread. The thread pool fills, so even pages that do not need recommendations cannot be served. Health checks fail, the load balancer removes instances, the remaining ones get more traffic and die too.

Check first: thread dump (many threads waiting on one client call), connection pool metrics, timeouts configured on that client, and whether the call is on the critical path.

Steps:

  1. Immediately: disable the feature with a feature flag, or fail it fast at the gateway, and restart saturated instances.
  2. Add a short timeout (for example 200 ms) on the call, and a fallback (empty or cached list) so the page renders without it.
  3. Add a circuit breaker and a bulkhead: a separate small pool for the recommendation calls.
  4. Load-test with a dependency that is slow, not only one that is down.

You added a node to the cluster to relieve load, and the cluster got slower and unstable. Why? Senior

What the interviewer is testing: rebalancing cost and how automatic reactions cascade.

Situation: a new node joined and data streams to it. Streaming reads and writes compete with live traffic on the already stressed nodes. Some nodes got slow, were marked down by the failure detector, which triggered more data movement.

Check first: streaming and compaction activity, disk and network utilisation, failure detector flaps (nodes marked down and up), and pending tasks or repairs.

Steps:

  1. Throttle the streaming rate (for example Cassandra nodetool setstreamthroughput), and pause other maintenance (repairs, compaction bursts) while it runs.
  2. Loosen failure detection temporarily, if the nodes are slow rather than dead, so they are not evicted.
  3. Add capacity before the cluster is stressed, and add nodes one at a time.
  4. Do the join during a low-traffic window.

Prevent: a runbook for scaling that includes throttles; capacity planning with headroom of 30 to 40% so that growth work is not done in an emergency; use fixed-slot designs that move whole partitions.

An "OrderCancelled" event arrives at a service before the "OrderCreated" event for the same order. What happens, and how do you design for it? Senior

What the interviewer is testing: out-of-order delivery across topics or retries.

Situation: the consumer gets cancel first (it came through a faster path, or the create was retried after a failure). It cannot find the order, so it either errors or ignores the cancel. Then the create arrives, and the order is created as active, though it was cancelled.

Check first: are the two events on the same topic and partition key? Different topics, or different keys, give no order guarantee. Do retries or dead-letter replays reorder messages?

Steps:

  1. Put all events for an entity on the same partition by the entity id, with one in-flight request per key, so the order is kept.
  2. Add a version or sequence number to each event. The consumer applies an event only if it is newer than the stored version, and records that a cancel arrived early (a tombstone state), so a late create does not revive the order.
  3. Buffer or park events that depend on missing predecessors, with a timeout and an alert.
  4. Make handlers state-based (set status to a state with a version) instead of relative (toggle).

Prevent: a design rule: every event has an entity id and a version, and every handler tolerates duplicates and reordering.

The nightly invoice job ran twice, and customers received duplicate invoices. How do you prevent a repeat? Intermediate

What the interviewer is testing: scheduling in a multi-instance deployment and idempotent work.

Situation: the app runs three instances, each with an in-process scheduler. Or a lock existed, but it expired while the job was still running, and a second instance began.

Check first: how many instances fired, at what time, with which run id; the scheduler config; lock timeout versus job duration; and whether a restart or deploy happened at the same time.

Steps:

  1. Make the work idempotent: a unique constraint on (customer_id, billing_period) in the invoices table, so a second run inserts nothing. This is the real fix.
  2. Make the trigger single: a Kubernetes CronJob with concurrencyPolicy: Forbid, or a leader lease, or a lock row (with the lock longer than the longest job, plus a heartbeat extension).
  3. Record the job run and its progress so a restarted run can resume instead of repeating.
  4. Void or credit the duplicate invoices already sent, and apologise.

Prevent: an alert on duplicate business keys, and a test that starts two runs at once.

After a price update, some users still see the old price for hours. The cache TTL is 24 hours. What do you do? Intermediate

What the interviewer is testing: invalidation strategy with bounded staleness.

Situation: the price changed in the database, but the cache entry was not invalidated, or the invalidation raced with a reader that put the old value back.

Check first: does the write path invalidate the cache at all? Is the failure in a particular layer (CDN, local in-process cache, Redis)? Is there a stale read race (reader fetched the old row, writer deleted the key, reader then stored the old value)?

Steps:

  1. Immediate: purge the key (or the key prefix) in every layer, including CDN and local caches.
  2. Invalidate by event: on a price change, delete the key (do not update it), triggered from the database change stream so the order is right.
  3. Shorten the TTL for volatile data (for example 1 to 5 minutes) and keep a long one only for static data.
  4. Store a version in the cached value and reject lower versions on write.
  5. For prices, re-validate at checkout against the source of truth, so a stale display never changes what is charged.

Prevent: define a maximum staleness for each type of cached data and test it.

The load balancer keeps marking a healthy but heavily loaded instance as down, which pushes more load to the others and they flap too. How do you stabilise it? Senior

What the interviewer is testing: failure detection feedback loops.

Situation: the health endpoint does real work (database check) and is served by the same busy thread pool. Under load, it times out, the instance leaves the pool, the others get more load, and they time out too.

Check first: what the health check does, its timeout and threshold (one failure removes it?), and whether health requests queue behind user requests.

Steps:

  1. Separate liveness (is the process up, cheap, no dependencies) from readiness (can it take traffic). Do not make liveness depend on a database or other services, or a database blip restarts everything.
  2. Serve health on a separate thread or port, and require several consecutive failures (for example 3 of 5) before removal.
  3. Limit the share of the fleet that can be removed at once (panic threshold: if more than about half are unhealthy, keep sending traffic to all).
  4. Add load shedding so an overloaded instance rejects excess work instead of slowing down for everyone.

Prevent: load-test at 120% of the expected peak and watch the health endpoint behaviour.

You run a 3-node etcd or ZooKeeper cluster, and one node dies. Then a second node is taken down for patching. What happens, and how should it have been done? Intermediate

What the interviewer is testing: majority arithmetic in operations.

Situation: with 3 nodes you need 2 for a quorum. One node already failed. Patching the second leaves only one node, which cannot reach a majority, so the cluster stops accepting writes (and linearizable reads). Everything that depends on it (leader elections, service discovery, Kubernetes control plane changes) stalls.

Check first: how many members are healthy (etcdctl endpoint health, endpoint status), and whether the failed node can be restored quickly.

Steps:

  1. Immediately bring one node back (stop the patch, or restore the failed node). As soon as two are up, the cluster resumes.
  2. If a node is lost for good, replace it by removing the dead member and adding a new one, before touching anything else.
  3. Never do planned maintenance while the cluster is already degraded; patch one node at a time and check health in between.

Prevent: run 5 nodes if you need to survive one failure during maintenance, spread across three zones, alert when the member count drops below the full size, and take regular snapshots.

The gateway timeout is 5 seconds, the service timeout is 10 seconds. Users see errors, but the database shows work completing. What is wrong? Intermediate

What the interviewer is testing: timeout hierarchy and orphaned work.

Situation: the outer call gives up after 5 s and returns an error (and may retry), while the inner call keeps running for up to 10 s and finishes the work. The user thinks it failed, retries, and the work is done twice. Meanwhile, the server wastes capacity on results nobody receives.

Check first: the timeout and retry settings at each hop (client, gateway, service, database driver), and whether cancellation is propagated.

Steps:

  1. Order the timeouts: each inner timeout must be shorter than the outer, with room for retries. For example client 8 s, gateway 6 s, service call 2 s per attempt.
  2. Pass a deadline down the chain and stop work when it passes. Cancel the database query if the caller has gone.
  3. Make the operation idempotent with a key, so a retry after a timeout is safe.
  4. Give the user an honest state: "we are still processing", with a status endpoint, rather than a hard failure.

Prevent: a documented timeout budget per request path, reviewed when anyone changes one value.
