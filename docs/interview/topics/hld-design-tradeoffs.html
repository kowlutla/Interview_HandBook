HLD: Design Trade-offs and Judgment interview questions

The "why did you choose X over Y" questions that probe senior engineers: consistency vs availability per feature, SQL vs NoSQL, push vs pull, sync vs async, monolith vs microservices, build vs buy, single vs multi-region, and more. Each answer gives a decision rule, a concrete example and the cost of the choice. Also covers scaling stages, ambiguity, design reviews, tech debt and behavioural architecture questions.

How to use this page

Trade-off questions have no single right answer. Interviewers grade the reasoning: do you state the options, name the deciding factor, give a concrete example, admit the cost and say what would change your mind? A good template for any answer is: "It depends on X. If X is A, I choose P because ...; the cost is ...; I would revisit if ...". Each answer below follows that spirit: a decision rule, an example, a cost. Never answer with "it depends" alone.

Say: "My default is ___. I switch to ___ when ___. The price I pay is ___, and I would watch ___ to know if I was wrong."

Consistency, availability and data-model trade-offs

How do you decide between consistency and availability for a feature? Senior

Decision rule: ask "what is the worst thing that happens if a user sees stale or conflicting data, versus sees an error?" If a wrong answer causes money loss, legal exposure or physical harm, choose consistency and accept errors during a partition. If a wrong answer is a minor annoyance and an error loses a sale or engagement, choose availability and reconcile later.

CAP says that during a network partition you must pick one of the two. Outside a partition the real everyday trade is latency vs consistency (the PACELC view): waiting for more replicas costs milliseconds. Decide per feature, not per company. One product usually has both kinds.

FeatureChoiceWhy
Bank balance and transferConsistency (CP)Double spend or lost money is unacceptable. Better to show "try again" than a wrong balance.
Shopping cartAvailability (AP)An unavailable cart loses sales. Merging two divergent carts is easy (union of items).
Likes / view counterAvailability, eventualBeing off by a few for seconds hurts nobody.
Inventory at checkoutConsistency at the last stepBrowse with stale counts, but reserve with a strict atomic decrement so you do not oversell.

Cost: CP features show errors or higher latency under failure; AP features need conflict resolution code and users occasionally see odd states.

Why would you make a shopping cart available rather than consistent? Intermediate

Amazon's Dynamo paper (2007) was motivated by exactly this: "add to cart" should always succeed, even during failures. When two replicas diverge, the system keeps both versions and merges them on read (union of items). The known side effect is that a deleted item can occasionally reappear, which is an acceptable annoyance compared with refusing a customer's write.

Rule: choose availability when writes are easy to merge (add-only sets, counters, last-writer-wins on harmless fields) and an error costs revenue. Cost: you must write merge logic and tolerate anomalies like resurrected items. The cart is not the order: the final checkout step is made strongly consistent.

Why must a bank balance be strongly consistent, and what does it cost? Intermediate

Two concurrent withdrawals against a stale read can both succeed and overdraw the account. The transfer must be atomic (debit and credit together) and serialised per account. Typical design: one primary per account shard, synchronous replication to at least one standby, transactions with row locks or optimistic version checks.

Cost: a write waits for a replica acknowledgement (extra milliseconds, more with cross-region), and during a partition the minority side refuses writes. You also limit write throughput per account, which is fine because one account rarely needs thousands of writes per second. For cross-service money movement you add a saga with compensations plus an immutable ledger rather than a distributed lock.

How would you build a likes counter, and which trade-off do you make? Intermediate

Exact real-time counts on a hot post cause a single hot row and heavy contention. Choices:

  • Exact, synchronous: one row update per like. Simple, but a viral post serialises on one row.
  • Sharded counter: spread one logical counter across N rows or keys and sum on read. Higher write throughput, slightly costlier reads.
  • Async aggregation: write likes to a log (the real source of truth, with a unique key per user and post for dedupe), and a consumer updates a cached count every second or so. The displayed number is eventually consistent.

Rule: keep the per-user "did I like this" fact exact (so the heart stays filled), let the aggregate count lag. Cost: the count can be briefly wrong, and the two numbers can disagree until the aggregator catches up.

How do you handle inventory without overselling while keeping browse fast? Senior

Split the path. Browse and search read a cached or replicated count and may be stale (show "only a few left"). Checkout performs an atomic conditional decrement: UPDATE stock SET qty = qty - 1 WHERE sku = ? AND qty > 0, and checks the affected row count. For high-demand drops, use a reservation with a TTL (hold for 10 minutes, release on expiry) so abandoned carts do not lock stock forever.

Cost: strict decrement on a hot SKU is a contention point; mitigate by queueing purchases for that SKU or pre-splitting stock into buckets. Some businesses deliberately oversell a little and apologise (airlines, hotels) because the expected cost of an apology is lower than the cost of unsold seats. That is a legitimate business decision, so ask.

How do you choose strong vs eventual consistency per feature, and how do you explain the middle options? Intermediate

There is a spectrum, not two points. Ask what guarantee the user needs:

  • Read-your-writes: after I post a comment, I see it. Route my reads to the primary (or to replicas that caught up) for a short window after my write.
  • Monotonic reads: I never see time go backwards. Pin a user to one replica.
  • Causal: a reply never appears before the post it answers.
  • Strong (linearizable): everyone sees the latest write. Needed for balances, unique usernames, leader election, locks.

Rule: default to the weakest guarantee users will not notice. Most "eventual" features really only need read-your-writes. Cost: stronger guarantees cost latency and availability; weaker ones cost code for anomalies and more explaining to support teams.

SQL vs NoSQL: how do you choose for a given workload? Basic

Default to a relational database (Postgres, MySQL) unless a specific requirement pushes you away. It gives transactions, joins, constraints and flexible ad hoc queries, and one well-tuned instance handles thousands of writes per second and terabytes with read replicas.

PressureLean towardsExample
Multi-row invariants, reporting, evolving queriesSQLOrders, payments, billing
Massive write rate, known access pattern, simple key lookupsWide-column or key-value (Cassandra, DynamoDB)Event/time-series storage, sessions, messages
Self-contained documents read as a wholeDocument store (MongoDB)Product catalogue, CMS content
Relationship traversalGraphFraud rings, recommendations
Full-text and faceted searchSearch engine (Elasticsearch)Product search, log search

Cost of NoSQL: you design tables around queries up front, give up or hand-build joins and secondary indexes, and often get weaker transactions. Changing the access pattern later is painful. Cost of SQL: scaling writes beyond one primary needs sharding, which you manage yourself.

Trap: "NoSQL is faster" or "SQL does not scale". Both are false as blanket claims. Say what access pattern and scale you have, with numbers.
When is it justified to use more than one database in a system? Intermediate

When two workloads have genuinely incompatible needs, such as transactional orders in Postgres plus search in Elasticsearch plus a Redis cache. Rule: each extra datastore must earn its place with a requirement the others cannot meet. Cost: every store adds operations, backup, security, monitoring, on-call knowledge and a data-sync problem (the search index is a derived copy and can drift). A team of five should rarely run more than two or three technologies. Keep one system of record, and treat others as rebuildable projections.

Normalize vs denormalize: how do you decide? Intermediate

Rule: normalize the system of record, denormalize the read paths that are hot and tolerant of slight staleness.

Normalized: each fact stored once, so updates are simple and consistent, but reads need joins. Denormalized: copy fields (for example the author name stored on each comment, or a precomputed order_total) so a read is one lookup, but an update must touch many places and can leave copies out of sync.

Example: a product page needs product, price, seller and rating. Join four tables on every view at 5,000 requests per second, or keep a denormalized read model refreshed by events. Cost: write amplification, a sync mechanism (triggers, change data capture, events), and a bounded staleness you must accept. In NoSQL you denormalize by default because joins are unavailable.

Schema-on-write vs schema-on-read? Intermediate

Schema-on-write (relational, warehouse tables): validate and structure data when it is stored. Bad data is rejected early, queries are fast and predictable, but changing the shape needs a migration. Schema-on-read (data lake files, JSON documents): store raw data and interpret it when queried. Ingestion is flexible and never rejects, but every reader must handle every historical shape and quality problems surface late.

Rule: use schema-on-write for data that drives business transactions and trusted metrics. Use schema-on-read at the raw landing zone, where sources change often and you want to keep everything, then promote cleaned data into typed tables. Cost of schema-on-read: "the schema lives in the heads of the readers", producing the data swamp. Mitigate with a schema registry and contracts.

Optimistic vs pessimistic concurrency control? Intermediate

Pessimistic: lock the row up front (SELECT ... FOR UPDATE), others wait. Optimistic: read a version number, do the work without a lock, and write only if the version is unchanged (UPDATE ... WHERE id=? AND version=?); on zero rows updated, retry or tell the user.

Rule: low contention and long user think time (editing a profile, a document) means optimistic. High contention on the same rows with a short critical section (ticket seats, account debit) means pessimistic, or an atomic conditional update, because optimistic retries pile up and waste work. Cost: pessimistic risks deadlocks and blocked threads when a holder stalls; optimistic needs retry handling and can starve under heavy conflict. Never hold a database lock while waiting for a human.

Soft delete vs hard delete? Intermediate

Soft delete (a deleted_at flag) allows undo, audit and referential history, but every query must filter it (forgetting once leaks deleted data), unique constraints get awkward, tables grow, and it can conflict with privacy law. Hard delete is simple and genuinely removes data.

Rule: soft delete user-facing content that people expect to restore, and anything with audit needs, then purge for real after a retention period. Hard delete personal data on a deletion request (GDPR "right to erasure") and in high-volume ephemeral tables. A common middle path: move deleted rows to an archive table. Cost: the purge job, the extra filter in every query (use views or ORM filters), and backups that still contain deleted data (which you must explain to compliance).

Rule: a single database and internal IDs means auto-increment. Sharded or multi-writer systems, offline clients, or IDs exposed in URLs mean time-ordered distributed IDs. Avoid random UUIDs as a clustered primary key on a big MySQL table. Cost of distributed IDs: bigger keys and indexes; Snowflake-style needs clock and worker-id management. You can keep an internal integer key and expose a separate public UUID.

Event sourcing vs plain CRUD: when is event sourcing worth it? Senior

CRUD stores current state and overwrites it. Event sourcing stores the immutable sequence of events (Deposited 50, Withdrew 20), and current state is derived by replaying them (with snapshots for speed).

Choose it when the history itself is the product or requirement: ledgers, audit trails, "what did the state look like last Tuesday", undo, or when you need many different read models from the same facts. Otherwise choose CRUD: most apps do not need it.

Cost: a steep learning curve, event schema evolution forever (old events must stay readable), eventually consistent read models, harder ad hoc queries, and awkward GDPR deletion (crypto-shredding is one approach). A cheap alternative for audit needs: CRUD plus an append-only audit table or change data capture.

Say: "I would not start with event sourcing unless the audit history is a core requirement. An audit log table gets most of the value at a fraction of the complexity."
Vertical scaling vs sharding: when do you shard? Senior

Rule: scale up first, add read replicas and caching second, shard last. A single modern machine can have hundreds of GB of RAM and tens of TB of NVMe, and handles a surprising load. Sharding is a one-way door that changes every query, transaction, migration and report.

Shard when you hit a ceiling that scaling up and replicas cannot fix: write throughput beyond one primary, a dataset that cannot fit one node plus backup and restore times, or the cost curve of the biggest machine bends sharply upward. Cost of vertical: a single point of failure, downtime on resize, a hard ceiling. Cost of sharding: cross-shard joins and transactions are lost or hard, a poor shard key creates hot shards, resharding is painful, and operational complexity multiplies. Consider managed distributed SQL (Spanner, CockroachDB, Vitess) before hand-rolled sharding.

Data flow and compute trade-offs

Push vs pull: how do you decide? Intermediate

Push: the producer sends data to consumers when it changes (webhooks, notifications, pushing a feed entry). Pull: consumers ask when they need it (polling, a feed built on read).

Rule: push when updates are rare relative to reads and consumers need them quickly; pull when consumers are many, slow or unpredictable, or when the consumer should control its own pace (back pressure). Kafka consumers pull for exactly this reason: a slow consumer simply falls behind instead of being overwhelmed. Cost of push: the producer must track subscribers, retry failures, and can flood a slow receiver. Cost of pull: latency up to the poll interval and wasted requests when nothing changed.

Fan-out on write vs fan-out on read? Senior

This is a question about where you spend work: at write time (copy the item into every follower's precomputed timeline) or at read time (gather and merge recent items from everyone the user follows).

Fan-out on writeFan-out on read
Read costVery cheap, one listExpensive, N lookups and a merge
Write costOne write per followerOne write
Bad caseA celebrity with millions of followers causes a write stormA user following thousands of accounts has slow loads
StalenessPrecomputed, small delayAlways fresh

Rule: when reads far outnumber writes (typical for feeds), precompute for normal accounts and compute on read for the few huge ones, then merge the two at read time (the hybrid approach publicly described by large social networks). Cost: two code paths, a threshold to tune, and wasted writes for inactive followers (skip users who have not logged in recently).

Synchronous vs asynchronous processing: how do you choose? Basic

Rule: do synchronously only what the caller needs to know the answer to before continuing. Everything else (emails, thumbnails, analytics, search indexing, notifications) goes async via a queue.

Example: placing an order: validate, charge, and reserve stock synchronously (the user needs a definite answer); send the confirmation email, update recommendations and warehouse notification asynchronously. The response time becomes the sum of the critical steps only, and a slow email provider no longer slows checkout.

Cost of async: the user cannot see the final result immediately (you need a status endpoint or a notification), you must handle duplicates (at-least-once delivery means idempotent consumers), ordering, dead letters, and debugging across a queue is harder. Do not make a call async just because it sounds scalable.

Queue vs direct call between services? Intermediate

A direct (RPC or HTTP) call is simple, gives an immediate result and easy tracing, but couples availability and speed: if B is down or slow, A fails or stalls. A queue decouples them in time, absorbs bursts, enables retries and lets many consumers react to one event.

Rule: direct call when you need the answer now (price lookup, auth check). Queue when the work can wait, bursts are expected, or several independent consumers need the event. Cost of the queue: another piece of infrastructure to run, at-least-once duplicates, no immediate result, ordering and poison messages to manage, and backlog monitoring (queue depth and age are the key alerts). Directly calling with timeouts, retries with backoff and a circuit breaker is often enough, and far simpler.

Batch vs stream processing? Intermediate

Batch processes bounded data on a schedule (nightly billing, daily reports). It is simple, cheap, easy to re-run and gives exact results. Stream processes events continuously and gives seconds of latency (fraud detection, live dashboards, alerts), but needs state management, handling of late and out-of-order events, and always-on infrastructure.

Rule: ask "what is the cost of data being an hour old?" If nobody acts within hours, batch. If a decision loses value in seconds (a fraudulent card is used again in 10 seconds), stream. Many teams start with micro-batches every few minutes as a cheap middle ground. Cost of stream: complexity in correctness (exactly-once semantics, watermarks) and operations. The lambda architecture (both paths) doubles the logic; prefer one engine that can do both if possible.

Cache vs recompute: when is a cache worth it? Intermediate

Rule: cache when the computation or fetch is expensive, results are reused many times (high read-to-write ratio, skewed popularity), and some staleness is tolerable. Do not cache when data is unique per request, changes constantly or must be exact.

Example: a product page rendered 10,000 times per minute and changing hourly is a perfect candidate; a per-user bank balance is not. Cost: invalidation (the hard part), stale data, cold start after a restart (a stampede onto the database), memory cost, and one more component that can fail. A cache must never be required for correctness: the system should still work, slower, without it. Measure the hit rate; a 30 percent hit rate rarely justifies the complexity.

Precompute vs compute on demand? Intermediate

Precomputing trades storage and write-time work for fast reads (materialized views, leaderboards, recommendation lists, search indexes). Computing on demand keeps data fresh and saves storage but costs latency on every request.

Rule: precompute when the query is expensive, asked often, and the inputs change less often than the question is asked. Compute on demand when the space of possible questions is huge and most will never be asked (arbitrary filters), or when data must be exact. Cost: a refresh pipeline, staleness, and wasted work on results nobody reads. Hybrid: precompute the common cases and compute the long tail lazily, then cache the result.

REST vs gRPC? Intermediate

REST over JSON: human readable, works with every browser and tool, easy caching with HTTP semantics, ideal for public and partner APIs. gRPC: binary Protocol Buffers over HTTP/2, strongly typed contracts, generated clients, streaming, smaller payloads and lower latency, ideal for internal service-to-service calls with high volume.

Rule: REST at the edge and for external consumers; gRPC (or similar) inside when throughput, latency or streaming matter and you control both ends. Cost of gRPC: not directly callable from browsers without a proxy (gRPC-Web), harder to debug by hand, needs schema and tooling discipline. Cost of REST: larger payloads, weaker contracts unless you add OpenAPI. Many systems use both. Do not switch just for speed if your bottleneck is the database.

GraphQL vs REST? Intermediate

GraphQL lets the client ask for exactly the fields it needs in one request, which solves over-fetching and many round trips for diverse clients (web, mobile, partners). REST gives simple resources, easy HTTP caching, and straightforward rate limiting and monitoring.

Rule: GraphQL when several frontends with differing data needs consume a rich, connected data graph. REST when the API is simple, public or cache-heavy. Cost of GraphQL: the N+1 query problem on the server (solve with batching such as DataLoader), harder HTTP caching (everything is a POST to one URL unless using persisted queries), expensive-query abuse (need depth and cost limits), and authorisation per field. A backend-for-frontend (BFF) layer is another way to solve client-specific shapes.

What design choices make an API idempotent, and why does it matter? Senior

Networks time out, and a client that did not get a response cannot tell whether the server processed the request. So clients retry, and a non-idempotent endpoint then charges twice or creates duplicates. Options:

  • Naturally idempotent verbs: PUT (set to this value), DELETE. Prefer "set balance state" style operations over "increment" where practical.
  • Idempotency key: client sends a unique key (UUID) with the POST; the server stores key plus result for a window (say 24 hours) and returns the stored result on a repeat. Stripe documents this pattern. Save the key and the effect in the same transaction, or a crash between them breaks the guarantee.
  • Natural unique constraint: a unique index on (order_id, payment_attempt) so the database rejects the duplicate.
  • Conditional update: version or ETag checks.

Cost: key storage and expiry, a race between two concurrent requests with the same key (use a lock or unique insert so one waits), and deciding what to do when the same key arrives with a different body (return an error). Consumers of queues need the same treatment because delivery is at-least-once.

JWT vs server-side sessions? Intermediate

Server sessions: an opaque ID in a cookie maps to state in a session store (Redis or database). Revocation is instant (delete the session) and the cookie reveals nothing, but every request needs a store lookup and the store must be highly available. JWT: a signed token carrying claims; any service can verify it locally without a lookup, which suits many microservices and cross-domain APIs. But it cannot be revoked before expiry without a deny-list (which brings back a lookup), and a stolen token works until it expires.

Rule: a single web application with a cookie means server sessions, simplest and safest. Many services or third-party clients means short-lived JWT access tokens (5 to 15 minutes) plus a revocable refresh token kept server-side. Costs: JWT token size on every request, key rotation, and the temptation to put sensitive or changing data into claims. Never store JWTs where scripts can read them if cookies with HttpOnly are possible.

Rate limiting at the gateway vs inside the service? Intermediate

Do both, for different jobs. The gateway enforces coarse, cheap, uniform limits before traffic reaches your servers: per API key, per IP, per tenant plan (for example 1,000 requests per minute). It protects the fleet from floods and abusive clients and keeps the limit logic in one place. The service knows business context the gateway cannot: limit by expensive operation (exports, searches), by resource, by concurrent jobs per tenant, or protect a downstream dependency (bulkheads).

Rule: identity-and-volume limits at the edge, cost-aware and resource-aware limits near the work. Cost: a shared counter store (usually Redis) in the hot path adds latency and a dependency; decide whether to fail open (allow traffic if the limiter is down) or closed (block). Fail open is usual for availability, with local in-memory limits as a backstop. Return HTTP 429 with a Retry-After header.

Architecture, ownership and operations trade-offs

Monolith vs microservices for a small team? Senior

Rule: start with a well-structured (modular) monolith. Split a service out only when a concrete pain justifies it: independent scaling needs, a team that is blocked by shared deploys, different reliability or compliance needs, or a different technology fit.

Microservices solve an organisational scaling problem (many teams deploying independently) more than a technical one. A team of five with four services pays for network failures, distributed tracing, versioned contracts, per-service pipelines, data consistency across services and on-call load, and gets little benefit. Several well-known companies publicly described moving some systems back to a monolith or fewer services after finding the overhead outweighed the gains.

Cost of the monolith: one deployable (a bad change affects everything), long builds as it grows, and boundaries erode unless enforced. Mitigate with modules, enforced package boundaries, separate schemas per module, and a clean internal interface so extraction later is cheap. Cost of microservices: everything above, plus the "distributed monolith" trap, where services must be deployed together.

Say: "I would build a modular monolith and extract the first service when a specific team or scaling need makes the split pay for itself."
What signals tell you it is time to split a service out of a monolith? Senior
  • One component needs very different scaling (the image-processing path needs 20 times the CPU of the rest).
  • Teams block each other: merge conflicts, release trains, "we cannot ship because their change is not ready".
  • A component has a different availability or security requirement (payments isolated for compliance scope).
  • A different runtime fits better (a Python ML service).
  • Build and test times make iteration painful even after normal optimisation.

Counter-signal: "it is cleaner" or "everyone does it" is not a reason. Extract along data ownership boundaries (the service owns its tables) and use the strangler pattern: route one capability at a time to the new service while the monolith keeps working.

Build vs buy: how do you decide? Senior

Rule: build what differentiates your business; buy (or adopt open source) what is commodity. Authentication, payments, email delivery, search, observability and feature flags are usually bought. A recommendation engine at a streaming company is core and worth building.

Count the whole cost: building is not just initial development but maintenance, on-call, security patches and the opportunity cost of the engineers (the largest cost). Buying costs licence fees that scale with usage, integration work, vendor lock-in, limits on customisation, and risk if the vendor changes price or disappears. Method: list requirements, estimate 3-year total cost for both, check exit cost (how hard to leave the vendor), and run a time-boxed proof of concept. Revisit when usage grows, since a bill that was trivial at 1 million events can dominate at 10 billion.

Managed service vs self-hosted? Intermediate

Managed (RDS, managed Kafka, hosted Elasticsearch) gives backups, patching, failover and scaling handled for you. You pay a premium (often noticeably higher than raw compute) and accept limited tuning, version lag and some lock-in. Self-hosting gives control, can be cheaper at large scale, and allows custom configurations, but you own upgrades, failure recovery, capacity planning and the 3 a.m. pager.

Rule: a small team should almost always go managed for stateful systems, since an engineer-week of operations costs more than the price difference. Self-host when scale makes the premium large, when you need unsupported features, or for strict data-sovereignty reasons, and only if you have people with the skills. Cost to remember: managed does not remove responsibility. You still own schema design, capacity limits, restore tests and cost.

Single region vs multi-region? Senior

Multi-region buys two things: survive a whole-region outage and lower latency for distant users (plus data residency laws). It costs a lot: data replication across regions (cross-region latency is typically tens to over a hundred milliseconds), conflict handling if you allow writes in several regions, roughly double infrastructure bills, more complex deployments and a lot more testing.

Levels of ambition, in rising cost: single region with multiple availability zones (the right default, survives a data-centre failure); backups plus a documented, tested restore in another region (cheap disaster recovery with hours of recovery time); warm standby region (minutes); active-passive with replicated data; active-active (hardest).

Rule: derive from the business: what is an hour of downtime worth, what RTO (time to recover) and RPO (data you can lose) are required, and how often do regions actually fail versus how often you cause outages with bad deploys? Most outages are self-inflicted, which multi-region does not fix. Go multi-AZ first; add a region when the cost of regional downtime exceeds the cost of the extra complexity, or regulation requires it. Also ensure the control plane (DNS, auth, config) is not itself a single-region dependency, a lesson from several large cloud outages.

How do you explain RTO and RPO when choosing a disaster-recovery strategy? Intermediate

RPO (recovery point objective) is how much data you can afford to lose, measured in time: RPO of 5 minutes means you may lose the last 5 minutes. RTO (recovery time objective) is how long you can be down. Tight values cost more: RPO near zero needs synchronous replication; RTO of seconds needs a hot standby with automated failover.

StrategyTypical RTOTypical RPOCost
Backup and restoreHoursHours (last backup)Low
Pilot light / warm standbyTens of minutesMinutesMedium
Active-activeNear zeroNear zeroHigh

Figures are indicative. Ask the business to price downtime: an internal reporting tool and a payment gateway deserve different strategies. Untested backups do not count; schedule restore drills.

One API gateway, or a backend-for-frontend per client? Intermediate

A shared gateway handles cross-cutting concerns once: auth, rate limits, routing, TLS. A BFF is a thin backend per client type (web, mobile, partner) that aggregates calls and shapes responses for that client. Rule: start with one gateway; add a BFF when clients genuinely need different payloads and the shared API is getting twisted by client-specific conditions. Cost: each BFF is another service to own, and business logic leaking into it creates duplication. Keep BFFs as thin composition layers.

Should microservices share a database? Intermediate

Default: no. A shared schema couples services invisibly: one team's column change breaks another, and you cannot deploy or scale independently. Each service owns its data and others use its API or consume its events. Cost of separate databases: no cross-service joins or transactions (use sagas, events, and denormalized copies), and reporting needs a separate pipeline. Pragmatic exception: during a migration, or for a read-only reporting replica, a shared database can be a temporary step, with a plan to remove it.

Distributed transaction (two-phase commit) vs saga? Senior

Two-phase commit gives atomicity across resources but blocks if the coordinator fails, holds locks across the network, and is unsupported by many services and queues. A saga splits the business transaction into local transactions, each with a compensating action (refund, release stock) if a later step fails. Rule: within one database use a normal transaction; across services use a saga (choreographed via events for simple flows, orchestrated by a coordinator when the flow has many steps or needs visibility). Cost: you accept temporary inconsistency (an order exists before payment completes), must design compensations that cannot themselves fail permanently, and need idempotent steps.

How do you decide whether to take on or pay down tech debt? Senior

Debt is a loan: it is fine if you know the interest rate and have a plan to repay. Take it deliberately when speed has a deadline value (a launch tied to a contract, validating an idea that may be thrown away), and write it down: what shortcut, why, the trigger to repay, and an owner. Do not take it in areas where interest compounds: security, data model and public API contracts, which are very hard to change later.

Prioritising repayment: rank by interest (how many engineer-hours per month it costs, how often it causes incidents or blocks features) divided by cost to fix. Fix debt in code you are about to change anyway (the "boy scout" approach), keep a standing 10 to 20 percent capacity, and tie big items to business outcomes ("this migration cuts deploy time from 2 hours to 15 minutes and removes 3 incidents a quarter"). Avoid the big-bang cleanup project with no visible value.

How do you decide whether to rewrite a system or refactor it? Senior

Default: do not rewrite. Rewrites take longer than estimated, the old system keeps changing while you build the new one, and the old code embeds years of edge-case knowledge that the new one will rediscover as bugs. Joel Spolsky's famous essay calls full rewrites among the worst strategic mistakes.

Rewrite is justified when: the technology is truly dead or unsupportable (no hires, no security patches), the architecture blocks a business requirement that cannot be reached incrementally, and the system is small enough to replace in a few months.

Preferred path: incremental replacement (strangler fig). Put a routing layer in front, move one capability at a time to the new implementation, run both in parallel (shadow traffic and compare outputs), and switch gradually with a rollback. Deliver value every few weeks and keep the option to stop. Cost: you run two systems for a long time and need the routing seam.

How do you estimate cost versus benefit of a technical option? Intermediate

Put numbers on both sides, roughly:

  • Benefit: revenue protected or gained, incidents avoided (downtime hours times cost per hour), engineer hours saved per month, latency improvement times conversion impact.
  • Cost: build time (engineers times weeks), ongoing run cost (infrastructure plus on-call plus maintenance, usually 20 to 30 percent of build cost per year as a rough rule of thumb), migration risk and opportunity cost.

Example: adding a Redis cache costs maybe two engineer-weeks plus a small monthly bill. If it cuts database load by 70 percent and avoids upgrading a database instance that costs 3,000 per month, it pays back fast. If the database is at 15 percent CPU, it does not. Compare options by payback time and by reversibility: prefer cheap, reversible moves, and demand stronger evidence for irreversible ones. State your assumptions out loud so the interviewer can challenge them.

Simple solution vs scalable solution: how do you avoid both over-engineering and under-engineering? Senior

Rule: design for about 10 times current load, know what you would do for 100 times, and build only the first. Build the simplest thing that meets today's requirements and keep the seams (clean interfaces, stateless services, an abstraction around the datastore) that make the next step cheap.

Premature optimisation (Knuth: "premature optimization is the root of all evil", usually meant about code micro-optimisation) applies to architecture too. A startup with 200 users that builds Kafka, Kubernetes and sharded databases spends its runway on infrastructure. Under-engineering is equally real: no indexes, no backups, a single instance with no failover for a paid product, or hard-coded assumptions (single tenant, single currency) that need a rewrite.

Test: "What specific number or requirement forces this complexity today, and what would it cost to add later?" If it is cheap to add later (a cache, a read replica, a queue), wait. If it is costly later (data model, tenancy model, ID strategy, API contract), decide it now.

Scaling and evolution

How does an architecture evolve as load grows 10x, then 100x? What breaks first at each stage? Senior

A useful narrative, starting from one app server and one database:

StageWhat breaks firstTypical fix
Baseline (hundreds of req/s)Missing indexes, slow queries, N+1 queriesProfile, add indexes, fix queries, connection pooling
10xDatabase CPU and connections; single app server is a single point of failure; slow static assetsLoad balancer plus stateless app servers, read replicas, caching hot reads, CDN, move slow work to a queue
30xReplication lag, cache hit-rate problems, one hot table, deployment frictionPartition large tables, better cache keys, separate read-heavy and write-heavy paths, split off the noisiest component
100xSingle write primary is the ceiling; hot keys; operational complexity; blast radius of one bad deployShard or move write-heavy data to a distributed store, async pipelines, multi-AZ then multi-region, cell-based architecture, rate limiting and load shedding, strong observability

The principle: measure, find the current bottleneck, fix the cheapest thing, repeat. The bottleneck moves. Order of moves: optimise code and queries, cache, replicate reads, scale out stateless tiers, async the slow work, and only then partition data. Also expect people limits: at 100x the organisation has to be split into teams, which is when service boundaries start to pay off.

Your single-database app is slowing down as traffic grows. What do you do, in order? Senior
  1. Measure: slow query log, top queries by total time, connection count, CPU, lock waits, cache hit ratio.
  2. Fix the worst queries: add or correct indexes, remove N+1 patterns, add pagination, select only needed columns.
  3. Add a connection pooler so thousands of app threads do not overwhelm the database.
  4. Cache read-heavy, stable results; send reporting and analytics to a replica.
  5. Move heavy writes that need no immediate result to a queue and batch them.
  6. Archive or partition old data so hot data fits in memory.
  7. Scale up the instance if still needed (cheap, quick).
  8. Only then consider sharding or a different store for the one workload that needs it.

Each step is cheaper and more reversible than the next. Skipping to sharding while an unindexed query causes the load is the classic mistake.

What stops a stateless tier from scaling horizontally, and what do you do about it? Intermediate

Anything that keeps per-user state in server memory: sessions, uploaded files on local disk, in-process caches that must agree, local scheduled jobs that would run twice. Fixes: externalise sessions (Redis or tokens), use object storage for files, use a distributed cache or accept short per-instance TTLs, and elect a single scheduler or use a job queue. Also check shared bottlenecks behind the tier: the database connection limit is hit long before CPU on app servers (100 instances times 20 connections is 2,000 connections).

A trade-off question: how do you handle a hot key or hot partition? Senior

Symptoms: one shard or cache node at 100 percent while others idle (a celebrity account, a flash-sale SKU). Options with their price: split the key (append a random suffix 0..N and aggregate on read, costing read complexity), replicate the hot item to many cache nodes or add a small local in-process cache with a short TTL (costing brief staleness), queue and coalesce writes, or request coalescing on reads so one miss fetches for all waiters. Choose by whether the hot traffic is reads (cache and replicate) or writes (split and aggregate).

When do you choose to degrade or reject traffic rather than scale? Senior

Scaling takes minutes and has limits; overload hits in seconds. A system without load shedding collapses for everyone when it saturates (queues grow, latency exceeds client timeouts, retries multiply load). Rule: decide in advance what to sacrifice: reject low-priority or anonymous traffic first, turn off expensive features (recommendations, exact counts), serve stale cache, or return a cheaper answer. Cost: some users see a reduced experience, but the core function stays alive. Combine with timeouts, bounded queues, retry budgets with jittered backoff, and circuit breakers.

Judgment, ambiguity and design reviews

How do you handle ambiguous or incomplete requirements? Senior
  1. Clarify the goal, not just the feature: who is the user, what outcome do they need, what is the deadline and what does success look like?
  2. Ask the numbers that change the design: users, requests per second, data size and growth, read/write ratio, latency target, availability target, consistency needs, geography, compliance. If nobody knows, estimate with a range and state it.
  3. List assumptions explicitly and get them confirmed ("I assume 1 million daily users, 99.9 percent availability, eventual consistency is fine for the feed").
  4. Separate must-have from nice-to-have and design for the must-haves with extension points for the rest.
  5. Find the irreversible decisions (data model, public API, tenancy) and nail those down; defer reversible ones.
  6. Reduce risk early: a spike or prototype on the scariest unknown, a thin vertical slice shipped behind a flag.

In an interview, spend the first few minutes on this out loud. It signals seniority more than any specific technology.

Trap: jumping straight to boxes and arrows, or asking twenty questions with no proposed defaults. Ask a few, then propose: "Unless you tell me otherwise I will assume X."
How do you explain a technical trade-off to a non-technical stakeholder? Intermediate

Translate into their currency: money, time, risk and customer experience. Avoid jargon; use an analogy; give two or three options with a recommendation.

Example: "We can make checkout always available, but then two people might occasionally buy the last item and we would have to apologise to one. Or we can guarantee no double sales, but during rare outages checkout would show an error for a few minutes. Based on your order volume I expect about 5 apologies a month versus maybe 10 minutes of errors a year. I recommend the strict option because apologies cost more than the short outage."

Structure: the decision needed, options with cost, time and risk of each, your recommendation and why, what you need from them. Quantify where possible, be honest about uncertainty, and say what would change your mind. Do not hide the downside of your preferred option.

You are asked to review someone else's design. What do you ask? Senior

Review the thinking before the boxes. A checklist of questions:

  • Problem and scope: What problem is this solving, for whom? What is explicitly out of scope? What are the numbers (load, data size, growth)?
  • Alternatives: What else did you consider and why was it rejected? A design with no alternatives has not been thought through.
  • Data: Who owns each piece of data? What is the source of truth? Consistency needs? What is the schema migration path?
  • Failure: What happens when each dependency is slow, down or returning garbage? Retries, timeouts, idempotency, partial failure, duplicate messages?
  • Scale: What breaks first at 10 times load? Any single point of failure or hot key?
  • Operations: How do we deploy, roll back, monitor, alert and debug? What is the runbook? What does it cost?
  • Security and privacy: authn and authz, tenant isolation, sensitive data, audit.
  • Evolution: How do we change the API or schema without downtime? What is hard to undo?
  • Simplicity: What could we remove? Is each component justified by a requirement?
  • Rollout: Feature flags, canary, migration plan, success metrics.

Style matters: ask questions rather than dictate, separate blocking concerns from preferences, and praise what is good.

How do you separate one-way-door from two-way-door decisions? Senior

A phrase popularised by Amazon: a two-way door can be reversed cheaply (a cache, a library choice behind an interface, a feature flag); a one-way door cannot (the primary key scheme, public API contracts, the tenancy model, the shard key, choice of cloud-specific data store holding years of data). Rule: move fast and decide with limited information on two-way doors; slow down, prototype, write a design doc and get review for one-way doors. Turn one-way doors into two-way where possible with abstraction layers, dual-writing during migration, versioned APIs and feature flags. Cost: over-abstraction for flexibility you never use is itself a smell.

What makes a good design document, and how do you drive a decision when people disagree? Senior

Good design doc: problem and goals (and non-goals), requirements with numbers, the proposed design, alternatives considered with honest pros and cons, risks, rollout and rollback plan, and open questions. Short enough to be read.

For disagreement: first agree on criteria (latency, cost, delivery date, operability), then score options against them rather than debating preferences. Run a time-boxed prototype or benchmark for factual disputes. If still split, the owner decides (disagree and commit), and the decision and its reasoning is recorded with the conditions under which it should be revisited. Keep it about the design, not the person.

How do you choose between two technologies you could both use (for example Kafka vs RabbitMQ, Postgres vs MongoDB)? Senior

Use criteria, not taste: fit with the access pattern and data model; required guarantees (ordering, durability, transactions); scale now and in 2 years; operational burden and team experience (a technology your team knows beats a theoretically better one it does not); ecosystem and hiring; managed offerings; cost; maturity and exit cost. Run a short proof of concept against your real workload, not a vendor benchmark.

Example: a task queue with per-message acknowledgement, routing and moderate volume suits RabbitMQ or a managed queue; a replayable event log feeding several consumers at high throughput suits Kafka. If the team has used neither and volume is small, a managed queue is the lower-risk pick. State the decision, the main trade-off accepted and the trigger to revisit.

What data do you use to validate or reverse a design decision after launch? Intermediate

Define success metrics before building: latency percentiles (p50, p95, p99, not averages), error rate, saturation (CPU, connections, queue depth and age), cost per request, deploy frequency, incident count, and the product metric the design was meant to move. Compare against the baseline. Set a review date and pre-agreed tripwires ("if p99 exceeds 500 ms or queue age exceeds 5 minutes, we revisit"). Keep a decision log so future engineers know why, not only what.

Quick estimate: how would you justify a design choice with back-of-envelope numbers? Intermediate

Example: 5 million daily active users, each making 20 reads and 2 writes per day. Reads: 100 million per day, about 1,200 per second on average, roughly 3 to 5 times that at peak (so about 5,000 per second). Writes: 10 million per day, about 115 per second average. A single tuned relational primary handles that write rate easily; reads need a cache or a few replicas. Conclusion: no sharding needed, which is the simple solution. If writes were 100 times higher (about 11,000 per second sustained) the conclusion changes. Show the arithmetic, state the peak factor, and let the number choose the architecture.

Behavioural architecture questions

How do you structure a strong answer to any behavioural architecture question? Senior

Use a situation-task-action-result shape, tuned for senior engineers (about 2 to 3 minutes):

  1. Context and stakes (20 seconds): the system, scale in numbers, team size, the business pressure.
  2. The problem and constraints: what was failing or required, deadline, budget, what you could not change.
  3. Options you weighed: two or three, each with the trade-off. This is the part interviewers most want.
  4. Your decision and why, including what you personally did versus what the team did (say "I", not only "we").
  5. Result with numbers: latency from 800 ms to 120 ms, cost down by 40 percent, incidents from 6 a quarter to 1.
  6. Reflection: what you would do differently, what you learned.

Prepare four or five stories in advance (scaled a system, a failure or outage, a decision you regret, a disagreement, a migration or rewrite) and reuse them across questions. Be truthful; interviewers probe with "why not X?" and "what went wrong?" and fabricated detail collapses.

"Tell me about a system you scaled." How do you answer? Senior

Pick a system where you can give before and after numbers and an ordered sequence of bottlenecks. Shape of a strong answer:

Before: one service, one database, 200 requests per second, p99 of 2 seconds at peak. Diagnosis: profiling showed 60 percent of database time in three queries and a thread pool exhausted by a slow downstream call. Steps: added indexes and fixed N+1 queries (p99 to 600 ms), added a cache for the read-heavy catalogue (database load down about 70 percent), moved report generation to a queue, added read replicas, set timeouts and a circuit breaker. Result: sustained 2,000 requests per second with p99 under 300 ms and lower cost per request. Lesson: measure first; the biggest wins were the cheap ones.

(Illustrative numbers, use your own real ones.) Emphasise how you found the bottleneck, why you did things in that order, what you deliberately did not do (we did not shard because the numbers did not need it), and what broke next.

"Tell me about a design decision you regret." How do you answer well? Senior

Choose a real decision with real consequences, not a fake weakness. Show ownership, the reasoning that was sensible at the time, the signals you missed, the impact, how you corrected it, and the process change.

Illustrative example: "We split a five-person team's product into eight microservices early on because it was fashionable. The result was slow feature delivery, constant cross-service debugging and a shared release process. I should have pushed for a modular monolith. When we saw lead time double, I proposed merging four services, did it incrementally over a quarter, and release lead time fell from days to hours. Now I require a written justification and a concrete trigger before any new service is created."

Avoid blaming others, avoid a decision with no consequence, and avoid ending without the lesson.

"How do you decide to rewrite?" or "Tell me about a migration you led." Senior

Show the decision framework from the rewrite question above: quantify the pain, prove that incremental fixes cannot reach the goal, estimate cost honestly with buffer, and describe a staged plan with a rollback. Describe the mechanics: strangler routing, shadow traffic and output comparison, a dual-write phase or change-data-capture, percentage-based cutover, a freeze on new features in the old path, and a clear definition of done (the old system is turned off, which many migrations forget). Mention a risk that materialised and how you handled it. Strong closing: what you measured to confirm success.

"Tell me about a time you disagreed with a technical decision." Intermediate

Show you argue with data and then commit. Example shape: you proposed a queue between two services, a senior colleague preferred direct calls for simplicity. You ran a load test showing direct calls failed when the downstream slowed, shared it, and proposed a smaller step (timeouts and a circuit breaker first, queue if error rates crossed a stated threshold). You got agreement, later metrics supported one side, and you adjusted. Demonstrate respect, evidence, a reversible compromise and the ability to disagree and commit.

"Tell me about an outage you caused or handled, and what changed afterwards." Senior

Structure: impact (users, minutes, money), detection (how and how long), mitigation first (rollback, failover, shed load) before root cause, root cause with the chain of contributing factors rather than one person, and the concrete fixes (alerts, tests, canary deploys, runbooks, removing the single point of failure). Use a blameless tone, and show that the fix addressed the system, not just the instance. If you name a public incident, keep to what is known: for example the 2012 Knight Capital loss was linked to a deployment that left old code active on one server, and the lesson drawn is about deployment consistency and kill switches.

"You have two weeks and a requirement that needs a six-week proper solution. What do you do?" Senior

Do not just say yes or no. Clarify what the two-week outcome must prove (a demo, a pilot with 10 customers, a hard contract date). Propose a thin slice that meets the real need: hard-code or manually operate the parts that will not be exercised yet, use managed services, support one tenant or one region, put it behind a feature flag. Record what was cut as explicit debt with a repayment plan and date, keep the irreversible choices (data model, API) right, and tell stakeholders plainly what the shortcut risks. Then protect time afterwards to harden it. Cost: some rework and a known-fragile area, accepted knowingly.

"How do you raise the quality of design decisions across a team?" Senior

Lightweight design docs for non-trivial changes, with an alternatives section; architecture decision records (short files: context, decision, consequences) kept in the repository; regular design reviews framed as questions; shared checklists for failure modes and operability; post-incident reviews that feed back into the checklists; and pairing juniors with seniors on design. Measure by outcomes: fewer repeated incident types and fewer late surprises in review. Keep the process proportionate: reversible changes need a paragraph, not a document.
