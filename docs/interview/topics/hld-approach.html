HLD: Interview Approach and Estimation interview questions

How a senior engineer runs a system design interview (steps, time split, requirements, trade-offs, pushback), back-of-envelope estimation (QPS, storage, bandwidth, cache sizing, latency numbers), core vocabulary (nines, SLI/SLO/SLA, percentiles, scaling) and how to pick between options and defend the choice.

How to use this page

Each question is a collapsible. Try to answer aloud for 60 seconds before opening it. The first half is about process (how to run the hour), the second half is about numbers and vocabulary (what to calculate and what words to use precisely). No full design problems are here; those live in the Handbook case studies.

The interview approach and time split

What is a system design interview really testing? Basic

It is not testing whether you know the "right" architecture, because there is none. It tests:

  • Structured thinking: can you take a vague prompt and turn it into requirements, numbers and a plan?
  • Breadth: do you know the standard building blocks (load balancer, cache, queue, database types, CDN) and what each costs?
  • Depth: on one or two parts, can you go below the box and explain the mechanism, failure modes and numbers?
  • Trade-off judgement: do you pick and justify, instead of listing technologies?
  • Communication and collaboration: do you listen, take hints, and stay calm under pushback? Interviewers often imagine "would I enjoy designing this with this person?"

For a senior engineer, the bar adds: you drive the conversation, you raise failure modes and operations before being asked, and you connect choices to cost and team size.

Walk me through the steps you follow in a system design interview. Basic
  1. Clarify requirements (functional and non-functional) and agree scope.
  2. State assumptions and estimate scale: users, QPS, storage, bandwidth.
  3. Define the API and core data model in a few lines.
  4. Draw the high-level design: clients, edge, services, data stores, async parts. Walk one read and one write through it.
  5. Deep dive on the 2 or 3 hardest or riskiest components, chosen with the interviewer.
  6. Address bottlenecks and failures: single points of failure, hot keys, retries, overload, multi-region.
  7. Wrap up: summarise decisions, name what you would monitor, and list what you would do next with more time.
Say: "I will start with requirements and rough numbers, sketch the end-to-end design, then go deep where you want. Tell me if you would rather steer elsewhere."
How would you split 45 minutes? Basic

These are guides, not rules. If the interviewer clearly wants to spend 25 minutes on one deep dive, go with it. The usual failure is spending 20 minutes on requirements and estimation and never reaching depth, or jumping into detail with no big picture.

You are 30 minutes in and have only drawn boxes. What do you do? Intermediate

Say it out loud and re-plan: "I have the overall shape; given the time left, which part would you like me to go deep on?" Then pick the single riskiest component and cover mechanism, trade-off and failure. Depth on one part beats shallow coverage of five. If you realise you skipped numbers, do a 60-second estimate to justify the one decision that depends on them (for example whether one database node is enough).

Why not start drawing right away? What goes wrong if you skip requirements? Basic

The prompt is deliberately vague ("design a photo sharing app"). Different answers to "who uploads, how big, how fresh must feeds be, must it work across regions" lead to different architectures. Without them you design for an imagined problem and cannot defend choices later, because you have no stated requirement to point to. Five minutes of questions also shows the interviewer you work like a senior engineer: you do not build before you understand.

Who should drive the interview, you or the interviewer? Intermediate

You drive; the interviewer steers. Propose the plan, narrate transitions ("now I will cover the write path"), and ask at decision points ("two options here, X and Y; I lean X because...; happy to go deeper on either"). Stay responsive: a hint or a question means the interviewer is steering, so follow it. Silence while thinking is fine if you say "give me a moment to think about the failure case", but long unexplained silence leaves the interviewer nothing to score.

Requirements, scope and assumptions

What clarifying questions should you ask, and in what order? Basic
  1. Users and use cases: who uses it, what are the top 3 to 5 actions?
  2. Scale: how many users (and daily active), requests per user per day, data size per item?
  3. Read or write heavy, and are there bursts (sales, live events)?
  4. Latency and freshness: how fast must a response be; can data be a few seconds stale?
  5. Availability and consistency: what happens if it is down for a minute; can two users briefly see different data?
  6. Durability: can we ever lose data? (Payments yes never; view counts maybe.)
  7. Geography: one region or global; any data residency rules?
  8. Constraints: existing systems, team size, budget, mobile versus web.

Ask questions that change the design. "What colour is the button?" does not; "is it OK to lose a like?" does.

What is the difference between functional and non-functional requirements? Give examples. Basic

Functional: what the system does, usually as verbs. "A user can upload a photo", "follow another user", "see a feed".

Non-functional (quality attributes): how well it does it. Scale (100M daily users), latency (feed loads in under 300 ms at p99), availability (99.99%), consistency (read your own writes), durability (no lost uploads), security, cost, operability.

Functional requirements tell you which components exist. Non-functional requirements decide the architecture: the same "store and fetch a photo" feature is a single database at 1,000 users and a sharded, CDN-fronted object store at 100 million.

How do you scope the problem, and why state assumptions out loud? Basic

Write three short lists: in scope (core, designed in detail), nice to have (mentioned, maybe one sentence), out of scope (explicitly dropped, for example "billing and admin tools"). Ask the interviewer to confirm.

Assumptions ("100M DAU, each uploads 0.1 photos a day, average photo 2 MB, 5 year retention") turn guesses into shared facts. Later when you say "this needs sharding" you can point to the number. If the interviewer disagrees with an assumption, that is good: it is cheap to fix now, expensive at minute 40.

The interviewer says "make it highly available and strongly consistent and cheap". How do you respond? Intermediate

These conflict, so push for priorities instead of agreeing to all. Under a network partition you must choose between consistency and availability (the CAP theorem); strong consistency across regions adds round-trip latency; high availability needs redundancy, which costs money.

Say: "Which data must be strongly consistent? Probably account balance and inventory. Which can be eventually consistent? Feeds, counters, search. I will use strong consistency on the small critical part and cheaper eventual consistency for the bulk."

This splits the system by data type, which is the real senior answer.

Why define the API and data model before the architecture? Intermediate

The API fixes what crosses the boundary, and the data model fixes what must be stored and how it is queried. Access patterns ("fetch last 20 posts of a user", "look up by id") decide the database type and keys, which in turn decide sharding. Three to five endpoints and the main entities with their key fields are enough, for example POST /photos, GET /feed?cursor=, and a Photo(id, owner_id, created_at, blob_url) record. Mention pagination, idempotency for writes, and auth in one line each.

The prompt is a single word ("design Instagram"). How do you avoid designing everything? Basic

Say that the product is huge and you will narrow it. Propose a core slice ("upload, follow, view feed; skip stories, ads, DMs"), state it, and ask for confirmation. Choosing a sensible slice is itself a skill the interviewer is observing. Keep a short list of what you left out so you can mention it in wrap-up.

Drawing the high-level design and going deep

How do you draw a good high-level design? Basic
  • Start from the client and move inward: client, DNS/CDN, load balancer or API gateway, stateless services, caches, databases, queues and workers.
  • Keep it to roughly 6 to 10 boxes at first. Label each arrow with what flows on it (request, event, file).
  • Separate the read path and the write path, and trace one request of each end to end, out loud.
  • Split synchronous (user waits) from asynchronous (queue, background) work.
  • Name the data store for each kind of data and why (relational for orders, object storage for files, cache for hot reads).
  • Leave detail for the deep dive; do not name every technology at once.
How do you choose what to deep dive into? Intermediate

Pick parts that are (1) the hardest or most unusual for this problem, (2) where your estimates showed a bottleneck, or (3) where the interviewer showed interest. Ask: "I see two risky parts: X and Y. Which would you like me to go into?" For each deep dive follow the same pattern: what problem, options, choice and why, how it fails, what we monitor. Typical deep-dive areas are the data model and sharding key, caching and invalidation, fan-out strategy, consistency of a critical write, and overload handling.

How do you find bottlenecks in your own design? Intermediate

Walk each component with three questions: What happens at 10x load? What happens when it dies? What happens when it is slow? Look for single points of failure (one database, one queue broker), hot spots (a celebrity user, one popular key), unbounded growth (queue depth, table size), chatty calls (a request that fans out to 30 services), and anything holding a lock across a network call. Then pair each finding with a mitigation: replicate, shard, cache, add a queue, rate limit, degrade gracefully.

How should you wrap up a design? Basic
  1. Summarise the design in 30 seconds against the original requirements ("reads at 50k QPS are served by cache; writes are durable through replicated storage").
  2. Name the key trade-offs you accepted ("eventual consistency for feeds in exchange for low latency").
  3. Say what you would monitor (latency percentiles, error rate, queue lag, cache hit ratio) and what alert would page you.
  4. List next steps or open risks: multi-region, abuse protection, cost, migration plan.

Ending with a crisp summary leaves the interviewer a clear last impression and shows you can close a design, not only start one.

Should you begin with a simple design and evolve it, or present the final scaled design? Intermediate

Begin simple (one app server, one database), show that it meets the stated load or where it breaks, then evolve: add a load balancer and stateless replicas, a cache, read replicas, sharding, a queue, a CDN, multi-region. This ties every addition to a concrete pain, so you avoid over-engineering, and it mirrors how real systems grow. Do not stay simple forever: if your estimate says 200k QPS, say plainly that the single database is dead on arrival.

Trade-offs, hints, pushback and common mistakes

How do you communicate a trade-off well? Intermediate

Use a four-part sentence: option, gain, cost, deciding requirement.

Say: "I will use asynchronous replication to the second region. We gain low write latency; we risk losing the last second or so of writes in a regional failure. Since the requirement says a few seconds of data loss is acceptable for comments, I accept that. For payments I would not."

Always name the alternative you rejected. Say "it depends" only if followed by "on X, and given X here, I choose Y".

The interviewer gives a hint. How should you react? Basic

Treat it as a free gift, not a sign of failure. Interviewers hint to unblock you or to move you toward the part they want to evaluate. Acknowledge it ("good point, that is the hot key problem"), restate it to confirm understanding, and incorporate it. Do not defend the old approach out of ego, and do not ignore the hint and carry on. Candidates who use hints well often score higher than ones who happened not to need them.

The interviewer challenges your choice ("why not just use X?"). What do you do? Intermediate
  1. Do not get defensive; assume it is a genuine question, or a probe of how well you reason.
  2. Restate the alternative fairly and give its real benefits.
  3. Compare against the requirements and numbers you agreed. Often your choice still wins, and you can say why.
  4. If their point is right, change the design openly: "That is a better fit because of Z; I would switch and here is the impact."
  5. If you do not know, say so, and reason from first principles.

Changing your mind for a good reason signals seniority; changing it just because they frowned, without a reason, does not.

What do you do when you do not know a technology the interviewer mentions? Basic

Be honest and bridge to what you do know: "I have not used that one; from the name I assume it is a distributed log. I have used Kafka, where we would do X. Is it similar?" Reason from properties (ordering, durability, throughput, consistency) rather than product names. Bluffing is the worst option because a single follow-up question exposes it and damages trust in everything else you said.

What are the most common mistakes in system design interviews? Basic
  • Designing before clarifying; assuming rather than asking.
  • Name-dropping technologies without reasons ("use Kafka and Redis and Cassandra").
  • Skipping numbers, or spending 15 minutes on exact arithmetic.
  • Over-engineering: microservices, multi-region and sharding for 1,000 users.
  • Single points of failure left in the diagram, and ignoring failure entirely.
  • Never going deep; everything stays at box level.
  • Silence, or monologue without checking in with the interviewer.
  • Treating trade-offs as one-sided ("Cassandra is just better").
  • Ignoring hints or arguing with them.
  • No wrap-up, no monitoring, no mention of operations.
How do you avoid over-engineering in an interview and in real life? Intermediate

Let numbers justify every component. If peak is 200 QPS, one well-indexed database and two stateless servers behind a load balancer is a correct answer; say so, then add "if it grows 100x, here is the next step". Each added piece (cache, queue, shard, extra region) brings operational cost, new failure modes and consistency questions, so it must solve a stated problem. A good test: "what requirement would break if I removed this box?" If none, remove it.

Any practical tips for the whiteboard or shared doc? Basic
  • Write the requirements and key numbers in a corner and refer back to them.
  • Use consistent shapes: boxes for services, cylinders for stores, arrows with labels.
  • Number the steps of a flow (1, 2, 3) and narrate while drawing.
  • Leave space; you will insert a cache or queue later.
  • Check in every few minutes: "does this make sense so far?"
Scenario: the interviewer says "skip the estimates, just design it". Do you? Intermediate

Respect the request, but keep a minimal version in your head and state it as an assumption: "I will assume around 10k reads per second and 1k writes per second so my choices are consistent." You still need some scale to decide whether to shard or cache. Do not argue; just quietly keep a rough order of magnitude and say it aloud in one sentence.

Scenario: at minute 35 the interviewer adds a new requirement ("now it must work in 3 regions"). How do you handle it? Senior

This tests adaptability, not whether your first design was perfect. Do not redraw everything. Ask what matters (latency for local users, disaster recovery, data residency), then identify which parts change: user routing (geo-DNS or anycast), data placement (partition by user home region versus fully replicated), write conflict handling (single writer per key versus last-write-wins versus CRDTs), and cross-region replication lag. State which parts stay the same (stateless services, caches per region). Call out the new trade-off: lower local latency, higher complexity and some staleness or conflict handling.

Back-of-envelope estimation

Why do back-of-envelope estimates matter, and how precise should they be? Basic

They tell you the order of magnitude of load and data, which decides the architecture: 100 QPS fits one database, 100,000 QPS needs caching, replicas and probably sharding; 1 TB fits one disk, 1 PB does not. They also reveal which resource is the bottleneck (CPU, memory, disk, network).

Precision should be about a factor of two. Round aggressively: use 100,000 seconds per day instead of 86,400, and 1 million instead of 1,048,576. Being off by 20 percent never changes the design; being off by 100x does.

How do you go from users to QPS? Work an example. Basic
  1. Start from DAU (daily active users), not registered users.
  2. Multiply by actions per user per day to get requests per day.
  3. Divide by about 100,000 seconds per day (really 86,400) for average QPS.
  4. Multiply by a peak factor (2 to 3 for consumer apps) for peak QPS.
DAU                = 50 million
Reads per user/day = 20       -> 1,000 million reads/day
Average read QPS   = 1e9 / 1e5 = 10,000
Peak read QPS      = 10,000 x 3 = 30,000
Writes per user/day= 1        -> 50M/day -> 500 avg -> 1,500 peak

A handy anchor: 1 million requests per day is about 12 per second; 1 billion per day is about 12,000 per second.

Why size for peak and not average? How big is the peak factor? Basic

Users are not spread evenly: most traffic arrives in a few busy hours (evening, lunch, a live event). A system sized for the average falls over when the peak arrives, and the peak is when users care most. Typical factors are 2 to 3 times average for steady consumer products, and 10 to 100 times for bursty events (flash sales, ticket releases, a goal in a final). Capacity must cover peak plus headroom (see below); cost control comes from autoscaling down off peak, not from under-provisioning the peak.

How do you estimate storage? Basic
Photos/day        = 50M DAU x 0.1 uploads    = 5 million
Size per photo    = 2 MB (after compression)
Per day           = 5M x 2 MB                = 10 TB
Per year          = 10 TB x 365              = 3.65 PB
Replication x3    = about 11 PB/year   (or about 5 PB with erasure coding at ~1.5x)

Remember to separate blobs (large files, dominate bytes, go to object storage) from metadata (small rows, dominate query traffic, go to a database). 5M rows per day at 500 bytes is only 2.5 GB per day, trivial next to the blobs. Include retention period, replication factor and indexes (often 20 to 100 percent overhead).

How do you estimate bandwidth? Basic

Bandwidth = QPS x bytes per request. Do ingress (uploads) and egress (downloads) separately, because reads are usually far larger.

Uploads: 5M photos/day x 2 MB = 10 TB/day -> 10e12 / 1e5 = 100 MB/s average (about 0.8 Gbps)
Views:   1 billion/day x 200 KB thumbnail = 200 TB/day -> 2 GB/s average (about 16 Gbps), peak x3 = about 48 Gbps

Watch units: bytes versus bits (a factor of 8), and MB/s versus Gbps. 48 Gbps egress is a strong signal to use a CDN, since serving it from your own origin would be costly and slow.

How do you size a cache? Intermediate

Access patterns are skewed (roughly a power law), so a small hot fraction serves most requests. A common rule of thumb is to cache the hottest 20 percent of daily data, and to confirm with the expected hit ratio.

Daily unique items read = 100 million, avg 1 KB each = 100 GB
Cache the hot 20%       = 20 GB
Add overhead (keys, pointers, fragmentation ~1.5x) = 30 GB
Replication/headroom    -> 2 to 3 nodes of 16 to 32 GB RAM

Then check throughput: one Redis node typically handles on the order of 50k to 100k simple operations per second (hardware and command dependent), so 30k peak QPS fits, but a hot key could still overload one shard. Sizing has two limits: memory (how much data) and ops per second (how much traffic); take the larger node count.

How do you estimate the number of servers? Intermediate

Servers = peak QPS / (QPS one server sustains), then add headroom and redundancy.

Peak 30,000 QPS; one app server handles about 1,000 QPS (measure it; depends on work per request)
30,000 / 1,000 = 30 servers
Run at 50 to 60% target utilisation -> about 50 servers
Spread over 3 zones so losing one zone still leaves enough: N+1 or 1.5x -> about 60

If a request spends 50 ms mostly waiting on I/O and a server runs 200 worker threads, throughput is about 200 / 0.05 = 4,000 QPS by Little's Law (concurrency = throughput x latency). CPU-bound work is limited by cores instead: cores / CPU time per request.

Why does the read/write ratio matter so much? Intermediate

Reads and writes scale by different tools:

WorkloadExampleScaling tools
Read heavy (100:1 or more)Product pages, feeds, URL redirectsCaching, read replicas, CDN, denormalised views
Write heavyMetrics, logs, IoT telemetrySharding, LSM-tree stores, batching, queues as buffers
BalancedChat, collaborative docsA mix, with care for ordering and fan-out

Reads scale by copying data; writes scale by splitting it, which is harder because splitting needs a partition key and breaks cross-shard transactions. So asking "what is the ratio?" early tells you which problem you actually have.

How do you sanity check an estimate? Intermediate
  • Compare to known scales: a very large global service has on the order of 100k to millions of QPS; if your small app says 5 million QPS, recheck.
  • Units: bytes versus bits, KB versus MB, per day versus per second.
  • Cross-check two ways: compute storage from users and from events; they should agree within a factor of 2 to 3.
  • Per-machine reality: does the per-node load fit what one node can really do (a database node rarely does more than low tens of thousands of simple queries per second, a disk a few hundred random IOPS if spinning)?
  • Order of magnitude only: write powers of ten (1e5) to avoid zero-counting errors.
What common mistakes happen in estimation? Intermediate
  • Using total registered users instead of DAU.
  • Forgetting peak factor, replication factor, indexes, or retention.
  • Mixing bits and bytes.
  • Treating all requests as equal; writes, uploads and searches cost far more than cached reads.
  • Over-precise arithmetic (86,400 and 1,048,576) wasting time.
  • Doing the maths and then not using it; every number should trigger a decision ("so we need a CDN", "so one node is enough").
How would you estimate the load of a fan-out (one write causing many deliveries)? Senior

Multiply the write rate by the average fan-out, but also look at the tail. If 1,000 posts per second each go to an average of 200 followers, delivery work is 200,000 writes per second. A user with 50 million followers turns a single post into 50 million operations, so averages hide the real problem. Estimate both: total average fan-out load (sizing) and maximum fan-out (design a special path, for example pull instead of push for very large accounts). Always ask for the distribution (average, p99, max), not only the mean.

Other useful values: 1 day = 86,400 s (about 1e5); 1 year = about 3e7 s; 1 million per day = about 12 per second; an ASCII character = 1 byte; a UUID = 16 bytes raw (36 as text); an int64 = 8 bytes; a 32-bit IPv4 address = 4 bytes; 1 Gbps = about 125 MB/s.

Also, 2^32 is about 4.3 billion (so a 32-bit id runs out), and a 64-bit id has about 1.8e19 values.

How many characters does a short code need to cover 5 billion items? Intermediate

A base-62 alphabet (a-z, A-Z, 0-9) gives 62^n combinations. 62^5 is about 916 million, 62^6 is about 56.8 billion, so 6 characters cover 5 billion, 7 characters (about 3.5 trillion) gives comfortable headroom. This pattern, "alphabet size to the power length", is the quick way to size any identifier space.

Numbers every engineer should know

What are the latency numbers every engineer should know? Basic

Popularised by Jeff Dean and updated by many authors. Exact values vary by hardware and era, so treat them as orders of magnitude.

OperationApprox time
L1 cache referenceabout 1 ns
Main memory (RAM) referenceabout 100 ns
Compress 1 KB with a fast codeca few microseconds
Read 1 MB sequentially from RAMtens of microseconds
SSD random readabout 100 microseconds (NVMe can be lower)
Round trip inside one data centerabout 0.5 ms
Read 1 MB sequentially from SSDabout 1 ms
Spinning disk seekabout 10 ms
Round trip across a continentabout 50 to 80 ms
Round trip between continentsabout 100 to 200 ms

Key lessons: memory is roughly 1,000 times faster than SSD for random access, a network hop in the same data center is about 5,000 times slower than a RAM read, and a cross-ocean trip is bounded by the speed of light (it cannot be engineered away, only avoided with caches, CDNs and regional deployments).

How do latency numbers change your design decisions? Give examples. Intermediate
  • Caching: a 0.5 ms cache hit versus a 5 to 10 ms database query makes caching hot data worthwhile.
  • Chatty services: 20 sequential calls at 1 ms plus processing is already tens of milliseconds; batch or parallelise.
  • Geography: a synchronous cross-region write costs at least one 100 ms+ round trip, so avoid it on the user's request path or accept async replication.
  • Disk: sequential writes (logs, LSM trees, Kafka) are far faster than random writes, which is why append-only designs scale.
  • Compress before sending over long distances; it is cheap compared with network time.
What rough throughput numbers should you remember for common components? Intermediate

Order-of-magnitude figures, highly dependent on workload and hardware, so say "about" and offer to measure:

ComponentRough per-node capability
Stateless web/app serverhundreds to a few thousand requests/s
Relational database (OLTP)thousands to low tens of thousands simple queries/s; far fewer writes with heavy transactions

Kafka brokerhundreds of MB/s with sequential I/O, many tens of thousands to hundreds of thousands msgs/s
Network interface1 to 25 Gbps typical in cloud, up to 100 Gbps on large instances

The skill is not memorising these but knowing which tier of the table (1k, 100k, 1M) your workload falls in, and choosing accordingly.

Core vocabulary and definitions

What is scalability? How is it different from performance? Basic

Scalability is the ability of a system to handle growing load (users, data, requests) by adding resources, without a redesign and without unacceptable drops in latency or availability. Performance is how fast it is at a given load. A system can be fast but not scalable (one beefy server that is great at 100 users and dead at 100,000), or scalable but slow per request (a distributed system with many hops). Good scalability means cost and latency grow gently as load grows, ideally linearly in cost.

Vertical versus horizontal scaling: what is the difference and when do you choose each? Basic
Vertical (scale up)Horizontal (scale out)
HowBigger machine: more CPU, RAM, diskMore machines behind a load balancer or partitioned
ComplexityLow; no code changeHigher; needs stateless design, partitioning, coordination
CeilingHard limit of largest machine; price rises steeplyPractically very high
FailureOne machine is a single point of failureSurvives node loss if designed for it
DowntimeOften needs a restart to resizeAdd nodes live

Pragmatic answer: scale up first while it is cheap and simple (a single database on a large instance serves a lot of products), and scale out the tiers that must, starting with the stateless application tier, which is easy. Data tiers are the hard part to scale out because state must be partitioned or replicated.

What is the difference between latency and throughput? Basic

Latency is the time for one operation (a request takes 80 ms). Throughput is how many operations complete per unit time (5,000 requests per second). They are related but independent: a highway with many lanes has high throughput, yet each car still takes an hour. Batching and pipelining raise throughput but often raise latency; parallelism raises throughput without changing single-request latency. Little's Law ties them together: in-flight requests = throughput x latency. As load nears capacity, queues form and latency rises sharply, so throughput targets must be paired with a latency target.

What is Little's Law and how do you use it? Intermediate

For a stable system, average number in the system = arrival rate x average time in the system (L = lambda x W). Uses:

  • Thread or connection pool size: 2,000 requests/s at 100 ms each needs about 200 concurrent requests in flight; a pool of 50 will queue.
  • Queue depth: if consumers take 20 ms per message and 1,000 messages/s arrive, you need about 20 concurrent consumers.
  • Detecting trouble: if latency doubles at the same arrival rate, in-flight work doubles, which can exhaust memory or threads.
What are "the nines" of availability? How much downtime does each allow? Basic

Availability = uptime / (uptime + downtime), expressed as a percentage of nines.

99.99% (four)52.6 minutes4.3 minutes
99.999% (five)5.3 minutes26 seconds

Each extra nine cuts the budget by 10x and costs far more: redundancy in every layer, multi-zone or multi-region, automated failover, and no manual steps. Ask whether the product truly needs it. Also note that a deploy that takes five minutes of downtime alone blows a four-nines budget.

How do you compute the availability of a system made of several components? Intermediate

In series (all must work): multiply. Three components at 99.9% each give 0.999 x 0.999 x 0.999 = about 99.7%. Chains of dependencies make availability worse than any single part, which is why long synchronous call chains hurt.

In parallel (redundant, any one suffices): 1 - (1 - a)^n. Two independent replicas at 99% each give 1 - 0.01 x 0.01 = 99.99%.

The parallel formula assumes independent failures. Shared causes (same zone, same bad deploy, same dependency, same config push) break the assumption, so real redundancy comes from independence: separate zones, separate failure domains, staggered rollouts.

Availability versus reliability versus durability: what is the difference? Intermediate
  • Availability: is the system up and responding now? (percentage of time or requests that succeed)
  • Reliability: does it give correct results consistently over time, with a low failure rate? A system can be available but returning wrong data, which is unreliable.
  • Durability: once data is acknowledged as saved, will it still be there later? Object stores advertise eleven nines of durability (annual object loss probability around 1e-11), yet their availability is lower, around 99.9 to 99.99 percent.

Example: a bank database that goes down for an hour is unavailable but durable; one that silently drops committed transactions has a durability failure, which is far worse.

What are MTBF and MTTR, and how do they relate to availability? Intermediate

MTBF is mean time between failures; MTTR is mean time to recover. Availability = MTBF / (MTBF + MTTR). To improve availability you either fail less often or recover faster. In modern cloud systems, reducing MTTR (fast detection, automated failover, easy rollback) is usually more achievable than reaching rare failures, since components fail constantly at scale.

Explain SLI, SLO and SLA with an example. Basic
TermMeaningExample
SLI (indicator)A measured number describing service behaviourFraction of requests under 300 ms and not an error
SLO (objective)Internal target for an SLI over a window99.9% of requests succeed in under 300 ms over 30 days
SLA (agreement)Contract with customers with consequences if missedBelow 99.5% monthly, customer gets service credits

The SLO is set tighter than the SLA so you are warned before you pay penalties. The gap between 100% and the SLO is the error budget: 99.9% leaves 0.1% (about 43 minutes a month) for failures, deploys and experiments. When the budget is used up, the team prioritises reliability work over features.

Why use p50, p95 and p99 latency instead of the average? Basic

The p99 latency is the value that 99 percent of requests are faster than. Latency distributions have a long tail: a few very slow requests are hidden by averages. If the average is 50 ms but p99 is 2 seconds, 1 in 100 users has a terrible experience, and heavy users (who make many requests) hit it often. Report p50 (typical), p95, p99 and sometimes p99.9, and set SLOs on percentiles. Never average percentiles across servers; merge the underlying histograms instead.

What is tail latency amplification and why does it matter in microservices? Senior

If a request calls many backends in parallel and waits for all, the chance of hitting at least one slow response grows quickly. If each backend is slow (above its p99) 1% of the time and you call 100 of them, the chance that at least one is slow is 1 - 0.99^100, about 63%. So the user's p50 starts to look like each service's p99.

Mitigations: reduce fan-out, set tight timeouts, hedged requests (send a second request after the p95 wait and take the first reply), good caching, load shedding, isolating slow neighbours, and avoiding garbage-collection pauses and noisy neighbours.

What is capacity planning and how do you do it? Intermediate
  1. Measure current peak usage and the capacity of one unit (requests per second per node, from a load test).
  2. Forecast growth (organic trend plus planned launches and seasonal events).
  3. Add headroom: run at about 50 to 70 percent of capacity at peak so a node failure or traffic spike does not tip you over; keep N+1 or N+2 redundancy.
  4. Account for lead time: hardware or cloud quotas and database migrations take time.
  5. Re-check regularly, and use autoscaling plus load shedding for what you cannot predict.

Why headroom: queueing theory says latency rises steeply as utilisation approaches 100 percent, so 90 percent utilisation at peak is already risky.

Define strong consistency, eventual consistency and CAP in one minute. Intermediate

Strong consistency: after a write completes, every reader sees it. Eventual consistency: if writes stop, all replicas converge, but readers may briefly see old data. CAP: during a network partition a distributed store must choose between consistency (reject or block some requests) and availability (answer, possibly with stale data). Without a partition you can have both, but there is still a latency versus consistency trade-off (the PACELC view). Choose per data type: strong for money and inventory, eventual for feeds and counters.

What does stateless mean and why is it important for scaling? Basic

A stateless server keeps no per-user data between requests; any state lives in a shared store (database, cache) or in a token sent by the client. Any server can then handle any request, so you add or remove servers freely, the load balancer needs no stickiness, and a crashed server loses nothing. State does not vanish; you push it to a tier built to hold it (databases, caches), which then becomes the hard part to scale.

What is a single point of failure and how do you remove one? Basic

A component whose failure takes down the whole system. Remove it with redundancy: multiple instances behind a load balancer, replicated databases with automated failover, multiple zones. Then check the next layer: the load balancer itself, DNS, the configuration service, the shared cache, a single certificate, even a single person who knows a procedure. Redundancy that is never tested (failover not rehearsed) is not real redundancy.

Picking between options and justifying the choice

How do you decide between two technologies or designs in an interview? Intermediate
  1. List the requirements that matter for this decision (consistency, query shape, write rate, latency, team skills).
  2. Name the two or three realistic options; do not invent strawmen.
  3. Compare on those criteria in a few words each, ideally with numbers.
  4. Choose and state the deciding factor.
  5. State the cost you accept and what would make you switch.
Say: "The access pattern is key-value lookups at 50k writes per second with no joins, so I choose a partitioned key-value store over a relational database. I lose ad-hoc queries and multi-row transactions; if the product later needs reporting I would stream the data into a warehouse."
How do you justify SQL versus NoSQL without sounding dogmatic? Intermediate

Anchor on access patterns, consistency needs and scale rather than fashion. Relational databases give transactions, joins, constraints and flexible queries, and scale far (one large node plus replicas handles most products). Choose a NoSQL family when the shape of data or load calls for it: key-value or wide-column for huge write rates and simple lookups, document for variable schemas read as a unit, graph for relationship traversals, search engines for text. Often the answer is polyglot: relational for the transactional core, cache and search alongside. Say what you give up (joins, transactions, secondary indexes) when you go NoSQL.

How do you use numbers to justify a decision? Intermediate

Turn a choice into a threshold. "Peak writes are about 3,000/s, one relational primary handles that comfortably, so no sharding yet. At 50x I would shard by user id." "Cache hit ratio of 90 percent cuts database reads from 30,000 to 3,000 per second, which one replica set can carry." A number plus a threshold shows the decision is reasoned and tells the interviewer when you would revisit it.

How do you choose between a managed service and building or self-hosting? Senior

Compare total cost of ownership, not licence price: engineers to operate, on-call burden, upgrade and security patching, scaling effort, and time to market. Managed services win for undifferentiated parts (databases, queues, object storage) where the team lacks deep expertise, at the price of vendor lock-in, less tuning control, and sometimes higher unit cost at large scale. Self-host or build when the component is core to your advantage, when scale makes the managed price prohibitive, or when requirements (latency, compliance, custom behaviour) cannot be met. Mention an exit path (open protocols, abstraction layer) to limit lock-in.

How do you treat reversible versus hard-to-reverse decisions? Senior

Spend design time in proportion to the cost of being wrong. Hard to reverse: the primary data store, the sharding key, public API contracts, id formats, event schemas, and cloud region strategy. Easy to reverse: cache technology, instance sizes, internal library choices. For hard ones, do the analysis and write down assumptions; for easy ones, pick a sensible default and move on. In an interview, flag this explicitly: "the shard key is the decision I would want to get right early".

Scenario: the interviewer will not give you scale numbers ("whatever you think"). What do you do? Intermediate

Make a clear, reasonable assumption and anchor it: "I will design for 10 million DAU, growing to 100 million, with 10 to 1 reads to writes. Is that in the right range?" Then check that the design works at the stated size and say how it changes at 10x. Choosing a plausible figure and sticking to it is better than refusing to commit.

Scenario: two designs both seem valid and you cannot decide in the interview. How do you proceed? Intermediate

Say so and decide anyway, using a tie-breaker: choose the simpler one, or the one cheaper to change later, or the one that fits the team's skills. Give the other as the alternative and the condition under which it wins ("if writes grow past one primary's capacity I would move to the partitioned option"). Interviewers prefer a justified decision over an endless comparison.

Scenario: in production p50 latency is fine but users complain and p99 is 5 seconds. How do you investigate? Senior
  1. Confirm with histograms by endpoint, region, tenant and hour; find whether the slow requests share a trait.
  2. Use distributed traces of slow requests to see which hop or dependency dominates.
  3. Check usual tail causes: garbage-collection pauses, a cold cache or cache stampede, a slow shard or noisy neighbour, lock contention, retries and timeouts stacking, connection pool exhaustion, a few huge payloads, and unindexed queries for particular tenants.
  4. Mitigate: tighter timeouts, hedged requests, bulkheads, pagination limits, or caching the heavy path.
  5. Add an SLO alert on p99 so you notice before users do.
Scenario: marketing announces a launch that may bring 10x traffic in two weeks. What do you do? Senior

Get numbers from marketing (expected peak, duration, which endpoints). Load test at the expected peak plus margin and find the first bottleneck (often the database, connection limits, a third-party API, or a rate limit). Pre-scale or pre-warm (caches, autoscaling minimums, quotas raised with the cloud provider), add load shedding and a degraded mode (disable expensive features), set a feature flag kill switch, prepare a runbook, and watch dashboards live with named on-call owners. After the event, review actual versus forecast and update the plan.
