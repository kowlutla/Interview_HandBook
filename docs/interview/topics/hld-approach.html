How to use this page

Each question is a collapsible. Try to answer aloud for 60 seconds before opening it. Every answer starts with a short Say this line you could speak in about 20 seconds, then explains the idea from scratch so you can learn it, not only recite it. The first half is about process (how to run the hour), the second half is about numbers and vocabulary (what to calculate and what words to use precisely). No full design problems are here; those live in the Handbook case studies.

The interview approach and time split

What is a system design interview really testing? Basic
Say this: "It is not a quiz on the one right architecture. It tests whether I can turn a vague problem into requirements and numbers, choose sensible building blocks, go deep on the risky parts, justify trade-offs, and work with the interviewer."

What it is. A system design interview (often called HLD, high-level design) gives you an open-ended prompt such as "design a photo sharing service" and about 45 to 60 minutes. There is no single correct answer, and the interviewer usually knows several valid ones. What they score is how you think.

Why it is built this way. Real senior work is rarely "implement this function". It is "the business wants X, we have Y engineers and Z budget, what do we build and what could go wrong?" The interview is a compressed version of that. A candidate who memorised the design of Twitter fails the moment the interviewer changes one requirement; a candidate who reasons from requirements adapts.

The interviewer is usually filling in a scorecard with these rows:

  • Structured thinking: can you take a vague prompt and turn it into requirements, numbers and a plan, in a sensible order?
  • Breadth: do you know the standard building blocks (load balancer, cache, queue, database families, CDN, object storage) and what each one costs you?
  • Depth: on one or two parts, can you go below the box and explain the mechanism, the failure modes and the numbers? "Use Kafka" is breadth; "partition by user id so one user's events stay ordered, and here is what happens when a consumer dies" is depth.
  • Trade-off judgement: do you pick and justify, instead of listing technologies? Every choice gains something and costs something; naming both is the skill.
  • Communication and collaboration: do you listen, take hints, narrate, and stay calm under pushback? Interviewers often ask themselves, "would I enjoy designing this with this person?"

What changes at senior level. You are expected to drive: you propose the plan, you raise failure modes, operations and cost before being asked, and you connect choices to team size and timeline ("this needs a team of five to run, we have two, so I would buy it managed").

Pitfall. Treating it as a memory test and reciting a known diagram. Interviewers probe one level below whatever you say, so the answer must come from understanding.

Walk me through the steps you follow in a system design interview. Basic
Say this: "I will start with requirements and rough numbers, define the API and data model, sketch the end-to-end design, then go deep where you want, cover bottlenecks and failures, and finish with a summary. Tell me if you would rather steer elsewhere."

Why a fixed framework at all. Under pressure, people either start drawing boxes immediately or freeze. A repeatable sequence means you always know the next move, it ensures you do not forget numbers or failure handling, and it lets the interviewer follow you. Think of it as a checklist you never have to improvise.

  1. Clarify requirements (functional and non-functional) and agree scope. Output: a short list on the board.
  2. State assumptions and estimate scale: users, QPS (queries per second), storage, bandwidth. Output: a handful of numbers and the realisation of what is hard (for example "reads dominate, 50,000 per second at peak").
  3. Define the API and core data model in a few lines. Output: 3 to 5 endpoints, 2 to 4 entities.
  4. Draw the high-level design: clients, edge (CDN, load balancer), services, data stores, async parts. Walk one read and one write through it.
  5. Deep dive on the 2 or 3 hardest or riskiest components, chosen with the interviewer.
  6. Address bottlenecks and failures: single points of failure, hot keys, retries, overload, multi-region.
  7. Wrap up: summarise decisions, name what you would monitor, and list what you would do next with more time.

Tiny example of the flow. Prompt: "design a notification service." Step 1: push, email, SMS? 10M users? Step 2: 10M users x 5 notifications a day = 50M/day, about 500 per second average, about 5,000 per second peak. Step 3: POST /notify, a Notification record. Step 4: API, queue, per-channel workers, provider adapters. Step 5: deep dive on delivery guarantees and retries. Step 6: what if the SMS provider is down? Step 7: summary.

Pitfall. Following the steps mechanically. If the interviewer is clearly interested in one area, the framework bends: you do not finish an exhaustive estimate when they want to talk about consistency.

How would you split 45 minutes? Basic
Say this: "Roughly: 5 to 7 minutes on requirements, 3 to 5 on estimates, 3 to 5 on API and data model, 8 to 10 on the high-level design, 12 to 15 on deep dives, and 2 to 3 to wrap up. The deep dive gets the biggest slice."

Why the split matters. The score mostly comes from depth and judgement, and those only appear in the later phases. If you burn 20 minutes on requirements and arithmetic, the interviewer sees organised preparation but no evidence of engineering depth. If you skip straight to detail with no big picture, the interviewer cannot tell whether the detail fits.

PhaseMinutes (45 min slot)Goal
Requirements, scope, assumptions5 to 7Shared definition of the problem
Estimation (light)3 to 5Order of magnitude, find the bottleneck
API and data model3 to 5Contract and entities
High-level design8 to 10End-to-end picture, both flows
Deep dives12 to 15Mechanism, trade-offs, failures
Wrap-up2 to 3Summary, monitoring, next steps

Those add up to 33 to 45 minutes at the extremes; in practice you will be near the middle of each range, and a 60-minute slot simply gives more room for deep dives.

How to keep time. Glance at a clock at the end of each phase. A good habit is to announce transitions ("I have the requirements; I will do a quick estimate, then sketch the design"), which also tells the interviewer where you are.

Guides, not rules. If the interviewer clearly wants 25 minutes on one deep dive, go with it; they are telling you where the score is. The usual failures are (a) 20 minutes on requirements and estimation with no depth, and (b) no big picture before diving into detail.

You are 30 minutes in and have only drawn boxes. What do you do? Intermediate
Say this: "I have the overall shape; given the time left, which part would you like me to go deep on?" Then pick the riskiest component and cover mechanism, trade-off and failure.

Why this is a real problem. A diagram of boxes proves breadth only. Interviewers cannot give "senior" credit without seeing at least one place where you understand the internals. At minute 30 of 45 you have roughly 12 minutes, so you cannot fix everything; you must choose.

What to do, step by step.

  1. Name the situation out loud. It is honest, shows self-awareness, and hands the interviewer a choice, which is also a chance to learn what they care about.
  2. Pick one component that is the riskiest or most distinctive for this problem (the fan-out for a feed, the sharding key for a large table, the exactly-once concern for payments).
  3. Go through it fully: the problem, two options, your pick and why, how it fails, what you monitor. That pattern fills 6 to 8 minutes.
  4. Use the remaining minutes for a quick failure pass over the rest ("single database is a single point of failure, so a replica with automated failover") and a 60-second wrap-up.

If you skipped the numbers, do a 60-second estimate for the one decision that depends on them. Example: "peak writes are about 2,000 per second, so one relational primary is enough; I will not shard."

Pitfall. Rushing through all five remaining boxes with one sentence each. That repeats the mistake at lower resolution. Depth on one part beats shallow coverage of five.

Why not start drawing right away? What goes wrong if you skip requirements? Basic
Say this: "The prompt is deliberately vague, and different answers to a few questions lead to different architectures. If I skip them I design for an imagined problem and cannot defend my choices."

The problem. "Design a photo sharing app" hides dozens of decisions. Is it 10,000 users or 100 million? Must a new photo appear in followers' feeds within a second, or is a minute fine? Is it one country or global? Can we ever lose a photo? Each answer moves the design.

A concrete before and after.

  • Answer A: "10,000 users, one country, a minute of staleness is fine." A single app server, one PostgreSQL, object storage for photos. Done in ten minutes.
  • Answer B: "100 million daily users, global, feed must be fresh within seconds." CDN, sharded metadata, a fan-out pipeline, multi-region replication.

Same prompt, two completely different systems. Jump in without asking and you will either over-build A or under-build B, and then you cannot say which, because you have no stated requirement to point at.

Why interviewers care. Five minutes of questions shows you work like a senior engineer: you do not build before you understand. It also gives you a rubric: later you can say "we agreed feeds can be 5 seconds stale, so a cache is fine" instead of arguing from taste.

Pitfall the other way. Asking for 15 minutes. Ask questions that change the design (see the clarifying questions answer), then commit to assumptions.

Who should drive the interview, you or the interviewer? Intermediate
Say this: "I drive, the interviewer steers. I propose the plan and narrate transitions, but I follow any hint or question immediately."

What it means. Driving is not talking nonstop. It means you always know what is next and you say it. The interviewer's job is to observe, not to run the session, and a candidate who waits to be told what to do looks junior. At the same time, the interviewer has a scorecard and may want to see a specific topic, so you must leave them room to redirect.

Concrete behaviours.

  • Propose the plan up front: "I will cover requirements, a quick estimate, the API, then the design, and I would like to save time for one deep dive."
  • Narrate transitions: "Now I will cover the write path."
  • Offer choices at decision points: "Two options here, polling or push; I lean push because of the freshness requirement; happy to go deeper on either."
  • Check in every few minutes: "Does this match what you had in mind?"
  • Treat questions as steering. A hint or a question means they want something; follow it, then return to your plan.

Silence. Thinking is fine if you announce it: "give me a moment to think about the failure case". Long unexplained silence gives the interviewer nothing to score, and they may assume you are stuck. Equally, a 10-minute monologue with no check-in risks heading down a path they do not care about.

Pitfall. Two extremes: passively waiting for instructions, or steamrolling past a hint because it breaks your plan.

How does the expected answer differ between a mid-level and a senior engineer? Senior
Say this: "A mid-level answer lists reasonable components. A senior answer explains why each exists and what it costs, walks through failures and recovery, states alternatives and what would change the decision, and covers operations and evolution."

Why levels differ. Companies hire seniors to make decisions that are expensive to reverse and to prevent incidents, so the interview checks for exactly those habits: asking what could go wrong, tying designs to cost and people, and sequencing the build.

AreaMid-levelSenior
RequirementsLists featuresPins down non-functional targets and questions the premise
DesignReasonable boxesExplains why each box exists and what it costs
FailureMentions replicasWalks through what breaks, blast radius, recovery, data loss window
Trade-offsStates a choiceStates alternatives, the deciding factor, and what would change the decision
OperationsRarelyMetrics, alerts, rollout, migration, cost, team skills
EvolutionFinal design onlySimple v1, then what changes at 10x

Same decision, two levels. Question: "Which database for orders?" Mid-level: "PostgreSQL, because it is relational." Senior: "PostgreSQL. Orders need multi-row transactions and constraints, peak is about 2,000 writes per second which one primary handles, a replica gives read scale and failover, and I would accept up to a few seconds of replication lag on the order-history page but not on checkout. If we reach 50x I would shard by customer id. Backups with point-in-time recovery are in the plan."

Notice the senior answer is not longer for its own sake; each added clause is a requirement, number, failure case or switch condition.

Pitfall. "Senior" does not mean using fancier technology. Overbuilding is itself a junior signal.

Requirements, scope and assumptions

What clarifying questions should you ask, and in what order? Basic
Say this: "Who uses it and for what, at what scale, read or write heavy, how fast and how fresh, how available and consistent, whether we may lose data, how global, and what constraints exist. I only ask what changes the design."

Why these questions. Each one maps to an architectural decision. The order moves from "what is the product" to "how demanding is it" to "what limits us", so each answer sets up the next.

  1. Users and use cases: who uses it, what are the top 3 to 5 actions? (Decides which components exist.)
  2. Scale: how many users (and daily active), requests per user per day, data size per item? (Decides single node versus distributed.)
  3. Read or write heavy, and are there bursts (sales, live events)? (Decides caching versus sharding, and peak sizing.)
  4. Latency and freshness: how fast must a response be; can data be a few seconds stale? (Decides cache and replication strategy.)
  5. Availability and consistency: what happens if it is down for a minute; can two users briefly see different data? (Decides CAP-style choices.)
  6. Durability: can we ever lose data? Payments: never; view counts: maybe. (Decides replication and write acknowledgement.)
  7. Geography: one region or global; any data residency rules? (Decides multi-region design.)
  8. Constraints: existing systems, team size, budget, mobile versus web.

Good versus bad questions. "Is it acceptable to lose a like during a failover?" changes the design (async versus sync replication). "What colour is the button?" does not. A useful trick is to phrase a question as an assumption you will take if they have no preference: "I will assume 50 million daily users and eventual consistency for the feed; shout if that is wrong."

Pitfall. Asking all eight questions as a long interrogation. Group them, accept "your call" as an answer, and move on within 5 to 7 minutes.

What is the difference between functional and non-functional requirements? Give examples. Basic
Say this: "Functional requirements say what the system does; non-functional say how well. The functional ones tell me which components exist; the non-functional ones decide the architecture."

Functional requirements describe behaviour, usually as verbs: "a user can upload a photo", "follow another user", "see a feed". They are features you could demo.

Non-functional requirements (also called quality attributes or "-ilities") describe qualities:

  • Scale: 100 million daily users.
  • Latency: feed loads in under 300 ms at p99.
  • Availability: 99.99 percent.
  • Consistency: a user sees their own post immediately (read-your-writes).
  • Durability: no acknowledged upload is ever lost.
  • Security, cost, operability, compliance.

Why the second kind decides the architecture. Take the single feature "store and fetch a photo". At 1,000 users, one server and one database do it. At 100 million users with a 99.99 percent target, it becomes a CDN in front of a replicated, partitioned object store, plus a sharded metadata database, plus monitoring and multi-zone failover. The feature is identical; the non-functional numbers changed everything.

How to use this in the interview. Ask for functional requirements to know what to draw, then deliberately ask for numbers on the non-functional ones and write them in a corner. Later, each design choice should point to one of them.

Pitfall. Listing only features and never getting any numbers. Without them, "we need a cache" is an opinion.

How do you scope the problem, and why state assumptions out loud? Basic
Say this: "I will write three lists: in scope, nice to have, and out of scope, and confirm them. Then I state numeric assumptions so later decisions can point at them."

Why scope. Every real product is too big for 45 minutes. The interviewer is watching whether you can choose a coherent slice rather than boil the ocean. Choosing and defending scope is itself a senior skill.

How, for a photo app.

  • In scope (designed in detail): upload photo, follow user, view home feed.
  • Nice to have (one sentence each): likes, comments, search.
  • Out of scope (explicitly dropped): stories, ads, direct messages, billing, admin tools.

Ask the interviewer to confirm. If they say "include likes", you have lost nothing.

Why assumptions out loud. You rarely get exact numbers. So you create them: "100M DAU, each uploads 0.1 photos a day, average photo 2 MB, 5-year retention." Now they are shared facts. When you later say "this needs sharding", you can point at the number rather than at a feeling.

If the interviewer disagrees ("make it 1 billion"), that is a win: it is cheap to correct at minute 8 and expensive at minute 40. It also shows them where the problem is hard for them.

Pitfall. Silent assumptions. If you assume strong consistency and the interviewer assumed eventual, you will argue past each other for ten minutes.

The interviewer says "make it highly available and strongly consistent and cheap". How do you respond? Intermediate
Say this: "Those pull against each other, so let me ask which data must be strongly consistent. I will use strong consistency on the small critical part and cheaper eventual consistency for the bulk."

Why they conflict.

  • Consistency versus availability: during a network partition (two groups of servers cannot talk), a store must either refuse some requests to stay consistent, or answer from possibly stale data to stay available. This is the CAP theorem. Even with no partition, strong consistency across regions costs at least one cross-region round trip per write (50 to 200 ms), so there is a latency price.
  • Availability versus cost: availability comes from redundancy: extra servers, extra zones, extra regions, tested failover. Each costs money and operational effort.

What a senior does. Do not agree to all three, and do not lecture. Ask for priorities per data type. For example, in a shopping system:

DataNeedApproach
Payments, inventory countStrong consistency, no lossSingle-leader relational DB, synchronous replica in same region
Product catalogue, reviewsAvailable, can be staleReplicated, cached, eventual
View counters, recommendationsCheap, approximate fineAsync, batched

This splits the system by data type, which gives the interviewer what they asked for in the places where it matters and keeps cost down elsewhere. That is the real senior answer.

Pitfall. Saying "just use a distributed database that gives all three". It does not exist; every product makes a trade-off, and the interviewer will ask which.

Why define the API and data model before the architecture? Intermediate
Say this: "The API fixes what crosses the boundary and the data model fixes what is stored and how it is queried. Access patterns decide the database and the sharding key, so I define them before drawing boxes."

Why it comes first. Architecture is a response to how data is read and written. Consider "fetch the last 20 posts of a user" versus "find all posts mentioning a word": the first wants an index on (user, time) in almost any store; the second wants a search engine. If you pick a database before listing the queries, you may pick the wrong family.

How much to write. Three to five endpoints and the main entities with key fields. For a photo app:

POST /photos            body: image + caption  -> 201 {photo_id}
GET  /feed?cursor=abc   -> {items:[...], next_cursor}
POST /follow/{user_id}

Photo(id, owner_id, created_at, blob_url, caption)
Follow(follower_id, followee_id, created_at)

From this you can already read off design facts: the feed is paginated by a cursor (not page numbers, which break when new items arrive); Photo lookups are by id and by owner and time, which suggests a partition key of owner_id; the file bytes belong in object storage with only the URL in the database.

One-liners to add. Pagination (cursor-based), idempotency for writes (a client-supplied key so a retry does not create two photos), and authentication (a token in a header).

Pitfall. Spending 10 minutes on perfect REST naming. The point is access patterns, not syntax.

The prompt is a single word ("design Instagram"). How do you avoid designing everything? Basic
Say this: "The product is huge, so I will propose a core slice, for example upload, follow and view feed, skip stories, ads and DMs, and confirm that with you."

Why. A one-word prompt is intentional. Instagram has hundreds of engineers and dozens of subsystems. The interviewer cannot expect a complete design in 45 minutes; they want to see whether you can cut a problem down to something that fits and still shows interesting engineering.

How to choose the slice.

  1. Pick the core user journey (post something, others see it).
  2. Prefer features that exercise interesting systems problems: the feed involves fan-out, caching and ranking; photo upload involves large blobs and a CDN. Prefer these over a login screen.
  3. State it in one sentence, ask "does this match what you had in mind?", then write the out-of-scope list.

Keep the out-of-scope list; in the wrap-up you can say "with more time I would add search and stories, and here is how the design would extend".

Pitfall. Either refusing to pick ("it depends what you want") or picking a slice so trivial that there is nothing to discuss.

Drawing the high-level design and going deep

How do you draw a good high-level design? Basic
Say this: "I start at the client and move inward, keep it to about 6 to 10 boxes, label every arrow, separate the read path from the write path and sync from async, and trace one request of each kind end to end."

What a high-level design is. A one-page picture of the major components and how data flows between them. It is the map the rest of the interview uses; the deep dives zoom into parts of it.

Why this style. A diagram that is too sparse says nothing; one with 30 boxes and technology names is unreadable and hides your reasoning. Starting from the client gives a natural order and mirrors what a request actually does.

  • Order: client, DNS and CDN (content delivery network: caches near users), load balancer or API gateway, stateless services, caches, databases, queues and workers.
  • Label arrows with what flows (a request, an event, a file). "Arrow" without a label hides decisions.
  • Separate read and write paths and walk each aloud. Example write: client sends photo, service stores the file in object storage, writes a metadata row, publishes a "photo created" event. Example read: client asks for feed, service checks cache, falls back to database.
  • Split synchronous from asynchronous. Synchronous means the user waits (store the photo). Asynchronous means a queue and workers do it later (generate thumbnails, fan out to followers).
  • Name the store for each kind of data and why: relational for orders, object storage for files, cache for hot reads.
  • Leave detail for the deep dive. Do not name Kafka, Redis and Cassandra in the first draw unless you can say why.

Read the figure left to right: a request passes the edge, reaches a stateless service, which checks the cache for reads and writes to the database, then drops a message on a queue for slower follow-up work (thumbnails, notifications, search indexing) so the user is not kept waiting.

Pitfall. Drawing a perfect diagram and never narrating a request through it. The walk-through is where the interviewer verifies you understand it.

How do you choose what to deep dive into? Intermediate
Say this: "I pick the parts that are hardest or most unusual for this problem, where my estimates showed a bottleneck, or where the interviewer showed interest, and I ask which they prefer."

Why deep dives exist. The high-level design proves breadth, but breadth is cheap to fake. A deep dive proves you can reason about mechanism: what actually happens inside the box, how it fails, and what it costs. It is usually where a "hire" becomes a "strong hire".

How to choose. Three filters:

  1. Hardest for this problem. A chat app: message ordering and delivery. A rate limiter: the counting algorithm and distributed counters. A feed: fan-out.
  2. Estimates flagged it. If you computed 30,000 reads per second, caching and replicas deserve depth; if you found 50 PB of storage, partitioning and tiering do.
  3. Interviewer interest. Offer the menu: "I see two risky parts: the fan-out and the feed ranking store. Which would you like me to go into?"

A repeatable pattern for each dive: what problem, the options, my choice and why, how it fails, what we monitor. Example for fan-out: problem (a post must reach 200 followers' feeds fast); options (push on write versus pull on read); choice (push for normal users, pull for celebrities); failure (a queue backlog delays feeds, so we monitor lag); monitoring (p99 time to appear in feed).

Common deep-dive areas: data model and sharding key, caching and invalidation, fan-out strategy, consistency of one critical write, and overload handling.

Pitfall. Diving into something easy you know well (a REST layer) because it feels safe, while skipping the hard bit.

How do you find bottlenecks in your own design? Intermediate
Say this: "For each component I ask three questions: what happens at 10x load, what happens when it dies, and what happens when it is slow. Then I pair each finding with a mitigation."

What a bottleneck is. The one resource that hits its limit first and caps the whole system: one database CPU, one network link, one hot partition. Improving anything else does nothing until it is fixed.

Why you must look for them yourself. The interviewer will probe your design; finding the weak spots first shows ownership. It is also what real design reviews and incident post-mortems do.

The three questions, with examples.

  • 10x load: which component saturates first? A single database primary at 10x write rate, a queue broker's disk, a connection pool.
  • It dies: is there a single point of failure? One database, one queue broker, one load balancer, one DNS provider, one config service.
  • It is slow (often worse than dead, because callers keep waiting and pile up): what do callers do? Do timeouts exist? Do retries multiply the load?

Also look for hot spots (a celebrity user, one popular key that overloads one shard), unbounded growth (queue depth, table size, log volume), chatty calls (a request fanning out to 30 services), and locks held across network calls.

Mitigation menu: replicate, shard, cache, add a queue to absorb bursts, rate limit, set timeouts and circuit breakers, degrade gracefully (serve stale data or disable a feature).

Pitfall. Naming a bottleneck without a fix, or "fixing" it by adding a box that creates a new single point of failure.

How should you wrap up a design? Basic
Say this: "Quick summary against the requirements, the key trade-offs I accepted, what I would monitor and alert on, and the next steps or open risks."

Why wrap up. The last two minutes shape the interviewer's memory and their written feedback. A design with no ending feels unfinished even if the content was good. A crisp close shows you can finish a design, not just start one, and connects the pieces back to the requirements you agreed at the start.

  1. Summarise in 30 seconds against the requirements. "Reads at 50k per second are served mostly from cache with a 90 percent hit ratio; writes are durable through a replicated primary; the feed is eventually consistent by a few seconds."
  2. Name the trade-offs you accepted. "Eventual consistency for feeds in exchange for low latency; higher storage cost from denormalised feeds."
  3. Say what you would monitor and what pages someone: latency percentiles, error rate, queue lag, cache hit ratio, replication lag.
  4. List next steps or open risks: multi-region, abuse protection, cost review, migration plan, the features left out of scope.

Pitfall. Running out the clock mid-sentence. Keep two minutes in reserve and announce "let me wrap up".

Should you begin with a simple design and evolve it, or present the final scaled design? Intermediate
Say this: "I start simple, one app server and one database, show where it breaks at the stated load, then evolve it step by step. Every addition is tied to a concrete pain."

Why evolve. Presenting the final 15-box architecture at once is a recitation, and it invites "why do you need that?" which you cannot answer without the story of how it became necessary. An evolving design also mirrors how real systems grow, and it avoids over-engineering.

A typical evolution, each step tied to a pain.

  1. v1: one app server plus one database. Fine for thousands of users.
  2. Single server dies or saturates: add a load balancer and stateless replicas.
  3. Database reads dominate: add a cache, then read replicas.
  4. Slow work blocks users: add a queue and workers.
  5. Static files and global users: add a CDN.
  6. Writes exceed one primary: shard.
  7. Regional outage or latency: multi-region.

Do not stay simple forever. If your estimate says 200,000 QPS, say plainly that a single database cannot serve it and jump to the scaled design. Evolution is a way to explain, not an excuse to avoid committing. A good compromise: draw the simple version in 2 minutes, mark where the estimates break it, then draw the scaled version and say which parts you added and why.

Pitfall. Spending 15 minutes on the simple version. Keep it brief.

Trade-offs, hints, pushback and common mistakes

How do you communicate a trade-off well? Intermediate
Say this: "Option, gain, cost, deciding requirement. And I always name the alternative I rejected."

Why a formula helps. Trade-offs are the main thing being scored, but under pressure people give vague statements ("it depends", "X is better"). A four-part sentence forces a complete thought, and the interviewer can check each part.

The four parts.

  1. Option: what you choose.
  2. Gain: what you get.
  3. Cost: what you give up or risk.
  4. Deciding requirement: why the cost is acceptable here.
Example: "I will use asynchronous replication to the second region. We gain low write latency, because the write does not wait for the far region. We risk losing the last second or so of writes in a regional failure. The requirement says a few seconds of loss is acceptable for comments, so I accept that. For payments I would not."

Notice the last two sentences: the decision is tied to a stated requirement and the answer shows when the decision would flip. That is what separates judgement from preference.

On "it depends". It is fine only if followed by "on X, and given X here, I choose Y". Alone, it sounds like avoiding a decision.

Pitfall. Presenting only the benefits of your choice. If you never state a cost, the interviewer will ask for one, and it looks like you had not thought about it.

The interviewer gives a hint. How should you react? Basic
Say this: "Good point, that is the hot key problem." Acknowledge it, restate to confirm, and incorporate it.

Why hints happen. Interviewers hint for two reasons: to unblock you when you are stuck, or to nudge you toward the area they are meant to evaluate. A hint is therefore not a verdict that you are failing; it is a free gift of direction, and often a signal of where the score is.

What to do.

  1. Acknowledge it briefly.
  2. Restate it to confirm you understood: "You are saying one celebrity user could overload a shard?"
  3. Use it. Work it into the design: "So I would handle large accounts differently: pull instead of push."

What not to do.

  • Defend the old approach out of ego.
  • Ignore the hint and carry on, which suggests you did not listen.
  • Over-apologise or deflate; just move on.

Candidates who use hints well often score higher than ones who happened not to need them, because collaborating on feedback is the actual job.

The interviewer challenges your choice ("why not just use X?"). What do you do? Intermediate
Say this: "Fair question. X would give us A, and here is why I still prefer my choice given the requirements; but if B matters more than we agreed, X is better and I would switch."

Why they push back. Either it is a genuine alternative they like, or they are testing whether your reasoning holds up and whether you fold or dig in blindly. Both are good signs: a challenge means they are engaged. Your job is to demonstrate reasoning, not to win.

  1. Stay calm and do not get defensive. Assume a real question.
  2. Restate the alternative fairly, including its real benefits. This shows you understand it.
  3. Compare against the agreed requirements and numbers. Often your choice still wins, and you can say why.
  4. If their point is right, change the design openly: "That is a better fit because of Z; I would switch, and the impact is..."
  5. If you do not know, say so and reason from first principles.

Example. "Why not just use a SQL database for the feed?" Answer: "For 5,000 feed reads per second SQL with an index on (user, time) works. At the 100x scale we assumed, a single primary struggles, and the feed is a precomputed list, which suits a key-value store. If we stayed at 5,000 I would pick SQL, since it is simpler."

Changing your mind for a good reason signals seniority; changing it because they frowned, with no reason, signals you have no conviction. Holding firm with no reasoning is just as bad.

What do you do when you do not know a technology the interviewer mentions? Basic
Say this: "I have not used that one. From the name I assume it is a distributed log, similar to Kafka, where we would do X. Is that close?"

Why honesty wins. No one knows every product. A system design interview checks concepts, and concepts transfer. Bluffing is the worst option because one follow-up question exposes it, and it damages trust in everything else you said.

The bridge technique.

  1. Admit you have not used it.
  2. Guess its category from the name or context, and say so as a guess.
  3. Reason from properties instead of product names: ordering, durability, throughput, consistency, query model.
  4. Connect to something you know and ask for confirmation.

Example: the interviewer says "what about using DynamoDB here?" and you have only used Cassandra. Say "I know Cassandra; I understand DynamoDB is a similar partitioned key-value store with a partition key and sort key. If so, the access pattern here, lookup by user and time range, fits, and the hot-partition issue we discussed applies as well."

Pitfall. Overconfident wrong claims about details you are guessing. Mark guesses as guesses.

What are the most common mistakes in system design interviews? Basic
Say this: "Designing before clarifying, name-dropping technologies without reasons, skipping numbers, over-engineering, leaving single points of failure, never going deep, and not wrapping up."

Knowing the list helps you self-correct in the room. Each mistake and why it hurts:

  • Designing before clarifying or assuming silently: you solve a problem nobody posed.
  • Name-dropping ("use Kafka and Redis and Cassandra"): lists tools instead of reasons, so the interviewer cannot tell if you understand any of them.
  • Skipping numbers, or spending 15 minutes on exact arithmetic: both fail; you want a factor-of-two estimate that triggers a decision.
  • Over-engineering: microservices, multi-region and sharding for 1,000 users shows poor judgement.
  • Single points of failure left in the diagram, and ignoring failure entirely.
  • Never going deep: everything stays at box level.
  • Silence, or monologue without checking in.
  • One-sided trade-offs: "Cassandra is just better".
  • Ignoring or arguing with hints.
  • No wrap-up, no monitoring, no mention of operations.

A pattern behind them. Almost all are either "not enough thinking visible" (silence, no reasons, no numbers) or "wrong level of ambition" (too little or too much). If you keep asking "what requirement justifies this?" you avoid most of them.

How do you avoid over-engineering in an interview and in real life? Intermediate
Say this: "Numbers justify every component. If a box does not solve a stated problem, I remove it. I say what I would add at 10x or 100x, but I do not build it now."

What over-engineering is. Building capacity or flexibility nobody needs yet: sharding at 500 QPS, microservices for a team of three, multi-region for a regional product.

Why it hurts. Each added piece (cache, queue, shard, extra region, new service) brings operational cost, new failure modes and consistency questions. A cache can serve stale data; a queue can lose or duplicate messages; a shard breaks cross-shard transactions. You pay these costs every day, even when the benefit never appears.

A concrete example. Peak is 200 QPS and 50 GB of data. A correct design: two stateless servers behind a load balancer, one well-indexed relational database with a replica for failover and backups. State this confidently, then add: "if it grows 100x (20,000 QPS) the first thing to break is the database, and the next step is a cache and read replicas; sharding comes much later".

Test to apply. "What requirement would break if I removed this box?" If none, remove it. Complement: "what number would make me add it?" If you can state the threshold, you can defer it.

Pitfall. Overcorrecting into under-engineering: never mention redundancy or backups because the load is small. A simple system still needs a failover story.

Any practical tips for the whiteboard or shared doc? Basic
Say this: "Keep requirements and numbers visible, use consistent shapes, number the steps of a flow, leave space, and check in often."

The interviewer reads your diagram while listening, and often takes notes from it afterwards. A clean board makes your thinking look clean.

  • Write requirements and key numbers in a corner and refer back: "the 30k peak QPS from earlier".
  • Consistent shapes: boxes for services, cylinders for stores, arrows with labels; a dashed line for async.
  • Number the steps of a flow (1, 2, 3) and narrate while drawing, so the picture and your words say the same thing.
  • Leave space: you will insert a cache or queue later; a packed diagram forces an ugly redraw.
  • Check in every few minutes: "does this make sense so far?"
  • Draw while talking. Silent drawing wastes time; talking without drawing loses the visual.

In a virtual interview with a shared doc, prefer simple text-and-box layouts to fancy tools; time spent aligning shapes is time not spent reasoning.

Scenario: the interviewer says "skip the estimates, just design it". Do you? Intermediate
Say this: "Sure. I will assume around 10k reads per second and 1k writes per second so my choices stay consistent."

Why not truly skip. You cannot decide whether to shard, cache or use one database without some sense of scale. The interviewer is not forbidding thinking about scale; they are saving time and probably do not want a long arithmetic detour.

What to do. Respect the request and do not argue. Silently keep a rough order of magnitude in your head, and state it in one sentence as an assumption, as above. Everything afterwards refers to it ("at 10k reads per second one cache node is enough"). If your numbers are far from what the interviewer had in mind, they will say so, which is useful information at no cost.

Why this works. It satisfies both sides: you follow their instruction, and the design remains grounded. The risk of "no numbers at all" is that you silently design for the wrong scale and the interviewer cannot tell.

Scenario: at minute 35 the interviewer adds a new requirement ("now it must work in 3 regions"). How do you handle it? Senior
Say this: "Let me ask what matters most: local latency, disaster recovery or data residency. Then I will show which parts change and which stay."

What this tests. Adaptability, not whether your first design was perfect. Real requirements change late, and the skill is controlled change: find the blast radius of the new requirement rather than throwing away the design.

Step 1: ask which goal. Three regions can mean very different things. Local latency wants data near users. Disaster recovery wants a copy elsewhere, not necessarily active. Residency wants certain users' data to stay in specific regions.

Step 2: identify what changes.

  • User routing: geo-DNS or anycast sends users to the nearest region.
  • Data placement: partition by user home region (each user has one home; simple) versus replicate everything everywhere (reads local, writes complicated).
  • Write conflicts: single writer per key (route writes to the home region), last-write-wins, or CRDTs (data types that merge automatically) for things like counters.
  • Replication lag: cross-region replication takes tens to hundreds of milliseconds, so readers elsewhere may see stale data.

Step 3: state what stays the same: stateless services (deploy a copy per region), per-region caches, the API.

Step 4: name the new trade-off. Lower local latency and better disaster tolerance, at the price of more complexity, some staleness or conflict handling, and higher cost.

Pitfall. Redrawing from scratch, or claiming multi-region is "just add replicas". The hard part is write handling and consistency.

Back-of-envelope estimation

Why do back-of-envelope estimates matter, and how precise should they be? Basic
Say this: "They give the order of magnitude of load and data, which decides the architecture and reveals the bottleneck. I aim for a factor of two, not exact arithmetic."

What it is. A back-of-envelope estimate is a quick calculation, with rounded numbers, of how much traffic, data and bandwidth the system must handle.

Why it exists. Architecture depends on scale in steps, not smoothly:

  • 100 QPS fits one database. 100,000 QPS needs caching, replicas and probably sharding.
  • 1 TB fits one disk. 1 PB does not; you need a distributed store.
  • 50 Mbps of egress is trivial. 50 Gbps means a CDN.

Without an estimate you cannot say which tier you are in, so every choice is a guess. The estimate also reveals which resource is the bottleneck: CPU, memory, disk or network. In many designs one number (say, egress bandwidth) dominates everything else.

How precise. A factor of two is fine. Round aggressively: use 100,000 seconds per day instead of 86,400, and 1 million instead of 1,048,576. Being off by 20 percent never changes the design; being off by 100x does. Writing numbers in powers of ten (1e5) also protects against zero-counting mistakes.

Pitfall. Precision theatre: spending five minutes on 86,400 x 1.07. And its mirror: computing numbers and then never using them. Each number should trigger a design decision.

How do you go from users to QPS? Work an example. Basic
Say this: "Start from daily active users, multiply by actions per user per day, divide by about 100,000 seconds per day for average QPS, then multiply by 2 to 3 for peak."

What QPS is. Queries (requests) per second, the standard measure of load. DAU is daily active users: those who use the product on a given day; it is more useful than registered users because many accounts are dormant.

Steps.

  1. Start from DAU, not registered users.
  2. Multiply by actions per user per day to get requests per day.
  3. Divide by about 100,000 seconds per day (really 86,400) for average QPS.
  4. Multiply by a peak factor (2 to 3 for consumer apps) for peak QPS.
DAU                = 50 million
Reads per user/day = 20       -> 1,000 million reads/day
Average read QPS   = 1e9 / 1e5 = 10,000
Peak read QPS      = 10,000 x 3 = 30,000
Writes per user/day= 1        -> 50M/day -> 500 avg -> 1,500 peak

So this system is read-heavy (20:1), 30,000 peak reads per second is too much for one database but comfortably handled by a cache tier, and 1,500 writes per second fits one primary. Notice how three numbers already shape the design.

A handy anchor: 1 million requests per day is about 12 per second; 1 billion per day is about 12,000 per second. With it you can sanity check without a calculator.

Pitfalls. Using registered users. Counting only "the main action" and forgetting that one page view triggers several backend calls (feed, ads, notifications), so backend QPS is often several times user-visible QPS.

Why size for peak and not average? How big is the peak factor? Basic
Say this: "Traffic is not spread evenly. A system sized for the average falls over at the peak, which is when users care most. Typical peak factor is 2 to 3x for steady products and 10 to 100x for bursty events."

Why peaks exist. People use products at similar times: evenings, lunch breaks, a goal in a final, a flash sale starting at noon. If 60 percent of daily traffic arrives in four hours, the busy-hour rate is far above the 24-hour average.

Worked example. A million requests a day averages 12 per second. If the busiest hour carries 10 percent of the day (100,000 requests in 3,600 seconds), the busy-hour rate is about 28 per second, over 2x average. A ticket drop where 500,000 people click at the same minute is 8,000 per second for that minute, hundreds of times average.

Type of trafficTypical peak-to-average
Steady consumer app2 to 3x
Business hours B2B tool3 to 5x (and near zero at night)
Flash sale, ticket release, live event10 to 100x

What to do with it. Capacity must cover peak plus headroom (see capacity planning). Cost control comes from autoscaling down off-peak, and from queues that smooth bursts, not from under-provisioning the peak. For extreme bursts, add load shedding or a waiting room.

Pitfall. Using one factor for all endpoints. Checkout may spike 50x during a sale while the profile page does not.

How do you estimate storage? Basic
Say this: "Items per day times size per item, times retention, times replication. I separate large blobs from small metadata because they live in different stores."

Why. Storage estimates decide whether one machine is enough, whether you need object storage and partitioning, and roughly what it costs.

Photos/day        = 50M DAU x 0.1 uploads    = 5 million
Size per photo    = 2 MB (after compression)
Per day           = 5M x 2 MB                = 10 TB
Per year          = 10 TB x 365              = 3.65 PB
Replication x3    = about 11 PB/year   (or about 5.5 PB with erasure coding at ~1.5x)

Replication versus erasure coding. Replication keeps 3 full copies (3x storage) for durability. Erasure coding splits data into fragments plus parity pieces so it survives losing some fragments at about 1.4 to 1.5x overhead; it saves a lot of space on cold data but costs CPU and slower reads and repair.

Blobs versus metadata. Large files dominate bytes and go to object storage. Small rows (id, owner, timestamp, URL) dominate query traffic and go to a database: 5M rows per day at 500 bytes is only 2.5 GB per day, trivial next to the 10 TB of blobs.

Do not forget: retention period (do we keep 5 years or forever?), replication factor, indexes (often 20 to 100 percent overhead on top of table data), and multiple versions of each file (thumbnails of several sizes may add 30 to 50 percent).

Pitfall. Estimating only today. If the product doubles each year, five years later the numbers are very different; say the growth assumption.

How do you estimate bandwidth? Basic
Say this: "QPS times bytes per request, ingress and egress separately, because reads are usually much bigger than writes. I watch bits versus bytes."

Why. Bandwidth tells you whether a single load balancer or network link can carry the traffic, and whether you must use a CDN. It is often the number that makes a design obviously need a particular component.

Uploads: 5M photos/day x 2 MB = 10 TB/day -> 10e12 / 1e5 = 100 MB/s average (about 0.8 Gbps)
Views:   1 billion/day x 200 KB thumbnail = 200 TB/day -> 2 GB/s average (about 16 Gbps), peak x3 = about 48 Gbps

Reading the result. Ingress is 0.8 Gbps: fine. Egress is 48 Gbps at peak: serving this from your own origin would be costly and slow, and a single data center link may not carry it. That number is the justification for a CDN, which caches the images in edge locations near users so most bytes never reach your servers.

Unit conversion. Storage and file sizes are in bytes; network speeds are in bits. 1 byte = 8 bits. So 1 GB/s = 8 Gbps, and 1 Gbps is about 125 MB/s. Mixing them gives an error of 8x, which is exactly the sort of mistake interviewers notice.

Pitfall. Ignoring protocol and replication overhead. Replicating each write to 2 other nodes triples internal network traffic even though the client sees only one copy.

How do you size a cache? Intermediate
Say this: "Access is skewed, so a small hot fraction serves most requests. I cache about the hottest 20 percent of daily data, add overhead, and then check throughput as well as memory."

Why a small cache works. Real traffic follows a power-law (Zipf) distribution: a few items (popular posts, trending products) get most of the requests, and a long tail gets few. So caching a fraction of the data can yield a high hit ratio (the share of requests answered from cache). The 20 percent rule is a rule of thumb, not a law; the real figure depends on how skewed your traffic is, and you confirm with the expected hit ratio.

Daily unique items read = 100 million, avg 1 KB each = 100 GB
Cache the hot 20%       = 20 GB
Add overhead (keys, pointers, fragmentation ~1.5x) = 30 GB
Replication/headroom    -> 2 to 3 nodes of 16 to 32 GB RAM

Second limit: operations per second. One Redis node typically handles on the order of 50k to 100k simple operations per second (hardware and command dependent). 30,000 peak QPS fits, but a single hot key lives on one node and can overload it even if the cluster has capacity. So sizing has two limits: memory (how much data) and ops per second (how much traffic). Compute both and take the larger node count.

What the hit ratio buys you. At 30,000 reads per second and a 90 percent hit ratio, only 3,000 reads per second reach the database. That one sentence is usually the justification for the cache.

Pitfall. Forgetting that cache entries need invalidation or a TTL (time to live), and that a cold cache after restart sends a flood to the database.

How do you estimate the number of servers? Intermediate
Say this: "Peak QPS divided by what one server sustains, then headroom for target utilisation and redundancy for losing a zone."

Why not just divide. A server running at 100 percent of its measured capacity has no slack: latency rises steeply near saturation (queueing), and one node failure shifts load to the rest. So you plan for roughly 50 to 60 percent utilisation at peak, and you ensure the survivors can carry the load after a failure.

Peak 30,000 QPS; one app server handles about 1,000 QPS (measure it; depends on work per request)
30,000 / 1,000 = 30 servers
Run at 50 to 60% target utilisation -> about 50 servers
Spread over 3 zones so losing one zone still leaves enough: N+1 or 1.5x -> about 60

Where the per-server number comes from. You can measure it with a load test, or estimate it.

  • I/O-bound (mostly waiting on database or network calls): use Little's Law. If a request spends 50 ms mostly waiting and a server runs 200 worker threads, throughput is about 200 / 0.05 = 4,000 QPS.
  • CPU-bound: cores divided by CPU time per request. 16 cores and 10 ms of CPU per request gives about 1,600 QPS.

Use whichever limit is lower; in practice memory, connections to the database, and downstream limits also matter.

Why 3 zones and 1.5x. With 3 availability zones, if each zone holds one third of the capacity needed, losing one leaves only 67 percent. To survive a zone loss you need each zone to carry up to half the load, hence about 1.5x total.

Pitfall. Stating a per-server QPS without saying it is an assumption you would verify by load testing.

Why does the read/write ratio matter so much? Intermediate
Say this: "Reads scale by copying data (caches, replicas, CDN); writes scale by splitting data (sharding). So the ratio tells me which problem I really have."

Why they scale differently. A read does not change anything, so any copy can answer it: add another replica and capacity grows. A write must be applied consistently, so it needs a single authoritative place (or a coordination protocol) per piece of data. To spread write load you must partition the data so different keys go to different primaries, and that needs a partition key and breaks cross-shard transactions and joins.

WorkloadExampleScaling tools
Read heavy (100:1 or more)Product pages, feeds, URL redirectsCaching, read replicas, CDN, denormalised views
Write heavyMetrics, logs, IoT telemetrySharding, LSM-tree stores, batching, queues as buffers
BalancedChat, collaborative docsA mix, with care for ordering and fan-out

An LSM-tree store (log-structured merge tree, used by Cassandra and RocksDB) turns writes into sequential appends, which makes it fast for write-heavy loads.

Example. A URL shortener's redirect path is read-heavy (a link is created once, clicked thousands of times). One cache in front plus replicas solves it, and sharding may never be needed. A telemetry system with 500,000 events per second is write-heavy, and no cache helps; you need partitioning and batching.

Takeaway. Asking "what is the ratio, and are there bursts?" early tells you which toolbox to open. Also remember replicas add replication lag, so a read-heavy design must say which reads can tolerate staleness.

How do you sanity check an estimate? Intermediate
Say this: "Compare to known scales, check units, cross-check two ways, and ask whether the per-machine load is realistic."

Why. A single slip (a missed factor of 1,000 or 8) can push the design in the wrong direction, and in a fast interview there is no spreadsheet to catch it. A 30-second check protects you.

  • Compare to known scales: a very large global service has on the order of 100k to millions of QPS. If your small app computes 5 million QPS, recheck. Conversely, 5 QPS for a global app is wrong too.
  • Units: bytes versus bits, KB versus MB, per day versus per second. Write the unit next to every number.
  • Cross-check two ways: compute storage from users and from events; they should agree within a factor of 2 to 3. For example, 5M photos a day at 2 MB should match 10 TB a day whichever route you take.
  • Per-machine reality: does the per-node load fit what one node can really do? A database node rarely sustains more than low tens of thousands of simple queries per second; a spinning disk gives only a few hundred random IOPS.
  • Powers of ten: write 1e5 instead of 100,000 to avoid zero-counting errors.
  • Ask what the number implies: 48 Gbps means CDN; 3 GB per day means a laptop.

Say the check aloud: "that seems a bit high; 50 TB a day would be a very large service, let me recheck my image size". Showing the habit is itself a positive signal.

What common mistakes happen in estimation? Intermediate
Say this: "Using registered users instead of DAU, forgetting peak, replication, indexes and retention, mixing bits and bytes, treating all requests as equal, and over-precise arithmetic."
  • Registered users instead of DAU. A product with 500M accounts may have 50M daily users: a 10x error.
  • Forgetting multipliers: peak factor, replication factor (3x), indexes (up to 2x), retention (years of data), and growth over the planning horizon.
  • Mixing bits and bytes: an 8x error.
  • Treating all requests as equal. A cached read costs microseconds of CPU; a search, an upload or a write with several index updates can cost 100x more. If you say "30k QPS" with a mix of reads and writes, cost differs per type. Split the QPS by type.
  • Over-precise arithmetic (86,400 and 1,048,576): burns time and adds false confidence.
  • Doing the maths and not using it. Every number should trigger a decision: "so we need a CDN", "so one node is enough", "so we must shard".
  • Ignoring the distribution. Averages hide hot keys and heavy users (see fan-out).

A useful routine: after each number, say aloud "which means...". If you cannot finish that sentence, the number was unnecessary.

How would you estimate the load of a fan-out (one write causing many deliveries)? Senior
Say this: "Multiply write rate by average fan-out for sizing, then look at the maximum: a user with tens of millions of followers needs a different path. I always ask for the distribution, not only the mean."

What fan-out is. One input event causing many output operations. A post to 200 followers is one write but 200 feed insertions if you push to followers' feeds ("fan-out on write"). The alternative, "fan-out on read", stores the post once and assembles each follower's feed when they open the app.

Average load (for sizing).

1,000 posts/s x 200 followers (average) = 200,000 feed writes/s

That is the steady load your feed store and queue must absorb; it is 200x higher than the visible write rate, so the write path is actually the heavy one.

Tail load (for design). Averages hide skew. If one account has 50 million followers, a single post means 50 million operations. At 100,000 deliveries per second that takes 500 seconds, so followers see the post minutes late and the queue is blocked behind it. This is the celebrity problem.

What you do with the estimate.

  • Sizing from the average: how many feed-store write nodes and queue partitions.
  • Design from the maximum: a hybrid. Push for normal accounts; for accounts above a follower threshold (say 100,000) do not fan out; pull their posts at read time and merge with the precomputed feed.

Ask for: average, p99 and max follower counts, and the active fraction (many followers are inactive, so skip fan-out for users who have not logged in recently).

Pitfall. Presenting only the mean fan-out. That is exactly the number that hides the problem.

What powers of two and unit conversions should you know? Basic
Say this: "Each 10 powers of two is about 1,000x: KB, MB, GB, TB, PB. A day is about 1e5 seconds, a year about 3e7."

Why memorise them. Estimation is conversion between units. With a handful of anchors you can do any calculation mentally without hesitating, which keeps momentum in the interview.

PowerApprox valueName
2^101 thousand1 KB
2^201 million1 MB
2^301 billion1 GB
2^401 trillion1 TB
2^501 quadrillion1 PB

Time and rate anchors. 1 day = 86,400 s (about 1e5). 1 year = about 3e7 s. 1 million per day is about 12 per second. 1 Gbps is about 125 MB/s.

Size anchors. An ASCII character is 1 byte; an int64 is 8 bytes; an IPv4 address is 4 bytes; a UUID is 16 bytes raw (36 as text). A typical tweet-sized text record is a few hundred bytes with metadata; a compressed web photo is a few hundred KB to a few MB.

Identifier space. 2^32 is about 4.3 billion, so a 32-bit auto-increment id runs out (a real production incident for fast-growing tables). A 64-bit id has about 1.8e19 values, effectively unlimited.

Worked use. "100 million users x 1 KB profile = 1e8 x 1e3 = 1e11 bytes = 100 GB." Three steps, no calculator.

How many characters does a short code need to cover 5 billion items? Intermediate
Say this: "Base-62 gives 62 to the power n combinations. 62^6 is about 56.8 billion, so 6 characters cover 5 billion; 7 characters gives huge headroom."

The general rule. The size of an identifier space is alphabet size to the power of length. Pick the smallest length whose result exceeds the number of items you need, plus headroom.

Base-62 uses a-z (26), A-Z (26) and 0-9 (10) = 62 symbols, all URL-safe.

Length62^nEnough for 5 billion?
5about 916 millionNo
6about 56.8 billionYes (11x headroom)

Why headroom matters. If codes are random, collisions grow as the space fills, so you need a space much larger than the item count. If codes are generated by encoding a counter, you can fill the space tightly but must make sure the counter itself is unique across servers.

The same trick elsewhere: hex (16 symbols) at 8 characters gives 16^8 = 4.3 billion (a 32-bit number); base-64 at 11 characters covers about 7e19, roughly a 64-bit number. This technique sizes any id, key or token space, including random API tokens (where you want unguessable, so a very large space).

Numbers every engineer should know

What are the latency numbers every engineer should know? Basic
Say this: "RAM about 100 ns, SSD random read about 100 microseconds, a round trip inside a data center about half a millisecond, a spinning disk seek about 10 ms, and a cross-ocean round trip 100 to 200 ms. They are orders of magnitude, not exact."

Why they matter. Design is mostly about where time goes. If you do not know that a network hop is thousands of times slower than a memory access, you cannot judge whether a design with 20 sequential calls is fine or hopeless. The list was popularised by Jeff Dean; exact values vary by hardware and era, so treat them as orders of magnitude and say "about".

OperationApprox time
L1 cache referenceabout 1 ns
Main memory (RAM) referenceabout 100 ns
Compress 1 KB with a fast codeca few microseconds
Read 1 MB sequentially from RAMtens of microseconds
SSD random readabout 100 microseconds (NVMe can be lower)
Round trip inside one data centerabout 0.5 ms
Read 1 MB sequentially from SSDabout 1 ms
Spinning disk seekabout 10 ms
Round trip across a continentabout 50 to 80 ms
Round trip between continentsabout 100 to 200 ms

The ratios to remember.

  • RAM is roughly 1,000x faster than an SSD for random access (100 ns versus 100 us).
  • A same-data-center network hop (0.5 ms) is about 5,000x slower than a RAM read.
  • A cross-ocean trip is bounded by the speed of light in fibre. It cannot be engineered away, only avoided with caches, CDNs and regional deployments.
  • Sequential access is far faster than random on every medium.

Mini example. A page that makes 40 sequential service calls at 0.5 ms network each spends 20 ms just on hops, before any work. Make them parallel or batch them.

How do latency numbers change your design decisions? Give examples. Intermediate
Say this: "They tell me what to avoid on the request path: remote calls in sequence, cross-region synchronous writes and random disk access. They justify caching, batching, append-only storage and CDNs."

Why. The numbers turn vague instincts ("that sounds slow") into arithmetic you can defend. Each design pattern in the list below exists because of a gap in the latency table.

  • Caching: a 0.5 ms cache hit versus a 5 to 10 ms database query means a cache cuts latency about 10x and removes load. Worth it for hot data.
  • Chatty services: 20 sequential calls at 1 ms each plus processing is already tens of milliseconds; batch the calls or run them in parallel. Beware: parallel calls make the slowest one decide the latency (see tail latency).
  • Geography: a synchronous cross-region write costs at least one 100 ms+ round trip. Either keep that off the user's request path, or accept asynchronous replication and the staleness that comes with it.
  • Disk: sequential writes (logs, LSM trees, Kafka) are far faster than random writes, which is why append-only designs scale well. A spinning disk does about 100 random IOPS but hundreds of MB/s sequentially.
  • Compress before sending over long distances; compression takes microseconds per KB, much cheaper than network time.
  • CDN: serving an image from an edge 10 ms away instead of an origin 150 ms away is the whole business case.

Worked comparison. A request that does 5 database queries at 5 ms each, sequentially, takes 25 ms. Batch them into one query with a join, or fetch from a cache at 0.5 ms each, and the request falls to about 3 to 5 ms. The table is what tells you to look there first.

Why. Latency numbers say how long one operation takes; throughput numbers say how many one machine can do, which is what you need to count machines. Knowing the rough tier lets you answer "is one node enough?" in seconds.

Core vocabulary and definitions

What is scalability? How is it different from performance? Basic
Say this: "Scalability is handling growing load by adding resources without a redesign. Performance is how fast the system is at a given load. They are different: a system can be fast but not scalable, or scalable but slow per request."

Definitions. Scalability is the ability to handle growth in users, data or requests by adding resources, while keeping latency and availability acceptable. Performance is speed at one load level: how long a request takes now.

Two examples that separate them.

  • Fast but not scalable: one beefy server with all data in memory answers in 2 ms at 100 users, and falls over at 100,000 because there is nowhere to add capacity. High performance, low scalability.
  • Scalable but slow per request: a distributed system with many hops answers in 150 ms, but you can double traffic by doubling machines. Lower performance, high scalability.

What good scalability looks like. Cost grows about linearly with load (twice the load needs about twice the machines, not ten times), and latency stays roughly flat. Things that block scaling are shared bottlenecks: one database primary, a global lock, a single coordinator, or state kept in server memory.

Why it matters in interviews. When someone says "make it scalable", ask "scale which dimension: users, data volume, request rate, or number of tenants?" Each has a different answer.

Vertical versus horizontal scaling: what is the difference and when do you choose each? Basic
Say this: "Vertical means a bigger machine; horizontal means more machines. I scale up first while it is cheap and simple, and scale out the tiers that must, starting with the stateless app tier."

What they are. Vertical scaling (scale up) upgrades one machine: more CPU, RAM, faster disk. Horizontal scaling (scale out) adds more machines and spreads work across them.

Vertical (scale up)Horizontal (scale out)
HowBigger machine: more CPU, RAM, diskMore machines behind a load balancer or partitioned

CeilingHard limit of largest machine; price rises steeplyPractically very high
FailureOne machine is a single point of failureSurvives node loss if designed for it
DowntimeOften needs a restart to resizeAdd nodes live

Why horizontal is harder. Spreading work needs the work to be separable. Stateless servers are easy: any server can take any request, so add another behind the load balancer. Data is hard: you must either copy it (replication, which needs a story for keeping copies in sync) or split it (sharding, which needs a partition key and breaks cross-shard joins and transactions).

Decision rule. A single database on a large instance serves a surprising amount of load (many products never outgrow it), so scale up the data tier first, add replicas for reads and failover, and shard only when one primary's writes or storage run out. Scale out the stateless tier from day one because it is cheap. When a system needs fault tolerance anyway, you need at least two nodes, which is already "out".

Pitfall. Saying "just scale horizontally" without saying what you do about state.

What is the difference between latency and throughput? Basic
Say this: "Latency is how long one operation takes; throughput is how many complete per unit time. They are related but independent: a wide highway has high throughput but each car still takes an hour."

Definitions. Latency: time for one operation, for example a request takes 80 ms. Throughput: operations completed per second, for example 5,000 requests per second.

How they relate and differ.

  • Parallelism raises throughput without changing latency: ten servers handle ten times the requests, each still takes 80 ms.
  • Batching and pipelining raise throughput but often raise latency: writing 1,000 rows in one batch is much more efficient per row, but each row waits for the batch to fill.
  • A faster single operation lowers latency and also raises per-thread throughput.

Little's Law ties them together: in-flight requests = throughput x latency. At 5,000 req/s and 80 ms each, 400 requests are in flight at any moment.

What happens near capacity. As load nears the maximum throughput, requests queue, and latency rises sharply (the graph looks like a hockey stick). So a throughput target means little without a latency target: "we handle 10,000 QPS at p99 below 200 ms" is a real requirement; "we handle 10,000 QPS" is incomplete, since a system that answers in 30 seconds technically meets it.

Interview use. When asked to "make it faster", ask which: reduce per-request latency (cache, fewer hops, faster storage) or increase throughput (more instances, batching). They need different fixes.

What is Little's Law and how do you use it? Intermediate
Say this: "In a stable system, average number in the system equals arrival rate times average time in the system. I use it to size thread pools, consumers and connection pools."

The law. L = lambda x W. L is the average number of items in the system (requests being handled, messages in flight), lambda is the arrival rate (per second), W is the average time each item spends in the system (seconds). It holds for any stable system regardless of the arrival pattern, which is why it is so useful.

Intuition. A coffee shop gets 2 customers a minute and each stays 5 minutes: on average 10 people are inside. Seats must cover about 10 (plus a margin), whatever the order in which people arrive.

Uses.

  • Thread or connection pool size: 2,000 requests/s at 100 ms each needs about 2,000 x 0.1 = 200 concurrent requests in flight. A pool of 50 threads will make requests wait in a queue and latency will balloon.
  • Queue consumers: if each message takes 20 ms and 1,000 messages/s arrive, you need 1,000 x 0.02 = 20 concurrent consumers just to keep up.
  • Database connections: 500 queries/s at 10 ms each means about 5 connections in use on average; a pool of 20 is plenty, and bigger pools just add contention.
  • Detecting trouble: if latency doubles at the same arrival rate, in-flight work doubles, which can exhaust threads or memory. That is how a slow dependency causes a cascading outage.

Caveat. It uses averages and a stable (non-overloaded) system. For the tail, you need percentiles and headroom.

What are "the nines" of availability? How much downtime does each allow? Basic
Say this: "Availability is uptime over total time as a percentage. 99.9 percent allows about 8.8 hours a year, 99.99 about 53 minutes, 99.999 about 5 minutes. Each extra nine is 10x less downtime and far more cost."

Definition. Availability = uptime / (uptime + downtime). The "nines" are shorthand for how many 9s the percentage starts with.

AvailabilityDowntime per yearPer month (about)

How to derive on the spot. A year has about 525,600 minutes. 0.1 percent of that is 526 minutes, about 8.8 hours (three nines). Each extra nine divides by ten.

Why each nine costs so much. Three nines can be reached with a redundant pair and a good on-call. Four nines needs redundancy in every layer, multiple zones, automated failover (a human who needs 10 minutes to wake and log in has already used a month's budget), safe deploys and quick rollback. Five nines needs multi-region active-active design and extreme operational discipline. A five-minute deploy outage alone blows the four-nines budget.

Decision rule. Ask whether the product needs it and measure from the user's view (successful requests), not "servers up". Internal tools are fine at 99.9 percent; payments or login may need 99.99. Also note planned maintenance counts unless the SLA excludes it.

How do you compute the availability of a system made of several components? Intermediate
Say this: "In series, availabilities multiply, so chains get worse. In parallel with independent failures, it is 1 minus (1 minus a) to the n, so redundancy improves it. The parallel formula only holds if failures are independent."

In series (every component must work): multiply. Three components at 99.9 percent each give 0.999 x 0.999 x 0.999 = about 99.7 percent, which is about 26 hours of downtime a year instead of 8.8. A request that calls 10 services at 99.9 percent each has about 99.0 percent availability. This is why long synchronous call chains hurt, and why you cut dependencies from the critical path or make them optional (degrade gracefully when a non-essential service is down).

In parallel (redundant, any one suffices): 1 - (1 - a)^n. Two independent replicas at 99 percent give 1 - 0.01 x 0.01 = 99.99 percent. Three give 99.9999 percent.

The catch: independence. The parallel formula assumes failures do not happen together. Shared causes break it: both replicas in the same zone, the same bad deploy rolled out to both, the same dependency (one database behind both), the same configuration push, the same expired certificate. Then redundancy buys little. Real redundancy comes from independence: separate zones and failure domains, staggered rollouts, and diverse dependencies.

Mixed example. A load balancer (99.99) in front of two app servers (99 each) in parallel in front of a database (99.95): 0.9999 x 0.9999 x 0.9995 = about 99.89 percent. The database, not the app servers, is now the weakest link, so replicate it next.

Availability versus reliability versus durability: what is the difference? Intermediate
Say this: "Availability is whether it answers now, reliability is whether it answers correctly and consistently over time, and durability is whether acknowledged data is still there later."

Why distinguish them. People use the words interchangeably, but they fail in different ways and need different fixes. Mixing them up in an interview signals shaky fundamentals.

  • Availability: is the system up and responding now? Measured as the percentage of time (or of requests) that succeed. Fix with redundancy and failover.
  • Reliability: does it behave correctly, consistently, over time with a low failure rate? A system can be available but wrong (returns stale or corrupted data, or a buggy calculation). Fix with testing, validation, safe deploys.
  • Durability: once data is acknowledged as saved, will it still be there after crashes, disk failures and years? Fix with replication, checksums, backups.
SituationAvailable?Reliable?Durable?

Numbers to know. Object stores advertise eleven nines of durability (annual probability of losing a given object around 1e-11), yet their availability is lower, around 99.9 to 99.99 percent. You can be temporarily unable to read data that is certainly not lost.

Design consequence. Choose per data class: payments need extreme durability even at the cost of availability; a view counter can lose a few updates but must stay available.

What are MTBF and MTTR, and how do they relate to availability? Intermediate
Say this: "MTBF is mean time between failures, MTTR is mean time to recover. Availability is MTBF over MTBF plus MTTR, so I can improve it by failing less often or recovering faster, and recovering faster is usually the better lever."

Definitions. MTBF: average time the system runs between failures. MTTR: average time from failure to recovery (detect, diagnose, fix or fail over, verify).

Formula and example. Availability = MTBF / (MTBF + MTTR). If a service fails once every 30 days (720 hours) and takes 1 hour to recover: 720 / 721 = 99.86 percent. Cut recovery to 5 minutes (0.083 hours): 720 / 720.083 = 99.99 percent. Failing twice as often with 5-minute recovery would still be about 99.98 percent, better than the original.

Why MTTR is the practical lever. At scale, components fail constantly (disks, nodes, bad deploys), and you cannot make failure rare enough through quality alone. But you can make recovery fast: good alerting (low time to detect), automated failover, one-click rollback, feature flags to switch off a bad change, and runbooks. This is why modern operations track time to detect and time to mitigate.

Caveat. MTTR averages hide a long tail: one 6-hour incident can matter more than ten 5-minute ones. Look at the distribution, and note that MTBF as a statistic is meaningful only with enough failures.

Explain SLI, SLO and SLA with an example. Basic
Say this: "SLI is the measurement, SLO is the internal target for it, SLA is the contract with consequences. The gap between 100 percent and the SLO is the error budget."

Why they exist. "The service should be reliable" is not testable. These three terms turn it into a measurement, a goal and a promise, and they let engineering and the business argue with numbers.

TermMeaningExample
SLI (indicator)A measured number describing service behaviourFraction of requests under 300 ms and not an error
SLO (objective)Internal target for an SLI over a window99.9% of requests succeed in under 300 ms over 30 days
SLA (agreement)Contract with customers with consequences if missedBelow 99.5% monthly, customer gets service credits

Why SLO is tighter than SLA. The SLO is the early warning: if you target 99.9 percent and the contract says 99.5 percent, you notice and fix problems before you pay penalties.

Error budget. The gap between 100 percent and the SLO. A 99.9 percent SLO over 30 days leaves 0.1 percent, about 43 minutes of unavailability (or 0.1 percent of requests failing). You spend that budget on failures, risky deploys and experiments. When the budget is nearly gone, the team slows feature releases and prioritises reliability work; when plenty remains, it can ship faster. This turns an argument between "ship faster" and "be safer" into a measurable policy.

Choosing SLIs. Pick what users feel: availability (success rate), latency (percentile), freshness, correctness. Measure at the user-facing edge, not on one server.

Pitfall. Setting an SLO of 100 percent (impossible and it blocks all change), or SLOs on averages instead of percentiles.

Why use p50, p95 and p99 latency instead of the average? Basic
Say this: "Latency has a long tail, and the average hides it. p99 is the value 99 percent of requests are faster than. I set SLOs on percentiles and never average percentiles across servers."

What a percentile is. Sort all request times from fastest to slowest. The p50 (median) is the middle value: typical user. The p95 is the value that 95 percent of requests beat. The p99 is the value that 99 percent beat; 1 in 100 requests is slower.

Why not the average. Imagine 99 requests at 50 ms and 1 request at 5,000 ms. The average is about 100 ms, which sounds fine, but one user in 100 waits 5 seconds. The average mixes the two groups into a number that describes nobody.

Why the tail matters more than 1 percent suggests.

  • Heavy users make many requests, so they hit the slow ones more often; a page that makes 20 requests will hit at least one p99 slow request about 18 percent of the time (1 - 0.99^20).
  • Slow requests often come from the most valuable cases: big customers with big data.

How to use them. Report p50 (typical), p95, p99 and sometimes p99.9, and set SLOs on percentiles: "p99 under 300 ms". Slice by endpoint and region.

Pitfall. Never average percentiles across servers or minutes: the average of two p99 values is not the p99 of the combined traffic. Merge the underlying histograms and compute from those.

What is tail latency amplification and why does it matter in microservices? Senior
Say this: "If a request fans out to many backends and waits for all, the chance one is slow grows fast. With 100 backends each slow 1 percent of the time, about 63 percent of requests hit a slow one. So the user's median starts to look like each service's p99."

What it is. Tail latency amplification is the effect that the slowest of many parallel calls decides the overall latency. Each individual service looks healthy at its own p50, but the combined request is dominated by the worst.

The math. If each backend is slower than its own p99 with probability 1 percent, the probability that at least one of N is slow is 1 - 0.99^N.

Fan-out NP(at least one slow call)
11%
10about 10%
100about 63%

This is why search and aggregation services (which touch many shards) and deep microservice chains see worse latency than any single component.

Mitigations.

  • Reduce fan-out: fewer shards per query, denormalise, push work to precomputation.
  • Tight timeouts and returning partial results rather than waiting for stragglers.
  • Hedged requests: if a reply has not arrived by roughly the p95 time, send the same request to another replica and take whichever answers first. This cuts the tail at the cost of a few percent extra load; it is safe only for idempotent requests.
  • Caching and load shedding to keep servers out of queueing.
  • Remove sources of pauses: garbage-collection pauses, noisy neighbours on shared hosts, background compaction.

Interview use. Mention it when you propose a scatter-gather design or a request that calls many services: "p99 matters more than p50 here because of fan-out".

What is capacity planning and how do you do it? Intermediate
Say this: "Measure current peak and per-node capacity, forecast growth, add headroom so a node failure or spike does not tip me over, account for lead time, and re-check regularly. Autoscaling and load shedding cover what I cannot predict."

Why it exists. Run out of capacity and you get an outage right when growth is happening. Over-provision and you waste money. Capacity planning is the discipline of staying between the two.

  1. Measure. Current peak usage, and the capacity of one unit (requests per second per node) from a load test that goes to the point where latency degrades, not just to the point of failure.
  2. Forecast growth. Organic trend plus planned launches and seasonal events (holidays, end of quarter).
  3. Add headroom. Run at about 50 to 70 percent of capacity at peak, and keep N+1 or N+2 redundancy so a node or zone failure is survivable.
  4. Account for lead time. Cloud quotas, hardware delivery, database migrations and reshards take days or weeks.
  5. Re-check regularly, and use autoscaling plus load shedding (rejecting excess requests quickly instead of falling over) for what you cannot predict.

Why headroom. Queueing theory says latency rises steeply as utilisation approaches 100 percent. A rough picture: a server at 50 percent utilisation has short queues; at 90 percent the average wait is many times longer and variable; at 99 percent it is unbounded in practice. So 90 percent at peak is already risky even though it looks efficient.

Example. Peak 30,000 QPS, 1,000 per node measured, target 60 percent: 30,000 / 600 = 50 nodes; add one zone's worth of redundancy; forecast 2x growth in a year and plan the next step (more nodes, or the database becomes the limit first).

Define strong consistency, eventual consistency and CAP in one minute. Intermediate
Say this: "Strong consistency means after a write completes, every reader sees it. Eventual means replicas converge if writes stop, but readers may briefly see old data. CAP says during a partition you must choose consistency or availability. I choose per data type."

Strong consistency (linearizability). The system behaves as if there is one copy: after a write returns, any later read, anywhere, sees it. Costs: coordination between replicas, so higher write latency and reduced availability during partitions.

Eventual consistency. Replicas may differ for a short time; if no new writes arrive they all converge. Benefits: lower latency, higher availability. Cost: a user may read stale data, and concurrent writes need a conflict rule.

Example. You post a comment. With strong consistency, your friend's very next page load shows it. With eventual consistency, the friend's request may hit a replica that has not yet received it and shows the old page; a second later it appears.

CAP. In a distributed store, when a network partition separates nodes (it will happen), you can keep either C (reject or block some requests so no one sees inconsistent data) or A (answer every request, perhaps with stale data). Without a partition you can have both, but there is still a latency versus consistency trade-off, described by the PACELC view: if Partitioned choose A or C; Else choose Latency or Consistency.

Decision rule. Strong for money and inventory; eventual for feeds, counters and search indexes. Name the intermediate guarantees where useful (read-your-writes, monotonic reads).

Pitfall. Saying "NoSQL is eventually consistent, SQL is strongly consistent". Many stores let you choose per operation.

What does stateless mean and why is it important for scaling? Basic
Say this: "A stateless server keeps no per-user data between requests. State lives in a shared store or in a token, so any server can handle any request and I can add, remove or lose servers freely."

What it means. If a server stores a user's shopping cart in its own memory, then the user's next request must go to the same server (sticky sessions), and if that server crashes the cart is gone. A stateless design instead keeps the cart in a shared store (a database or Redis) or encodes identity in a token (such as a signed JWT) that the client sends each time.

Why it matters.

  • Easy horizontal scaling: add servers behind the load balancer; no data to move.
  • No stickiness needed: the load balancer can send any request to any server and balance evenly.
  • Failure is cheap: a crashed server loses nothing; the next request goes elsewhere.
  • Easy deploys and autoscaling: replace instances at will.

The catch. State does not vanish; you push it to a tier built to hold it (databases, caches, object stores). That tier becomes the hard part to scale and make highly available. "Stateless" is therefore a way to concentrate the hard problem in a few specialised places, not to remove it.

Practical notes. Local in-process caches are fine as an optimisation if the data can be rebuilt. For WebSocket connections you necessarily keep connection state on a server, so you add a routing layer to know which server holds which user.

What is a single point of failure and how do you remove one? Basic
Say this: "A component whose failure takes down the whole system. I remove it with redundancy and automated failover, then look at the next layer, because there is always another."

What it is. A single point of failure (SPOF) is a part with no backup that the whole system depends on. A load balancer with one instance, one database primary with no standby, one DNS provider, a single message broker.

How to remove it.

  • Stateless services: several instances behind a load balancer, across zones.
  • Databases: a replica with automated failover, or a clustered, quorum-based store.
  • Queues: a clustered broker with replicated partitions.
  • Network edge: multiple load balancers (cloud load balancers are managed redundant pairs), multiple zones.

Then check the next layer. Removing the app SPOF exposes the database; removing that exposes the load balancer, DNS, the configuration service, the shared cache, the single certificate that can expire, the single deployment pipeline, and even the one person who knows a procedure.

Redundancy must be real. Failover that has never been rehearsed often fails when needed (stale credentials, a standby too small, replication lag, no one knows the steps). Test it with game days and chaos experiments. Also note that redundant nodes sharing a cause (same zone, same deploy) are one failure domain, not two.

Cost note. Not every SPOF is worth removing; weigh the cost against the availability target (see the nines).

Picking between options and justifying the choice

How do you decide between two technologies or designs in an interview? Intermediate
Say this: "I list the requirements that matter for this decision, compare two or three realistic options, choose and name the deciding factor, state the cost I accept and what would make me switch."

Why a method. "I like X" is not an argument. A method keeps the comparison honest and makes it easy for the interviewer to follow and challenge.

  1. List the requirements that matter for this decision (consistency, query shape, write rate, latency, team skills). Not all of them; the ones that discriminate.
  2. Name two or three realistic options. No strawmen: if one option is obviously absurd, it is not an option.
  3. Compare on those criteria in a few words each, ideally with numbers.
  4. Choose and state the deciding factor. One reason that tipped it.
  5. State the cost you accept and what would make you switch.
Example: "The access pattern is key-value lookups at 50k writes per second with no joins, so I choose a partitioned key-value store over a relational database. I lose ad-hoc queries and multi-row transactions; if the product later needs reporting I would stream the data into a warehouse."

Decision rule. If requirements do not discriminate between options, pick the simpler or more familiar one and say so. Mention operational factors (team experience, managed offering) as legitimate tie-breakers.

Pitfall. Comparing on features the problem does not need, or declaring a winner without a deciding factor.

Why not a religion. Both camps have loud advocates, and a dogmatic answer is a red flag. A relational database on one large node plus replicas serves most products for a long time; NoSQL systems exist to solve specific problems.

How do you use numbers to justify a decision? Intermediate
Say this: "I turn a choice into a threshold: this load fits one node, so no sharding yet; at 50x I would shard by user id."

Why. A design with no thresholds is opinion. A design with thresholds shows you understand the mechanism, and tells the interviewer when you would revisit. It also lets you resist over-engineering honestly.

Pattern: number, capacity, conclusion, trigger.

  • "Peak writes are about 3,000 per second. One relational primary handles that comfortably, so no sharding yet. At 50x I would shard by user id."
  • "A 90 percent cache hit ratio cuts database reads from 30,000 to 3,000 per second, which one primary plus a replica carries."
  • "Egress is 48 Gbps at peak, more than we want to serve from origin, so a CDN is required, not optional."
  • "Metadata is 2.5 GB a day, so after 5 years it is under 5 TB; one database node with replicas holds it. The images are 10 TB a day, so those go to object storage."

Notice each sentence has a number from the estimation phase, a capacity you know, and a decision. That is how the estimation work pays off.

Pitfall. Quoting a node capacity without caveat. Say "about" and "I would load test".

How do you choose between a managed service and building or self-hosting? Senior
Say this: "I compare total cost of ownership, not licence price. Managed wins for undifferentiated parts where we lack deep expertise; self-hosting or building wins when the component is our advantage, the scale makes managed prohibitive, or requirements cannot be met otherwise. I plan an exit path."

Why it is a real decision. Teams are small and time is the scarcest resource. Running your own Kafka or database cluster means patching, upgrades, capacity, backups, on-call at 3 a.m. A managed service converts that into a bill.

What goes into total cost of ownership.

  • Engineers to operate it and their on-call burden.
  • Upgrade and security patching effort.
  • Scaling and failure-handling effort.
  • Time to market (a managed queue is live in an hour).
  • Infrastructure cost, including idle capacity.

What to do. For one-way doors: do the analysis, write down assumptions and the access patterns they rest on, review with others, and design for evolution (versioned APIs and schemas). For two-way doors: pick a sensible default, ship, measure, change if needed.

In the interview, flag it: "the shard key is the decision I would want to get right early; the cache technology I can change later."

Scenario: the interviewer will not give you scale numbers ("whatever you think"). What do you do? Intermediate
Say this: "I will design for 10 million DAU growing to 100 million, with about 10 reads to 1 write. Is that in the right range?"

Why they do this. Often they are testing whether you can commit under uncertainty, the way a real product manager who "does not know yet" forces you to. Refusing to commit, or asking again and again, looks like inability to make progress.

What to do.

  1. Make a clear, reasonable assumption and anchor it with a comparison (a well-known product of a similar size).
  2. Invite correction with one sentence, then proceed.
  3. Derive the load numbers (QPS, storage) from it and use them.
  4. Check that the design works at the stated size and say how it would change at 10x.

Choosing a plausible figure and sticking to it beats refusing. If the interviewer later says "make it ten times bigger", you have a baseline to compare against and can say what breaks first.

Scenario: two designs both seem valid and you cannot decide in the interview. How do you proceed? Intermediate
Say this: "Both work. I will pick the simpler one, because it is cheaper to change later, and I will name the condition under which the other wins."

Why decide at all. In real work, two options being close in value means the cost of picking wrong is small, so speed matters more than perfection. Interviewers prefer a justified decision over an endless comparison, which burns time and signals indecision.

Tie-breakers, in order.

  1. Simpler (fewer moving parts, fewer failure modes).
  2. Cheaper to change later (more reversible).
  3. Fits the team's skills and existing platform.
  4. Lower cost at the stated scale.

Say the other as the alternative with its trigger: "If writes grow past one primary's capacity I would move to the partitioned option; until then, the relational option is simpler and gives me transactions."

This shows judgement: you recognise the options are close, you decide with a rule, and you know when to revisit.

Scenario: in production p50 latency is fine but users complain and p99 is 5 seconds. How do you investigate? Senior
Say this: "I find what the slow requests share using histograms by endpoint, region and tenant, trace a few of them to see which hop dominates, check the usual tail causes, mitigate, and add a p99 alert."

Why it is a tail problem. A fine median with a terrible p99 means most requests are normal and a minority are very slow. That points to something conditional: a specific tenant, endpoint, node, data size, or event (like a garbage-collection pause), not general slowness.

  1. Characterise. Latency histograms sliced by endpoint, region, tenant, instance and hour. Does the slow tail share a trait? Is it constant or periodic (every minute, at the top of the hour)?
  2. Trace. Use distributed traces of slow requests to see which hop or dependency dominates (a database query, a downstream service, a queue wait).
  3. Check usual tail causes.
    • Garbage-collection pauses (long JVM stop-the-world pauses).
    • Cold cache or a cache stampede (many requests miss together and hit the database).
    • A slow shard, hot partition or noisy neighbour.
    • Lock contention.
    • Retries and timeouts stacking (a 1 s timeout with 3 retries is 3 s plus).
    • Connection pool exhaustion (requests wait for a connection).
    • A few huge payloads or unpaginated queries.
    • Unindexed queries that only hurt for large tenants.
  4. Mitigate. Tighter timeouts, hedged requests for idempotent reads, bulkheads (isolate resources per tenant or dependency), pagination limits, caching or precomputing the heavy path.
  5. Prevent. Add an SLO alert on p99 per endpoint so you notice before users do.

Pitfall. Looking at averages or the global dashboard; the answer is always in the slice.

Scenario: marketing announces a launch that may bring 10x traffic in two weeks. What do you do? Senior
Say this: "Get numbers from marketing, load test at expected peak plus margin to find the first bottleneck, pre-scale, add load shedding and a degraded mode, prepare a runbook and a kill switch, and watch live with named owners."

Why it needs a plan. A 10x surge is exactly the situation where an untested system fails, in public, on the day the business cares most. Two weeks is enough to find and fix the first one or two bottlenecks if you start now.

  1. Get the facts. Expected peak (how many, when, how fast does it ramp?), duration, which endpoints (landing page, signup, checkout), and what a failure costs.
  2. Load test at expected peak plus margin (say 1.5x). Find the first bottleneck. Common ones: the database (CPU, connections), connection pools, a third-party API with a rate limit, a single-threaded component, cache hit ratio dropping under new access patterns.
  3. Fix and repeat. The first bottleneck is rarely the last.
  4. Pre-scale and pre-warm: raise autoscaling minimums (autoscaling lags a sharp spike), warm caches, request cloud quota increases, increase database capacity.
  5. Protect the core: load shedding, rate limits, a queue or waiting room for sign-ups, and a degraded mode (turn off expensive non-essential features such as recommendations).
  6. Operational readiness: feature-flag kill switches, a runbook, rollback plan, a freeze on unrelated deploys, a named on-call owner for each component, a live dashboard, and a communication channel.
  7. After the event: compare actual versus forecast, review what broke, update the capacity plan.

Pitfall. Only scaling up the web tier; the database and third-party dependencies are the usual first casualties.
