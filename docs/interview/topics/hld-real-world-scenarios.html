HLD: Real-World Scenarios and Incidents interview questions

Situational "what would you do" questions about running production systems (latency spikes, bad deploys, hot keys, retry storms, split brain, bad migrations, leaked credentials and more), plus the well-known industry outages (S3 2017, GitHub 2018, Cloudflare 2019, Facebook 2021, Knight Capital, GitLab, CrowdStrike and others) and the design lessons each one teaches.

How to use this page

Senior interviews often drop the whiteboard and ask "it is 2 a.m., X is happening, what do you do?". The interviewer wants to see a calm, ordered method: stop the bleeding first, find the cause second, fix it properly third, and make sure it cannot happen again fourth. Each scenario below is answered that way: a short "Say" line you can speak in 20 seconds, then the reasoning underneath so you understand why that is the right move.

The second half covers real outages that interviewers like to reference. For those, each answer walks through the timeline step by step: what happened, why it happened, and what it teaches. Incident details come from the public post-mortems as best remembered and are hedged where write-ups differ. Do not quote exact numbers from memory in an interview; quote the lesson and the mechanism.

The general method

Production is on fire. What is your general order of operations? Senior
Say: "Mitigate first, diagnose second. I use the four golden signals (latency, traffic, errors, saturation) to find which tier is unhappy, then ask what changed."

Why order matters. Under stress, engineers tend to dive into the interesting question ("why is it broken?") while customers keep suffering. The method exists to force the boring-but-valuable question first: "how do we stop the pain?" Understanding the root cause can take hours; a rollback takes minutes. You can always investigate afterwards, using the logs and dumps you preserved.

  1. Confirm and scope. Is it real (monitoring itself can be wrong)? Who is affected: all users, one region, one tenant, one endpoint? Since when? A precise start time is the single most useful fact, because you can line it up against deploys and config changes.
  2. Ask "what changed?" Deploys, config pushes, feature flags, traffic shape, cron jobs, certificate or quota expiry, upstream vendor changes. Most incidents correlate with a change in the previous hour or so. A shared change log or deploy timeline makes this a 30-second lookup.
  3. Mitigate before you understand. Roll back, flip the flag off, shed load, fail over, scale out. Restoring service beats finding the cause. Take a heap dump, thread dump or log snapshot first if it is cheap, so you can investigate later.
  4. Communicate. One incident commander who coordinates (not the person debugging), a single status channel, regular updates even when the update is "no change", and a customer-facing status page. Without this, ten people try ten fixes at once and make it worse.
  5. Find the root cause once stable, from the signals you preserved.
  6. Fix and prevent. A blameless postmortem, action items with owners and dates, and a test or guardrail so this class of failure is caught earlier next time.

Small example. Error rate jumps from 0.1 percent to 8 percent at 14:02. You check the deploy log: a release finished at 14:00. Rolling back takes four minutes and the errors disappear. Only then do you diff the release, find the new code calling a method that times out, and write the fix. Total customer pain: about six minutes instead of the hour it would have taken to debug live.

Pitfalls. Rolling back is not always safe (a data migration may have run, see the rollback question below). Also beware of "hero debugging": the instinct to keep digging when a known-safe mitigation is available.

What are the golden signals and the USE and RED methods, and how do they help in an incident? Basic
Say: "Golden signals are latency, traffic, errors and saturation. RED is for services, USE is for resources. I use RED to find the unhappy service, then USE to find the saturated resource inside it."

What they are. These are three checklists of metrics so that in an incident you do not stare at a hundred dashboards wondering what matters.

MethodApplies toMetricsQuestion it answers
Four golden signals (Google SRE book)Any user-facing systemLatency, traffic, errors, saturationIs the system healthy from the user's point of view?
REDRequest-driven servicesRate, Errors, DurationWhich service or hop is slow or failing?
USEResources (CPU, disk, memory, pool, network)Utilization, Saturation, ErrorsWhich resource is the bottleneck?

Why they exist. Metrics are plentiful, attention is not. A fixed checklist makes sure you check every service and every resource the same way, instead of fixating on the one graph you happen to know.

How to use them, step by step.

  1. Open RED for each service on the request path. Find the first one (nearest the user) whose duration or error rate moved. Because failures climb up a call chain, the culprit is usually the deepest unhappy service, not the front door.
  2. On that service apply USE to its resources: CPU utilization, thread pool queue (saturation), connection pool wait, disk IO, memory and GC, network drops. "Saturation" means work is waiting in a queue: it is the early warning that utilization alone misses.
  3. Check the error counters on each resource (disk errors, retransmits, rejected tasks).

Percentiles, not averages. If 1 percent of requests take 10 seconds and the rest take 100 ms, the average is about 200 ms and looks fine, yet one user in a hundred is miserable (and a page that makes 50 calls will hit the slow tail on almost every load). Always look at p95 and p99.

Pitfall. Averaging percentiles across hosts is mathematically wrong; aggregate histograms instead.

What makes a good blameless postmortem, and why does the "blameless" part matter? Intermediate
Say: "A timeline, impact, root cause plus contributing factors, what went well and badly, and owned action items. Blameless means I ask how the system allowed the mistake, not who made it."

What it is. A postmortem is a written review done after an incident so that the organization learns from it. Its job is to change the system, not to assign guilt.

Contents of a good one.

  • Summary and impact: who was affected, for how long, and what it cost (users, money, SLO budget).
  • Timeline: when it started, when it was detected, escalated, mitigated, resolved. The gaps between those times are themselves findings (a 20-minute gap between start and detection means the alerting is weak).
  • Root cause and contributing factors: nearly always several. A typo is a trigger; the missing limit, the missing canary and the untested recovery are the real causes.
  • What went well / what went badly / where we got lucky. "Where we got lucky" is the most honest section.
  • Action items with an owner and a date, each prevent, detect or mitigate a specific gap. Items without owners never happen.

Why blameless. If engineers fear punishment they hide facts ("I ran a command, but I will not say which"), and you lose the true cause. People also stop reporting near misses. Real example: in the S3 2017 and GitLab 2017 incidents (below) a human typed a bad command, yet the published actions were about tooling guardrails, not discipline. The question "how did the system let a tired human delete the primary?" has a fixable answer; "why was he careless?" does not.

Pitfall. Blameless does not mean accountability-free: the organization still owns finishing the action items. A common failure is a postmortem whose actions are "be more careful".

How do you make sure you hear about an incident before customers do? Intermediate
Say: "Alert on symptoms users feel, using SLO burn rates, plus outside-in synthetic probes that run real journeys. Cause-based alerts like CPU are secondary."

The problem. Many teams alert on causes (CPU above 80 percent, disk above 90 percent). Those fire constantly without any user impact (alert fatigue) and, worse, stay silent when something you did not think of breaks. Customers are the best detector, and you want to beat them.

How to do it.

  1. Define an SLO for each key user journey, for example 99.9 percent of checkouts succeed within 2 seconds over 30 days. That gives an error budget: 0.1 percent of requests may fail.
  2. Alert on burn rate, meaning how fast you are spending the budget. Multi-window rules are the standard: page if a short window (say 5 minutes) AND a longer window (1 hour) both burn at, for example, 14x the sustainable rate (a fast burn that would exhaust the budget in about two days). Open a ticket for a slow burn (for example 6x over six hours). The two windows together cut false alarms while still catching real problems quickly. The exact multipliers are tuning choices, not laws.
  3. Synthetic probes: scripts that perform a real login, search or checkout every minute from several places. They catch outages in traffic you do not currently have (a region with no users at 3 a.m.).
  4. Black-box from outside your network. A dashboard that lives in the failed region, or behind the failed DNS, goes dark with the system it watches. Facebook 2021 (below) is the cautionary example: internal tools broke with the network.

Pitfall. Alerts without a runbook or clear owner just wake people up. Every page should be actionable, otherwise delete or downgrade it.

Scenarios: latency, load and capacity

Latency suddenly spikes on a service. How do you investigate? Senior
Say: "Scope it, ask what changed, trace a slow request to find which span grew, then check saturation on that component. Mitigate by rolling back or shedding load while I dig."

Mental model. A request's latency is the sum of time spent in each step: queueing for a thread, running your code, waiting on a database or downstream call, waiting for a lock or GC pause. A spike means one of those parts grew. Your job is to find which one, fast.

Step 1: scope. Is it every request or only the tail (p99)? All endpoints or one? One region, host or tenant? A spike on one host points to that host (bad node, noisy neighbour); on one endpoint points to its code or query; everywhere points to a shared dependency or a global change.

Step 2: what changed? Deploy, config, flag, traffic mix, dependency version, a cron job starting. Line up the start time of the spike against the change log.

Step 3: trace a slow request. With distributed tracing, open a p99 trace and see which span is long. Example: the request takes 2.4 s, and 2.1 s of it is a single call to the inventory service. Now you know where to look, and you have skipped debugging everything else.

Step 4: check saturation on that component: CPU, GC pause time, thread pool queue depth, connection pool wait, disk IO, network retransmits. Queues growing are the earliest sign.

Common culprits: a slow dependency, a missing index after data growth, GC pressure, lock contention, a noisy neighbour on shared hardware, cold caches after a restart, no connection reuse (a fresh DNS lookup and TLS handshake on every call), and a retry storm adding load on top of load.

Mitigate: roll back the change, shed or rate-limit low-priority traffic, scale out if CPU-bound (it will not help if the bottleneck is a shared database), serve cached or stale data, and tighten timeouts so slow calls stop holding threads.

Prevent: latency SLOs with burn-rate alerts, per-dependency timeouts and circuit breakers, load tests on realistic data volumes, and canary deploys that compare latency automatically.

The error rate climbs right after a deploy. What do you do? Senior
Say: "If the timing matches, roll back or halt the rollout first, then diagnose by comparing the new version's errors against the old version's."

Why roll back first. A deploy is the most likely cause when the timing lines up, and a rollback is the cheapest, best-understood mitigation you have. Debugging live in front of users prolongs the pain. A rollback that does not fix things is also useful information: it says the deploy was not the cause.

  1. Halt or roll back. With progressive delivery, stop the rollout so the blast radius stays at the canary percentage.
  2. Compare versions side by side over the same time window. Which error type is it: 5xx thrown from your code, a 4xx because a request contract changed, or timeouts to a dependency?
  3. Check what else shipped at the same moment: config, flags, a schema migration, a dependency upgrade, an API change from another team. The deploy may be a bystander.
  4. Reproduce in staging using a failing request copied from the logs, then write a regression test.

Trap 1: rollback safety. If the deploy included a schema change or wrote data in a new format, the old code may not read it (see the "cannot roll back" question). Know before you click.

Trap 2: latent problems. A deploy restarts every instance, which empties caches and connection pools. The errors may come from the cold start or a traffic pattern, not new bugs. If errors fade after 10 minutes, it was warm-up.

Prevent: progressive delivery (1, 10, 50, 100 percent) with automated analysis that halts when error rate regresses against the baseline; feature flags to separate deploy from release; backward-compatible changes (expand, then contract).

Database CPU is at 100 percent. What do you check and do? Senior
Say: "Find the top queries by total time, check whether a plan or index changed or traffic changed, kill the runaway work, add the missing index, then fix the query or cache properly."

Why it is serious. The database is usually the one component you cannot scale out cheaply. At 100 percent CPU every query slows, connections pile up, the app's pools exhaust, and the outage spreads upward.

Find who is using the CPU.

  • Rank queries by total time (calls multiplied by average time): pg_stat_statements in PostgreSQL, performance_schema or the slow query log in MySQL, Query Store or DMVs in SQL Server. A cheap query run a million times per minute is as harmful as one huge query, so ranking by slowest single run misses it.
  • Plan flip? The optimizer may have switched to a full scan after statistics went stale or the table grew. Was an index dropped, or never created for the new data volume?
  • Traffic change? A new feature, bot traffic, or a cache that stopped working and sent all reads to the database.
  • Lock pile-up? Many sessions spinning on the same row or waiting on one long transaction.

Example. pg_stat_statements shows SELECT * FROM orders WHERE customer_ref = $1 consuming 70 percent of time. EXPLAIN shows a sequential scan over 80 million rows because there is no index on customer_ref. Creating one with CREATE INDEX CONCURRENTLY (so writes are not blocked) drops CPU to 20 percent in minutes.

Mitigate now: kill runaway queries, add the missing index online, switch off the offending feature, shift reads to replicas, throttle the caller, or scale the instance up as a stopgap.

Fix properly: rewrite the query, add covering indexes, remove N+1 patterns (one query per row), add a cache with correct invalidation, use keyset pagination instead of large OFFSET, and move analytics to a replica or warehouse.

Prevent: slow-query dashboards, review of queries in new code, load tests on production-sized data, and statement timeouts so no single query can run forever.

A query that was fast for a year suddenly becomes slow at scale. Why, and what do you do? Intermediate
Say: "Something crossed a threshold: table size, memory fit, plan choice or skew. I run EXPLAIN ANALYZE with real parameters, compare plans, refresh statistics and fix the index or query."

Why "fast for a year" does not mean "good". Many queries are O(n) or worse; at 100,000 rows they take 5 ms and nobody notices. At 50 million rows the same query takes seconds. The code did not change; the data did.

Typical triggers.

  • Plan flip: the optimizer decides by estimated cost. Past a size or after stale statistics it may choose a hash join or sequential scan instead of an index lookup.
  • The index stopped fitting in memory. While the hot part of the index sat in RAM, lookups were fast; once it grew beyond the buffer cache, every lookup becomes disk IO. This looks like a sudden cliff.
  • Hidden O(n): large OFFSET (the database still walks past all skipped rows), unindexed LIKE '%x%', functions on indexed columns (WHERE lower(email) = ... cannot use a plain index on email).
  • Data skew: one tenant has 60 percent of rows, so the plan good for the average tenant is bad for the big one.
  • Contention rises with concurrency (lock waits), not data size.

Steps. Run EXPLAIN (ANALYZE, BUFFERS) on the real query with the real, slow parameter values (plans depend on parameter values). Compare against the older plan if you saved one. Refresh statistics (ANALYZE). Add or fix the index. Switch to keyset pagination (WHERE id > last_seen ORDER BY id LIMIT 50). Partition or archive old data.

Prevent. Test with 10x today's data and track latency per query fingerprint over time, so a slow upward drift shows up months before the cliff.

Traffic doubles in a week (a launch or viral event). How do you prepare? Senior
Say: "Doubling is rarely about web servers. I load test to find the first non-horizontally-scalable bottleneck, pre-scale, protect the database, check third-party quotas, and prepare kill switches."

Core idea. Stateless tiers scale by adding machines; stateful or shared things (the primary database, a lock, a single queue partition, a vendor quota) do not. The capacity of the whole system is the capacity of its first bottleneck.

  1. Measure headroom. Peak utilization per tier today, and which resource saturates first. If the database is at 55 percent CPU at peak, doubling means 110 percent: it is the problem, not the web servers.
  2. Load test at 2x and 3x with production-like data and traffic mix (not just one easy endpoint). Find the first bottleneck, fix it, run again. Each run reveals the next one.
  3. Scale the easy parts: autoscaling for stateless tiers, but pre-warmed, because reactive scaling takes minutes and a sudden spike arrives faster than that. Use CDN and caches for read-heavy content.
  4. Protect the hard parts: read replicas, a connection pooler, queue-based write smoothing, rate limits, and load-shedding priorities (checkout over browse).
  5. Check external limits: third-party API quotas (payment, email, maps) and cloud account quotas (instance counts, IPs, IOPS, load balancer capacity). Some need days to raise.
  6. Prepare the human side: runbook, kill switches for expensive features, degraded modes ("read-only mode"), an on-call plan and a war room for launch day.

Pitfall. Load tests on an empty database or with cached data produce flattering and useless numbers. Also remember warm-up: a freshly scaled instance has cold caches and JIT.

Your autoscaler is flapping (scale up, scale down, repeat). Why, and how do you fix it? Intermediate
Say: "The control loop is oscillating. I add hysteresis and stabilization windows, scale on a better signal like queue depth or requests per instance, and scale out fast but in slowly."

What flapping is. An autoscaler is a feedback loop: it measures a metric, adds or removes instances, and the change feeds back into the metric. Feedback loops oscillate when the reaction is too fast, too strong or based on a delayed signal.

Worked example. Rule: scale out when average CPU is above 60 percent, scale in when below 55 percent. Four instances run at 65 percent, so a fifth is added; average CPU drops to 52 percent, so it is removed; CPU is back at 65 percent. Repeat forever, with new instances constantly starting, warming up and being killed.

Common causes.

  • Thresholds too close together (no dead band).
  • The metric responds to the scaling itself (average CPU falls when you add instances).
  • Slow startup: the metric stays high while new capacity is still warming, so the autoscaler keeps adding, then overshoots and scales in.
  • Noisy metric or too short a window.

Fixes.

  • Hysteresis: scale out at 70 percent, scale in at 40 percent.
  • Cooldown or stabilization windows: the Kubernetes HPA has a scale-down stabilization window (the default is on the order of minutes), so it only scales in to the highest recommendation in that window.
  • Better signal: queue depth, in-flight requests, or requests per instance scale linearly with load, unlike CPU averages.
  • Asymmetric speed: scale out quickly, scale in slowly.
  • Warm pools or minimum replicas to hide startup time.
  • Readiness: instances that are not yet ready must not be counted as capacity.
You suspect a memory leak in production. What do you do? Senior
Say: "Confirm the trend after GC, mitigate with staggered restarts, take heap dumps from a leaking instance and compare them, and also rule out a limits misconfiguration."

What a leak looks like. In a managed runtime such as the JVM, memory that is still reachable cannot be collected even if the program no longer needs it. Symptom: used memory after garbage collection climbs steadily and does not come back to a baseline; GC runs more often and takes longer; finally an OutOfMemoryError or the container is OOM-killed. Across restarts the graph is a sawtooth. The key word is "after GC": a high peak before GC is normal.

Mitigate first. Restart on a schedule or at a memory threshold, but stagger it (rolling restarts) so the whole fleet does not die together. Add instances if needed. If the leak started with a deploy, roll back.

Investigate.

  1. Take a heap dump from a leaking instance (a live instance that has grown a lot, ideally while it is out of the load balancer so the pause does not hurt users). Take a second one later.
  2. Compare the two. In a tool such as Eclipse MAT, open the dominator tree and the "leak suspects" report and see which object types keep growing and what holds them.
  3. Typical findings: unbounded caches or maps, listeners never deregistered, per-request data stored in static or thread-local state, unclosed streams or connections, unbounded in-memory queues, per-tenant data held forever.

Rule out "not a leak". A container memory limit lower than heap plus native overhead (metaspace, thread stacks, direct buffers) gets the container killed while the heap looks fine. Off-heap growth (direct buffers, native libraries) does not show in a heap dump at all and needs native memory tracking.

Prevent. Bounded caches (maximum size and TTL), bounded queues, an alert on the post-GC trend, soak tests that run for hours, and canaries that run long enough for a leak to show before wide rollout.

A disk is filling up on a production host. How do you handle it? Intermediate
Say: "Find what is growing, including deleted-but-open files, free space safely, then add retention, rotation and a time-to-full alert."

Why it matters. A full disk is a hard failure, not a slowdown. Databases stop accepting writes or crash, logging fails, processes cannot create temp files. So you act well before 100 percent.

  1. Find what is growing. du -sh /* | sort -h down the tree to the offender. Also check for files that are deleted but still held open by a process: lsof +L1 lists them. The space is not freed until the process closes the file, a classic mystery where df shows full but du shows plenty free.
  2. Usual suspects. Logs without rotation or at DEBUG level; core dumps or heap dumps (an OOM crash loop writes one each time); database WAL or binlog that is not archived or purged (a stuck replica or an abandoned replication slot makes the primary keep all WAL for it); temp files; a runaway message log.
  3. Mitigate. Rotate or truncate logs (truncate rather than delete if the process still holds the file), delete old dumps, expand the volume (cloud volumes can often be grown online), and remove the stuck replication slot only after confirming nobody needs it.
  4. Prevent. Alert at around 70 and 85 percent plus on predicted time-to-full (a linear forecast catches a fast leak that threshold alerts catch too late), log rotation and retention, shipping logs off-host, quotas, and watching inodes as well: a disk can be full with free bytes but no free inodes if millions of tiny files exist.
Trap: alerting only at 99 percent. A fast-growing file can fill the remaining 1 percent in a minute, long before a human reacts.
One tenant is hurting everyone else in a multi-tenant system (noisy neighbour). What do you do? Senior
Say: "Prove it with per-tenant metrics, throttle that tenant immediately, then design in quotas, bulkheads and cell or shard isolation so one tenant cannot consume shared capacity."

The problem. In a multi-tenant system all customers share workers, pools and databases to keep cost low. Shared resources mean one customer's huge import, expensive report or runaway integration can starve everyone else. That is the noisy neighbour.

Detect. You need per-tenant dimensions on metrics (requests, CPU time, DB time, bytes, queue wait). Without them you cannot even prove it is one tenant: the symptoms just look like "the system is slow". Keep cardinality sane by tracking the top N tenants.

Mitigate now. Throttle that tenant with a per-tenant rate or concurrency cap, move their work to a lower-priority queue, or temporarily put them on dedicated capacity. Contact them if it is a runaway script.

Design fixes.

  • Per-tenant quotas and fair queuing: weighted fair share so one tenant cannot hold the whole worker pool. Example: 20 workers, each tenant limited to 4 concurrent jobs, so a tenant submitting 10,000 jobs queues behind itself rather than blocking others.
  • Bulkheads: separate thread pools, connection pools or queues per tenant tier, so exhaustion stays contained.
  • Cells or shuffle sharding: split the fleet into independent cells and assign each tenant to a small subset. A bad tenant then affects only the few others who share its cell.
  • Shard by tenant with the ability to move a big tenant to its own shard.
  • Cost-based limits (query complexity, rows scanned), not just request counts, because one request can be a thousand times costlier than another.

Pitfall. Throttling by request count while the abuse is a few very expensive requests. Measure resource cost, not hits.

A third-party API you depend on (payments, email, maps) goes down. What happens and what should you have designed? Senior
Say: "Without protection their outage becomes ours through hung threads. I use short timeouts, circuit breakers, queues where the business allows, idempotency keys, and a fallback or second provider for critical paths."

What happens without protection. Your service calls the vendor with a long or no timeout. The vendor does not refuse; it just hangs. Each of your threads waits 30 seconds, the pool fills, your whole service stops answering, including the parts that never touch the vendor. Their outage has become your outage (a cascading failure, see below).

Design for it.

  • Short timeouts sized to the vendor's normal latency (for example p99 times 2), not the default of 30 seconds or infinity.
  • Circuit breaker: after N failures it opens and calls fail immediately for a cool-off period, then it probes with a few trial calls. This stops you hammering a vendor already struggling.
  • Fallback behaviour: hide the maps widget, not the whole page; show cached exchange rates; mark an order "payment pending".
  • Decouple through a queue where the business allows it: accept the order, charge asynchronously; enqueue the email and send it later.
  • Idempotency keys so that retries after recovery do not double-charge or double-send.
  • Second provider for critical paths (route payments across two PSPs), with a documented switch.
  • Monitor the vendor with your own synthetic checks and their status feed; keep the contract SLA in mind.

During the outage. Communicate to customers, queue what you can, and when the vendor returns reconcile: compare your records with theirs, because some calls that looked like failures actually succeeded on their side.

Scenarios: databases, caches and queues

Replica lag keeps growing. How do you investigate and what do you do? Senior
Say: "Lag means the replica applies changes slower than the primary produces them. I find the bulk write or the slow apply, relieve it, route lag-sensitive reads to the primary, and design for read-your-writes."

What replica lag is. A replica copies the primary's change stream (WAL in PostgreSQL, binlog in MySQL) and replays it. Lag is the delay between a commit on the primary and its visibility on the replica. A small steady lag (milliseconds) is normal. Lag that keeps growing means replay is slower than production of changes, so the gap widens until you fix something.

Why it hurts. Users write, then read from a replica and do not see their own change ("I saved it and it vanished"). Failover to a lagging replica loses the unreplicated data. A replica that is minutes behind serves stale data to reports.

Common causes.

  • A heavy write burst, a huge batch update or bulk delete.
  • Single-threaded apply: classic MySQL replication replays serially while the primary commits in parallel, so the replica cannot keep up.
  • Long queries on the replica blocking replay (PostgreSQL recovery conflicts: replay waits, or cancels the query, depending on settings).
  • An underpowered replica (smaller instance, slower disk) or a network limit between regions.
  • One giant transaction that must be applied as a unit and appears only when it finishes.

Investigate. Look at the lag graph (when did it begin?), the primary's write rate, the replica's CPU, IO and apply-thread state, long-running queries on the replica, and whether one big transaction is in flight.

Mitigate. Stop or throttle the bulk job; kill long replica queries; remove the lagging replica from the read pool until it catches up; enable parallel apply (for example multi-threaded replication in MySQL); upgrade the replica.

Product-level safety (read-your-writes). After a user writes, read their data from the primary for a few seconds, or pin the session to the primary. A stronger approach: return a version token (an LSN or GTID) with the write, and have reads go only to a replica that has reached that position. Break batch jobs into small chunks with sleeps between them, alert on lag in seconds, and have the application stop using replicas past a threshold.

The cache hit ratio suddenly collapses. What do you do? Senior
Say: "Protect the database first because misses fall through to it, then find why the cache stopped hitting: node loss, key change, eviction or synchronized expiry."

Why this is urgent. A cache absorbs most reads so the database only sees a small fraction. If the hit rate falls from 98 percent to 40 percent, database traffic rises about 30x (misses go from 2 percent to 60 percent of reads). A database sized for 2 percent misses falls over, and then everything fails.

Likely causes.

  • A cache node died or restarted, so it is cold (all its keys are gone).
  • A flush, or a key-prefix or version change in a deploy: new code builds keys the old entries do not match.
  • Eviction: memory is full or a big new key set pushed out the useful entries.
  • TTLs identical, so everything expired at the same moment.
  • A crawler or bot scanning cold keys, or cache penetration (requests for keys that do not exist, which are never cached).

Mitigate. Shed load or rate limit; enable request coalescing so only one miss refills a key while the others wait; serve stale data while refreshing in the background; warm hot keys by hand; scale the cache or fix the memory limit; roll back the key change.

Prevent.

  • Add random jitter to TTLs so expiries spread out.
  • Use consistent hashing so a node loss only moves a slice of keys, not all of them.
  • Cache negative results with a short TTL, or put a Bloom filter in front for keys that cannot exist.
  • Never change key format without a dual-read period (read new key, fall back to old).
  • Size the database to survive a cold cache, or put load shedding in front of it.
Everything stampedes the database after a restart or cache flush (thundering herd). What is happening and how do you stop it? Senior
Say: "Many concurrent misses all recompute the same value. Fix it with single-flight coalescing, TTL jitter, stale-while-revalidate and gradual ramp-up."

What happens, step by step.

  1. A hot key (say the homepage data, read 5,000 times a second) expires at time T.
  2. In the next 200 ms, 1,000 requests all miss and all query the database for the same expensive result.
  3. The database slows, so each recomputation takes longer; more requests arrive meanwhile and also miss.
  4. The pile-up deepens (a positive feedback loop) until the database falls over.

A restart is worse: every client reconnects at once, every cache is empty, and every connection pool opens together. This is the "herd".

TechniqueHow it helps
Request coalescing (single-flight, a lock per key)One request recomputes; the others wait for or reuse the result. 1,000 queries become 1
TTL jitterKeys do not all expire at the same instant (TTL = 300 s plus a random 0 to 60 s)
Stale-while-revalidate / soft TTLServe the old value while one worker refreshes it, so users never wait

Which to choose. For a known set of hot keys use proactive refresh or stale-while-revalidate (best user experience). For unpredictable keys use coalescing. Always add jitter since it is free. For restarts, ramp up gradually and keep shedding as the last line.

Pitfall. A lock per key must have a timeout, otherwise a crashed lock holder blocks the key forever; and coalescing in-process only collapses requests per instance, so with 100 instances you still get 100 queries (a distributed lock or a shared cache layer solves it).

You find a hot partition or hot key. How do you handle it? Senior
Say: "First prove it is skew with per-partition metrics, then choose between spreading the key (salting, sub-counters) and absorbing the load (caching, batching)."

What it is. Partitioned stores (sharded databases, Kafka, DynamoDB) spread load by key, assuming keys are spread evenly. If one key gets far more traffic than the rest (a celebrity account, a viral product, one giant tenant), the partition holding it saturates while others sit idle. Adding more nodes does not help, because one key lives on one partition.

Another cause: monotonic keys. Timestamps or auto-increment ids as the partition key send all new writes to the latest range, which is one partition.

Symptoms. Throttling or high latency for a subset of requests, one shard at 95 percent while the average is 20 percent.

Fixes by type.

  • Hot read key: cache it in process, replicate across several cache nodes by using key suffixes (product:42#0 to #7, read a random one), or use a CDN.
  • Hot write key (a like counter): split into N sub-counters, write to a random one, and sum them on read. Or buffer increments and flush in batches.
  • Skewed partition key: salt it (user_id#hash(...) mod 8) at the cost of a scatter-gather read, or pick a higher-cardinality key. Give giant tenants dedicated partitions.
  • Monotonic key: hash the prefix or use a different ordering so writes spread.
  • Adaptive capacity or auto-split where the store supports it helps, but never relies on it for a single hot key: one key cannot be split.

Trade-off. Salting spreads writes but makes reads more expensive; caching absorbs reads but not writes. Match the fix to whether the heat is on reads or writes.

A queue backlog keeps growing. What do you do? Senior
Say: "Backlog grows when arrivals exceed processing, so I find which changed: consumers slowed or died, or producers spiked. I scale or fix consumers, dead-letter poison messages and shed low-priority work."

The arithmetic. Backlog growth equals arrival rate minus processing rate. If 1,000 messages per second arrive and consumers handle 800, the queue grows by 200 per second, 12,000 per minute, forever, until something changes. Backlog does not fix itself unless processing exceeds arrivals for a while.

  1. Consumers. Alive? Crash-looping? Stuck? Did time per message rise (slow dependency, bigger payloads, a poison message retried forever)? Check lag per partition: one huge partition means a hot key or a stuck consumer.
  2. Producers. Did the rate spike: a batch job, a retry loop, an upstream bug producing duplicates?
  3. Mitigate. Scale consumers (in Kafka-like systems, parallelism is capped by partition count, so you may need more partitions); route poison messages to a dead-letter queue; pause low-priority producers; drop or sample non-critical messages; add a priority lane for important traffic.
  4. Fix. Batch processing, removing a slow synchronous call, and making consumers idempotent so retries are cheap.
  5. Prevent. Alert on lag growth and message age (how old the oldest message is, which reflects user impact; depth alone can mislead), a DLQ with limited retries, backpressure to producers, and capacity tests.
Trap: adding consumers does not help if the bottleneck is the database they write to (they just hammer it harder), or if ordering needs one consumer per key. Find the real bottleneck first.
One bad message blocks the whole queue or partition. What is the pattern to handle it? Intermediate
Say: "That is a poison message. Retry a bounded number of times with backoff, then send it to a dead-letter queue with its error, commit past it, and alert."

What happens. In an ordered log such as a Kafka partition, the consumer must process message 41 before 42. If message 41 always throws (malformed JSON, unexpected null, a schema mismatch), the consumer does not commit, restarts, reads 41 again, and fails again, forever. Everything behind it is stuck: head-of-line blocking.

The pattern.

  1. Catch the failure and classify it. Transient (a timeout, a deadlock, a dependency blip): retry with backoff, possibly through a delayed retry topic so the main partition keeps moving. Permanent (parse error, validation failure): do not retry; send to the DLQ immediately.
  2. After a bounded number of attempts, publish the message to a dead-letter queue with metadata: original topic, partition, offset, error, stack trace, attempt count.
  3. Commit the offset past it so the consumer moves on.
  4. Alert on DLQ growth; a silent DLQ is a place where messages go to be forgotten.
  5. Keep a tool to inspect and replay DLQ messages after the fix.

Prevent. Validate and version schemas at the producer (a schema registry), so bad messages are rejected at the source.

Trade-off. Skipping a message breaks strict ordering for that key. If ordering is critical, you may have to pause the key and page a human rather than skip.

Connection pool exhaustion: requests hang and the logs say "timeout waiting for connection". What now? Senior
Say: "All connections are checked out. Either demand rose, each request holds a connection longer, or connections leak. I take a thread dump, look at the database's sessions, and fix the hold time rather than blindly raising the pool size."

What a pool is. Opening a database connection is expensive, so apps keep a small pool (say 20) and requests borrow one, use it and return it. If all 20 are borrowed, the 21st request waits; if the wait exceeds the pool timeout you get "timeout waiting for connection".

Why it exhausts. Little's law: connections in use = arrival rate x time held. If 100 requests per second each hold a connection for 50 ms, you use 5 connections. If a slow query raises hold time to 2 seconds, you need 200. Same traffic, 40x more connections. Or a connection leaks (not closed on an error path) and the pool slowly drains.

Check.

  • Pool metrics: active versus max, and wait time.
  • A thread dump: what are the threads holding connections doing? (Waiting on a lock, calling a remote service, stuck in a loop.)
  • The database's view: sessions "idle in transaction", long-running, or waiting on locks.
  • A recent change, especially a remote call added inside a transaction.

Mitigate. Kill stuck sessions, restart leaking instances, shed load. Raising the pool size is a risky fix: the database has a total connection limit across all app instances (50 instances x 40 connections = 2,000), and more concurrent queries usually make the database slower, not faster. Keep pools small and put a pooler (PgBouncer, RDS Proxy) in front when many instances connect.

Fix and prevent. Use try-with-resources so connections always return; set leak-detection thresholds and max lifetimes; never do remote calls inside a transaction; set statement and lock timeouts; size pools by Little's law; alert on pool wait time.

Scenarios: cascading failure and retry storms

A retry storm is taking down a dependency. What is happening and how do you stop it? Senior
Say: "Retries multiply load on an already weak dependency and keep it down. Stop it by shedding load and disabling retries; prevent it with backoff with jitter, retry budgets, one retry layer and deadlines."

The multiplication effect. A dependency slows briefly. Every client times out and retries. Retries happen at several layers at once: the SDK retries 3 times, the service wraps that in 3 more, the gateway in 3 more. One user request can turn into 3 x 3 x 3 = 27 calls. Load jumps just when the dependency is weakest, so it cannot recover, and stays down even after the original trigger has gone. This self-sustaining state is called a metastable failure.

Timeline of a storm.

  1. t=0: a database failover causes 10 seconds of slowness.
  2. t=10: requests time out; clients retry immediately, tripling traffic.
  3. t=20: the recovered database is now receiving 3x the normal load and stays saturated.
  4. t=60: still down, although the original fault ended 50 seconds ago.

Stop it now. Shed load at or in front of the dependency; disable retries by config or flag; open circuit breakers manually; rate limit clients; restart in stages; let traffic back gradually.

Design to avoid it.

  • Exponential backoff with jitter (wait 100 ms, 200, 400 ... each randomized) and a cap on attempts, so retries spread out.
  • Retry budget: retries may be at most about 10 percent of requests (a typical figure, tune it), instead of a per-request count. During a real outage the budget runs out and retries stop automatically.
  • Retry at one layer only, not at every layer of the chain.
  • Circuit breakers and server-side load shedding returning a fast 503 with Retry-After.
  • Deadlines propagated down the chain so work for a caller who has already given up is dropped.
  • Idempotent operations so retries are safe.
One slow dependency causes a cascading failure across the platform. How does it happen and how do you contain it? Senior
Say: "A slow dependency is worse than a dead one, since callers hold threads waiting. Pools fill hop by hop up the call chain. Contain it with timeouts, circuit breakers, bulkheads, load shedding and fallbacks."

How it unfolds.

  1. D becomes slow (not dead, just slow: 5 seconds per call instead of 50 ms).
  2. C has 200 threads. Each is now stuck for 5 s waiting on D, so after a moment all 200 are busy and C cannot take new work, including requests that do not need D.
  3. B's calls to C now hang or time out; B's thread pool fills the same way.
  4. A, the front door, fills last. The whole platform looks down although only D is broken.

Why "slow" is worse than "down". A dead service refuses connections instantly and callers fail fast. A slow one holds resources until timeouts expire.

Containment.

  • Timeouts at each hop, shorter than the caller's own timeout (so the callee gives up before the caller does).
  • Circuit breakers to stop calling D while it is bad.
  • Bulkheads: a separate thread or connection pool per dependency, so D can only exhaust its own pool and other features keep working.
  • Load shedding at each service when it is overloaded, returning fast errors.
  • Fallbacks and cached data; async decoupling with queues; avoid deep synchronous call chains.
  • Careful health checks: if one slow dependency makes every instance fail health, the load balancer removes all of them and worsens the outage (next question).
A shared dependency fails, health checks fail everywhere, and the load balancer removes every instance. What went wrong? Intermediate
Say: "The health check was too deep. I separate liveness from readiness, keep shared dependencies out of liveness, and configure the load balancer to fail open when everything looks unhealthy."

What happened. The health endpoint checked the database. The database had a 30-second blip. Every instance reported unhealthy at the same moment, the load balancer removed all of them, and users got errors from the balancer itself, even though the application processes were running fine and could have served cached or partial data. A brief dependency issue became a total outage. If instances are also restarted on failed health (a Kubernetes liveness probe), the damage is worse: a restart storm with cold caches.

Distinguish the three questions.

CheckQuestionOn failureShould include shared dependencies?
LivenessIs the process alive and not deadlocked?Restart itNo
ReadinessCan this instance serve traffic right now?Stop routing to it, do not restartOnly checks that are local or instance-specific
Deep or dependency checkIs the whole stack healthy?Alert, dashboardsYes, but for monitoring, not for routing

Fixes.

  • Keep dependency checks out of liveness. A shared database being down is not fixed by restarting your pods.
  • Configure the load balancer with a minimum healthy percentage or "fail open": if all targets look bad, route to all of them anyway. Many load balancers can do this; it is a deliberate setting.
  • Degrade gracefully (serve cached or partial responses) instead of failing health.
  • Use a short grace period or several consecutive failures before marking unhealthy, to ignore blips.

Scenarios: consistency, messaging and time

After a network partition you discover two nodes both think they are the primary (split brain). What do you do? Senior
Say: "Fence one side so only one accepts writes, pick the side with the most complete data, freeze auto-failover, then reconcile the divergent writes. To prevent it, use quorum-based election and fencing tokens."

What split brain is. A cluster has one primary that accepts writes. If the network splits and the standby cannot see the primary, it may decide the primary is dead and promote itself. But the old primary is still alive and still serving its side of the network. Now two nodes both accept writes and their data diverges. When the network heals you cannot simply merge: the same row may have two different values.

Immediate steps.

  1. Stop writes to one side (fencing): revoke its storage or network access, power it off, or block it at the load balancer. Do this before anything else; every second adds more divergent writes.
  2. Choose the side with the most complete or most important data as the survivor, and freeze automated failover so the tooling does not flip again.
  3. Reconcile: find the writes that landed on the losing side during the overlap. Conflicting rows need business rules (latest order wins? sum the balances?) or manual review. Replay the safe ones.
  4. Fix downstream: systems that consumed events or replication from the wrong primary must be corrected too.

Design to avoid it.

  • Quorum-based leader election (Raft, Paxos, ZooKeeper, etcd): a leader needs votes from a majority, so at most one side of any partition can have a leader. With 5 nodes split 3-2, only the 3 side can elect.
  • Fencing tokens: each leadership term gets an increasing number; the storage layer rejects writes bearing an older number, so a zombie leader cannot do damage even if it believes it is still leader.
  • STONITH (shoot the other node in the head): the new primary powers off the old one before taking over.
  • An odd number of voters across failure domains, or a witness node.
  • Do not let a failover tool promote on a short blip; require agreement from several observers or a longer timeout.

Data is inconsistent between two services (the order says paid, the payment service says no). How do you handle it? Senior
Say: "Reconcile now against the declared source of truth. Long term: one owner per fact, a transactional outbox, idempotent consumers, sagas with compensation, and routine reconciliation."

Why it happens. Two separate databases cannot be updated atomically. The classic mistake is a dual write: the service updates its own database and then publishes an event (or calls another service). If the process crashes between the two steps, one side has the change and the other does not. Other causes: a lost or duplicated message, out-of-order events, a saga that failed halfway, or two services both claiming to own the same fact.

Short term. Run a reconciliation job that compares both sources, repairs the difference according to the declared authority (the payment provider is the truth for payments), and writes an audit record of every repair. Pause the automatic flows that keep creating new mismatches if you can.

Long term.

  • One owner per fact. Others keep read-only copies, updated from events.
  • Transactional outbox: in the same local transaction, write the business row and an "outbox" row for the event. A separate relay reads the outbox and publishes (at least once). Either both happen or neither does.
  • Idempotent consumers and versioned or timestamped events, so duplicates and reordering are harmless.
  • Sagas with compensating actions and timeouts for multi-service workflows (charge, then ship; if shipping fails, refund).
  • Routine reconciliation as a standard safety net, with alerts on mismatch counts. Treat it as a feature, not a sign of failure: every real distributed system needs one.

Example. Order service sets status=PAID and then publishes OrderPaid. It crashes in between. With the outbox the status and the event commit together, and the relay publishes after restart, so the shipping service eventually hears about it.

Customers were charged twice. How could it happen and how do you prevent it? Senior
Say: "Retries or redelivery re-ran the charge. Prevent it with an idempotency key stored atomically with the result, conditional state transitions, and consumer dedupe. Exactly-once delivery does not exist; at-least-once plus idempotence does."

How it happens. The network is unreliable, so callers retry and brokers redeliver. A client sends a charge, the server charges the card, but the response is lost; the client times out and retries, so the card is charged again. Other causes: a user double-clicking, a queue redelivering after a consumer crashed before acknowledging, a failover replaying a request, a job scheduled on two nodes.

Immediate. Find duplicates by looking for repeated (customer, amount, time window, reference) tuples, refund them, and notify customers.

Prevention.

  1. Idempotency key: the client generates a unique id per logical operation (one per checkout attempt) and sends it. The server stores it under a unique constraint with the result. A repeat with the same key returns the saved result instead of charging again. Payment providers expose this as an idempotency-key header.
  2. Atomic dedup: write the state change and the dedup record in the same transaction. Two separate steps (check, then write) have a race.
  3. Conditional updates: compare-and-set on a version or state machine, such as UPDATE orders SET status='PAID' WHERE id=? AND status='PENDING', so the transition happens once.
  4. Consumer dedupe by message id for queue-driven flows.
  5. Scheduled jobs: use a lock or leader election, plus a dedup guard inside the job, because locks can expire while the job is still running.
Trap: "exactly-once delivery" is not possible over an unreliable network. You get at-least-once delivery plus idempotent processing, which together behave like exactly-once effects. (Some systems, such as Kafka transactions, offer exactly-once within a bounded scope, but the side effects outside, such as a card charge, still need idempotency.)
Messages are being lost: producers say sent, consumers never saw them. Where do you look? Senior
Say: "Walk the pipeline hop by hop and ask who acknowledged what: producer, broker, consumer, routing. Count messages at each stage with a unique id per message."

Approach. A message passes through several hands. At each hand-off ask "who took responsibility, and when?" Loss is almost always a place where someone took responsibility too early.

  1. Producer. Fire-and-forget without waiting for an ack; the process exited before its buffer flushed; no retry on failure; the DB commit succeeded but the publish failed (a dual write; fix with an outbox).
  2. Broker. Weak acknowledgement (in Kafka, acks=1 means the leader acked before followers copied it, so a leader crash loses the message); replication factor 1; unclean leader election (an out-of-date replica becomes leader); retention shorter than consumer lag, so messages expired before being read.
  3. Consumer. Auto-commit offsets before processing finished (a crash then skips the message); an exception swallowed and acknowledged; a filter bug that drops messages; messages sent to a DLQ nobody watches.
  4. Network and routing. Wrong topic or routing key; a queue TTL expiring messages.

Debug. Add end-to-end counters (produced, broker received, consumed) and a unique message id you can search in each hop's logs. The stage where the count first drops is where the loss occurs.

Fix. For Kafka: acks=all with a replication factor of 3 and min.insync.replicas of 2, disable unclean leader election, commit offsets after processing succeeds, use the outbox pattern on the producer side, alert on DLQs, and reconcile producer and consumer counts periodically.

What kinds of bugs does clock skew cause, and how do you design around it? Senior
Say: "Clocks on different machines disagree and can jump. I avoid timestamps for ordering and correctness: monotonic clocks for durations, logical clocks or sequences for ordering, and fencing tokens instead of lease timing."

Why it is a problem. Each machine has its own clock, kept roughly right by NTP. They drift (milliseconds, sometimes seconds, far more if NTP breaks) and can jump backward when corrected. Code that assumes "time is the same everywhere" is subtly wrong.

Real bugs.

  • Last-write-wins by timestamp: node A (clock 2 s behind) writes later but stamps an earlier time; its write is silently discarded in favour of an older one.
  • Lease or lock expiry decided locally: a process pauses for a long GC, its lease expires on the clock server, another node takes the lock, and the first resumes still believing it holds it. Two writers.
  • Certificate or token validation fails ("not yet valid" or "expired") on a host with a wrong clock.
  • TTL and ordering errors: negative durations, scheduled jobs running twice or not at all, events appearing out of order.
  • Leap seconds have caused hangs or CPU spikes in some systems in the past.

Design around it.

  • Measure durations with a monotonic clock (never goes backward), not wall-clock time.
  • For ordering use logical clocks (Lamport, vector clocks) or database-generated sequence numbers.
  • Use fencing tokens (increasing numbers checked by the storage) instead of trusting lease timing.
  • Monitor NTP offset and alert on it; leave margins on token and certificate validity.
  • Where wall-clock order truly matters, use a time source with bounded uncertainty: Google Spanner's TrueTime exposes an uncertainty interval and uses "commit wait" so timestamps are safe.
Someone accidentally deleted production data. How do you restore, and how do you prevent it? Senior
Say: "Stop the damage, freeze replication if the delete has not yet propagated, restore to a point in time into a separate instance, and merge the lost rows back so valid newer writes are not lost. Prevent with tested restores, least privilege and delayed replicas."

Immediate. Stop further damage (disable the job, revoke the access) and preserve evidence. Replicas apply deletes too, so pause replication immediately on any replica that has not yet applied the delete; a deliberately delayed replica (say one hour behind) exists exactly for this. Decide the target: restore the whole database to a point in time, or only the affected rows.

Restore step by step.

  1. Take the most recent base backup and replay WAL or binlog up to a moment just before the mistake (point-in-time recovery, PITR). Do this on a separate instance, not production.
  2. Extract the lost rows from the restored copy.
  3. Merge them back into production. Restoring over production would erase the valid writes made after the deletion, so you merge, not overwrite.

Cheaper options first: soft-deleted data can be undeleted at the application layer; object stores with versioning let you restore previous versions.

Prevent.

  • Backups tested by actually restoring, on a schedule. An untested backup is a hope, not a backup (the GitLab 2017 lesson).
  • PITR enabled; a delayed replica; backups kept in a separate account or region with deletion protection (so a compromised or mistaken admin cannot delete them as well).
  • Least privilege: no production admin credentials for daily work; break-glass access, peer review for destructive commands, and prompts that make the target environment obvious.
  • Soft deletes with retention windows; deletion-protection flags on databases and buckets.
  • Know your RPO (how much data you may lose) and RTO (how long recovery may take) and design backups to meet them.

Remember: replication is not backup; it copies the delete.

Scenarios: change, deploy and configuration

A schema migration goes wrong in production. How do you do migrations safely? Senior
Say: "Use expand, migrate, contract: add backward-compatible structure, dual write, backfill in throttled batches, switch reads, then remove the old structure in a later release. Use online DDL and lock timeouts."

How migrations hurt.

  • ALTER TABLE may take a lock that blocks reads and writes on a big table. Worse, a waiting ALTER blocks every query queued behind it, so one slow transaction can freeze the table.
  • Adding a column with a default may rewrite the whole table in some engines or older versions (modern PostgreSQL and MySQL can often add it instantly, but check your version).
  • Adding an index non-concurrently can block writes.
  • A backfill saturates IO and replication.
  • Renaming or dropping a column breaks the old application version, which is still running during a rolling deploy (code and schema never switch at the same instant).

The safe pattern, step by step (example: split name into first_name and last_name):

  1. Expand: add the nullable new columns (cheap and backward compatible); create indexes online or concurrently.
  2. Deploy code that writes both old and new, and still reads the old.
  3. Backfill existing rows in small batches, with throttling, while watching replica lag.
  4. Switch reads to the new columns; verify with comparisons or shadow reads.
  5. Contract: stop writing the old column, and drop it in a later release once rollback is no longer needed.

Guardrails. Test on a production-sized copy. Set a lock timeout so the migration fails fast instead of queuing everything behind it. Use online schema-change tools (gh-ost, pt-online-schema-change, or the engine's native online DDL). Keep each step independently reversible. Run migrations separately from code releases.

You need to roll back a release but cannot because data has already changed. What do you do? Senior
Say: "Roll forward with a hotfix, or disable the feature with a flag while the data stays. The real answer is prevention: make every release backward compatible with the previous one."

Why it happens. A rollback only reverts code. If the new version wrote data in a new format, ran a destructive migration, or published messages the old code cannot parse, the old code now meets data it does not understand. Reverting would cause new errors.

Options during the incident.

  1. Roll forward: fix the bug and ship quickly. Often the fastest, if the pipeline is quick.
  2. Redeploy old code if it happens to tolerate the new data (test this carefully; it often does not).
  3. Write a fixer script to convert the data back, then roll back.
  4. Disable the new feature with a flag while the data remains in place.

Prevention is the real answer.

  • Every release should be compatible with the previous one: old code runs on the new schema, and new code runs on the old schema. Then rollback is always safe.
  • Separate schema changes from the code releases that need them (expand first, contract much later).
  • Ship risky features behind flags.
  • Version message and API formats.
  • Never drop or rewrite data in the same release that depends on it.
  • Practice rollback as part of the release process, so you know it works.

A configuration change broke everything at once. Why is this so common and how do you protect against it? Senior
Say: "Config often skips the safeguards code goes through: it is pushed globally and instantly with no canary. Treat config as code, roll it out progressively, keep a last known good, and have a kill switch that does not depend on what is broken."

Why config is dangerous. Code changes usually pass through review, tests and canaries. Config often does not: it is pushed to every instance at once, changes are small and look harmless (one line), and a bad value (an empty list, a wrong regex, a lowered limit, a reference to an expired certificate) is read by every instance at the same moment. The blast radius is the whole fleet in seconds. Several famous outages were global pushes of this kind: Facebook 2021 (a network command), Cloudflare 2019 (a WAF rule), CrowdStrike 2024 (a content update).

Protections.

  • Config as code: version control, review, validation and schema checks before applying (reject an empty allow-list or a limit of zero).
  • Progressive rollout: one cell or region first, then widen, with automatic rollback on health regressions.
  • Last known good: if the new config fails to parse or health checks fail, the instance falls back to the previous one rather than crashing.
  • Emergency kill switch that does not depend on the broken component (do not put the switch behind the service you are trying to disable).
  • Audit trail: record every change so "what changed?" is a quick lookup.
  • Roll out risky config separately from code, so you can tell which caused a problem.
How do feature flags help during incidents, and what are their risks? Intermediate
Say: "Flags separate deploy from release, giving a seconds-fast kill switch and gradual exposure. The risks are stale flags, flag-service outages, untested combinations, and unreviewed flag changes."

What a flag is. A conditional in code, if (flags.enabled("new_checkout", user)), whose value comes from a config service you can change at runtime without redeploying.

How it helps in an incident.

  • Kill switch: turn off a misbehaving feature in seconds. A rollback takes minutes and may carry data risks; flipping a flag does not.
  • Gradual rollout: release to 1 percent of users, watch metrics, then widen. A bad feature then hurts 1 percent, not 100.
  • Load shedding: switch off expensive features (recommendations, exports) when the system is under strain, keeping the core alive.

Risks.

  • Stale flags pile up as dead code and combinations nobody tests. Remove flags once a rollout is complete.
  • Flag service outage: cache the last known values locally and default to a safe value if it is unreachable.
  • Untested combinations: with 10 flags there are 1,024 states.
  • Flag changes are production changes: they need audit and review like deployments.
  • Reusing an old flag for a new meaning is dangerous: Knight Capital (below) is the cautionary tale.

Scenarios: infrastructure, regions and security

An outage is caused by an expired certificate or a DNS problem. How do you respond and prevent it? Intermediate
Say: "Certs: issue and deploy a new one, reload, find every place the old one lives, and automate renewal with expiry alerts. DNS: remember TTL caching, lower TTL before changes, use more than one provider, and keep your recovery tooling off the DNS you are fixing."

Expired certificate. A TLS certificate has an expiry date. After it, clients refuse the handshake. The treacherous case is an internal service-to-service link using mTLS: it fails suddenly, looks like random errors, and the cert may have been issued years ago by someone who left.

  1. Issue a new certificate and deploy it. Many servers cache the certificate in memory, so reload or restart.
  2. Find every place the old one was used: load balancers, mTLS between services, client trust stores, pinned certificates.
  3. Prevent: automated issuance and renewal (ACME, a certificate manager), short-lived certs (so renewal is routine and exercised), an inventory, alerts at 30, 14 and 7 days, and a synthetic check that reads the certificate actually being served and its expiry.

DNS. Symptoms: intermittent "cannot resolve" errors, or traffic going to the wrong place after a change.

  • Caching: a record's TTL means a change, or a rollback of a change, takes that long to propagate, and some resolvers ignore TTLs. Lower the TTL before a planned change, not during the incident.
  • Review and stage DNS changes like code.
  • Avoid one DNS provider for critical zones (the Dyn 2016 lesson).
  • Make sure your monitoring and recovery tooling do not depend on the same DNS you are repairing (Facebook 2021).
A whole cloud region goes down. What happens, and what should the design have been? Senior
Say: "It depends on the DR strategy chosen beforehand: backup and restore, pilot light, warm standby or active-active, trading cost against RTO and RPO. In the event I fail over by criteria, accept the RPO, and watch for hidden single-region dependencies."

Definitions. RTO is how long you can be down; RPO is how much recent data you can lose. Each strategy buys a different pair, at a different cost.

StrategyRTO / RPONotes
Backup and restore in another regionHours / minutes to hoursCheapest; slow; must be rehearsed
Pilot light (data replicated, compute off)Tens of minutesNeeds infrastructure as code to scale up quickly
Warm standbyMinutesScaled-down copy always running
Active-active multi-regionNear zeroHardest: conflict handling, data residency, cost

The honest answer is that what happens in the event is decided by the architecture you chose long before the incident.

During the event.

  1. Decide to fail over. A human decision with clear criteria is often better than an automatic one, since a bad automatic failover (the GitHub 2018 case) can itself cause damage. Pre-agree the criteria so it is fast.
  2. Shift traffic through DNS or global traffic management (watching TTLs).
  3. Promote replicas in the surviving region and accept the RPO: writes not yet replicated are lost or must be recovered later.
  4. Watch capacity. Everyone else is failing over too, so the standby region may lack capacity. Reserved capacity matters.

Hidden dependencies are the usual surprise: a global control plane, a single-region auth service, DNS, or the deployment tool living in the failed region. After recovery plan the fail-back as carefully as the failover. Run game days so the first failover is not in production anger.

A credential (API key or database password) was leaked, for example committed to a public repository. What do you do? Senior
Say: "Revoke or rotate immediately, assume compromise, audit what it accessed, hunt for persistence, notify as required, then add secret scanning and short-lived credentials."

Why immediate rotation. Automated scanners watch public repositories continuously, and leaked cloud keys are often abused within minutes. Deleting the commit or rewriting git history does not help: forks, caches and scrapers already have it. So assume it is compromised, and do not first spend time deciding whether it was abused.

  1. Revoke or rotate the credential now. If rotation is hard, that itself is a finding.
  2. Scope the blast radius. What could the credential access? Check audit logs (cloud trail, database logs, API usage) for use from unknown IPs, unusual regions or odd times.
  3. Contain. Revoke sessions or tokens issued with it. Check for persistence: new users, access keys, access rules, scheduled jobs, new compute instances (crypto-mining is common). Rotate anything it could read, such as secrets in the same vault.
  4. Notify. The security team, and legal or customers if data was accessed. Many jurisdictions have regulatory deadlines (some within 72 hours).
  5. Prevent. Secret scanning in pre-commit hooks and CI; a secret manager with short-lived dynamic credentials (so a leak expires by itself); least privilege and scoped keys; IP or network restrictions; and a rehearsed rotation procedure. A credential you cannot rotate quickly is a risk.
You are under a DDoS attack or a sudden abusive traffic spike. What do you do? Intermediate
Say: "First tell attack from a legitimate surge by traffic shape. Then layer defences: CDN or DDoS service at the edge, WAF and rate limits, bot challenges, caching, and protecting the origin."

Is it an attack? A marketing campaign also floods you with traffic. Look at the shape: are sources, user agents and paths diverse and plausible? Do requests carry valid sessions and complete real journeys? Attacks often hit one path, with identical headers, from unusual networks.

Two kinds of attack need different defences.

  • Volumetric (network or protocol floods): the goal is to saturate bandwidth. You cannot absorb it at your origin; you need a CDN or DDoS-protection provider with huge edge capacity.
  • Application-layer: a small number of requests that are costly for you (search, login, export, anything that does heavy database work). Edge volumetric protection does not stop these; you need rate limits, caching, authentication, and cheap rejection.

Layered defence.

  1. CDN or DDoS service absorbs volumetric floods.
  2. WAF rules and rate limits per IP, token and path.
  3. Bot challenges (CAPTCHA, proof-of-work) for suspicious sessions.
  4. Cache expensive public pages.
  5. Autoscaling with a spend cap, since unlimited scaling turns an attack into a large bill.
  6. Block by ASN or country only if clearly attributable.
  7. Protect the origin: allow only the CDN's addresses to reach it.

Pre-incident design. Keep origin IPs hidden, make expensive endpoints authenticated or cheap to reject, and have an escalation contact at the CDN or provider before you need one.

AWS S3 us-east-1 outage (2017): what happened and what does it teach? Senior
Say: "A typo is not a root cause; the root cause is a tool that allowed one mistyped input to remove too much capacity too fast, plus core subsystems that had not been cold-restarted at current scale."

Background. S3 is a storage service that countless sites, tools and even other AWS services depend on. Two core subsystems matter here: the index subsystem (metadata and location of every object) and the placement subsystem (decides where new objects are stored, and needs the index).

Timeline (per AWS's post-event summary, as I recall it).

  1. Trigger. In late February 2017 an engineer on the S3 team was debugging why the billing system was slower than expected. Following an established playbook, they ran a command to take a small number of servers out of a billing-related subsystem.
  2. The mistake. One input to the command was entered incorrectly, so a much larger set of servers was removed than intended, including servers supporting the index and placement subsystems.
  3. Impact. Those subsystems lost so much capacity that they needed a full restart. Without the index, S3 GET, LIST, PUT and DELETE requests in the region failed. Anything depending on S3 in us-east-1 (websites, build tools, other AWS services, and even the AWS status dashboard's own admin console) broke or degraded.
  4. Slow recovery. These subsystems had not been fully restarted for years. S3 had grown enormously, and safety checks and metadata loading made the restart take much longer than anyone expected. The outage lasted a few hours.

Why it escalated. Three latent conditions lined up: a tool with no guard on how much it could remove at once, no minimum-capacity floor, and an untested cold-start path whose duration had silently grown with scale.

Lessons (and AWS's stated fixes).

  • Guard operational tooling: remove capacity slowly, enforce a minimum capacity floor, require confirmation for a large blast radius. AWS reported changing the tool to do this.
  • Rehearse cold restarts of core components. Recovery time grows with scale if untested.
  • Partition into smaller cells so a single subsystem failure has a smaller blast radius; AWS reported work on this.
  • Hidden single-region dependencies, and a status page that does not depend on the thing it reports on.
GitHub 2018 outage: database failover and split brain. What happened? Senior
Say: "A 43-second network blip triggered an automatic failover to the other coast, creating writes in two places. Lessons: automatic failover on a blip is dangerous, cross-site latency changes behaviour, and restore time must be measured."

Background. GitHub ran MySQL clusters with a primary in a US East Coast data center and replicas elsewhere, managed by an orchestration tool (Orchestrator) that can promote a replica when it thinks the primary is gone.

Timeline (October 2018, per GitHub's post-mortem as I recall it).

  1. Trigger. Routine maintenance on networking equipment briefly cut connectivity between their East Coast network hub and the primary East Coast data center. The break lasted on the order of 40 seconds.
  2. Failover. During that gap the orchestration tool, seeing the primaries as unreachable, promoted new primaries in the West Coast data center.
  3. Divergence. When connectivity returned, the East Coast primaries still held a few seconds of writes that never reached the West. The West primaries had also accepted new writes. Each side held data the other lacked: split brain, and no way to simply fail back without losing data.
  4. Latency side effect. The application expected a local primary. Cross-country round trips made many operations slow, adding user-visible degradation on top of the data problem.
  5. Recovery. GitHub chose to preserve data integrity over availability. They kept the service degraded (some features paused, webhooks and Pages delayed) while restoring from backups and re-synchronizing and reconciling the divergent writes. Degraded service lasted roughly a day.

Lessons.

  • Automatic failover on a brief network blip is dangerous. Weigh the cost of an unnecessary failover against waiting. A short pause often costs less than a split brain.
  • Failing over across a high-latency link changes application behaviour, so test cross-site operation in advance.
  • Quorum and fencing are needed so two primaries cannot both accept writes; replication across sites must be designed for both sides diverging.
  • Choose consistency over availability when correctness is at stake, and be transparent with users (GitHub posted frequent updates).
  • Restoring from backups took long, so measure and practise restore time (your real RTO).
Cloudflare 2019 outage: regular expression CPU exhaustion. What happened? Senior
Say: "A WAF rule with a catastrophically backtracking regex was pushed to the whole fleet at once and exhausted CPU everywhere. Lessons: progressive rollout even for 'safe' rules, linear-time regex engines, and a global kill switch."

Background. Cloudflare's edge servers run a Web Application Firewall (WAF) that inspects every HTTP request against rules, many of them regular expressions. Rules are updated often to respond to new attacks, and at that time were pushed globally within seconds.

What is catastrophic backtracking? Many regex engines try alternatives by backtracking. For a pattern with nested or overlapping repeats (like (a+)+b or .*.*=.*) a non-matching input can force exponentially many attempts. A few KB of input can consume seconds of CPU.

Timeline (July 2019, per Cloudflare's post-mortem as I recall it).

  1. A new WAF managed rule is merged and passes the test suite, which did not measure CPU cost.
  2. It is deployed globally; the rule was in a "simulate" mode that logs rather than blocks, but the regex still ran on every request.
  3. CPU on edge machines worldwide reaches 100 percent, processing the pathological regex. Traffic returns 502 errors; global traffic drops sharply.
  4. Engineers initially suspect an attack because the pattern looks like one. Internal tools slowed as well.
  5. They use a global kill switch to disable the WAF managed rules, and service recovers in under an hour in total (about half an hour of impact by their account).
  6. The rule is fixed and re-released carefully; the regex engine used was later changed.

Lessons.

  • Roll out even "safe" rule or config pushes progressively with canaries, not globally at once.
  • Use regex engines with linear-time guarantees (RE2-style) for fast-changing or untrusted patterns, or enforce CPU limits and timeouts per rule.
  • Have a fast global kill switch that still works when the platform is overloaded.
  • Include performance tests (CPU cost under adversarial input) in the change pipeline, not just correctness tests.
Facebook (Meta) October 2021 outage: BGP and DNS. What happened? Senior
Say: "A maintenance command disconnected the backbone, and DNS servers designed to withdraw their BGP routes when unhealthy did so, making the whole company vanish from the internet. Lessons: out-of-band access, fail-safes that cause global failure, and tested guardrails."

Background. Two internet systems matter. BGP is how networks advertise to the rest of the internet "these IP addresses are reachable through me". DNS turns names like facebook.com into IP addresses. Facebook runs its own authoritative DNS servers, advertised through BGP.

Timeline (October 2021, per Facebook's engineering write-ups as I recall them).

  1. Trigger. During routine maintenance, a command intended to assess the availability of global backbone capacity was issued. It unintentionally took down the connections that link Facebook's data centers.
  2. Guardrail failure. An audit tool meant to stop such a command had a bug and did not block it.
  3. Fail-safe becomes failure. Facebook's DNS servers run health checks: if they cannot reach the data centers, they conclude they are unhealthy and withdraw their BGP advertisements (so traffic goes to healthy ones). Here all of them lost contact at once, so all withdrew.
  4. Vanishing. With the routes gone, the rest of the internet could no longer find Facebook's DNS servers; every Facebook name stopped resolving (Instagram and WhatsApp too).
  5. Slow recovery. The internal tools and remote access engineers needed relied on the same network and DNS, so they were unreachable. Reports say engineers had to go on site to data centers, and physical and security controls slowed access. Service came back in about six hours, and traffic was restored in stages to avoid power and load surges.

Lessons.

  • Out-of-band access: the tools and communication used for recovery must not depend on the systems being recovered.
  • A safeguard that "fails safe" locally can be globally catastrophic: a health check that withdraws routes turned a partial fault into a total one.
  • Guard rails and audit tools for dangerous network commands need to be tested too.
  • Independent monitoring and communication channels, and a rehearsed recovery for total backbone loss.
Knight Capital (2012): what happened? Senior
Say: "A deploy reached seven of eight servers; the eighth ran old code in which a reused flag woke a dead test routine and flooded the market. Lessons: verified automated deploys, never repurpose flags, remove dead code, and independent kill switches."

Background. Knight Capital was a US market-making firm. Its order-routing system, SMARS, ran on eight servers. The company was preparing for a new NYSE program starting 1 August 2012 and shipped new code for it.

Timeline (from the SEC findings and widely reported analyses, hedged).

  1. Dead code lurking. Years earlier, an old feature (often called "Power Peg", a test routine for moving stock prices) had been retired but its code remained in the system.
  2. Flag reused. The new release reused the same flag that, in the old code, switched on Power Peg.
  3. Partial deploy. Deployment was manual and reached only seven of the eight servers; the eighth kept the old code. Nobody verified that all servers matched.
  4. Market open. Orders for the new program carried the flag. On the seven updated servers it meant the new behaviour. On the eighth it activated Power Peg, which did not know when an order was already filled and kept sending child orders.
  5. Flood. The firm sent a large volume of unintended orders in the first 45 minutes. Warning messages had been generated earlier but were not acted on and there was no clear kill switch or runbook.
  6. Making it worse. Engineers rolled back the new code on the seven good servers, which pushed all eight into the old flag-triggered behaviour and increased the damage.
  7. Result. A loss of roughly 440 million dollars in about 45 minutes, which nearly ended the company; it was later acquired.

Lessons.

  • Automate deployments and verify every host runs the same version.
  • Remove dead code and never repurpose an old flag for a new meaning.
  • Pre-trade risk limits and kill switches independent of the strategy code (exposure caps, order-rate caps).
  • Act on early alerts: clear ownership and runbooks.
  • Rolling back a partial deploy can compound damage. Know the right action beforehand.
GitLab (2017) database deletion and backup failures: what happened? Senior
Say: "A tired engineer deleted the primary's data directory instead of the secondary's, and every backup path turned out to be broken. A backup never restored is not a backup, and replication is not backup."

Timeline (31 January 2017, per GitLab's public post-mortem as I recall it).

  1. Load spike. A spam-driven spike (and a deletion job for an abusive user) loaded the PostgreSQL primary. Replication to the secondary fell behind and then stopped.
  2. Resync attempt. Late at night, an engineer, trying to rebuild the secondary, tried several fixes, and eventually decided to wipe the secondary's data directory so it could be re-seeded.
  3. Wrong host. They ran the delete (rm -rf) in a terminal that was connected to the primary, not the secondary. They noticed after a few seconds, but most of roughly 300 GB was already removed.
  4. Backup discovery. The team then found that their backups did not work as assumed: regular dumps had been failing silently (a PostgreSQL version mismatch in the dump tool, and the failure emails were not delivered or not seen), disk snapshots were not enabled for that database host, and replication, the other copy, was the thing that had just broken.
  5. Lucky snapshot. A snapshot taken manually about six hours earlier (for refreshing a staging environment) existed, and was used for recovery. Restoring from it was slow, partly because of slow disks.
  6. Result. About six hours of database data were lost (issues, merge requests, comments, not git repositories) and the site was down for many hours. GitLab publicly posted notes live and published a candid post-mortem.

(Exact numbers such as the number of backup methods and hours are as I remember them; do not quote them as exact.)

Lessons.

  • Test restores regularly and make a failed backup page someone.
  • Destructive commands on production need guard rails: a clear prompt showing the host, different credentials or colours for production, a second pair of eyes.
  • Avoid risky maintenance while exhausted; incident hygiene and handover matter.
  • Replication is not backup: it copies deletions too.
  • Transparency builds customer trust.
Google authentication outage (December 2020): what happened? Senior
Say: "Quota automation believed the identity service was using no resources and shrank its quota to near zero, so authentication failed everywhere. Lessons: sanity bounds on automation, protect shared identity services, and keep sessions working briefly without the auth backend."

Background. Google's central identity system issues and checks logins for most of its products. A quota-management system automatically sets how much capacity each backend service may use, based on reported usage.

Timeline (December 2020, per Google's incident report as I remember it).

  1. Migration. Google was moving the identity service's user-ID component to a new quota system.
  2. Bad input. During the migration the usage reported to the quota system for that service was effectively zero.
  3. Automation acts. Seeing "zero usage", the automation reduced the service's allotted quota to near zero. A safeguard that should have blocked a drastic reduction did not apply because of how the migration was handled.
  4. Failure. The authentication backend started rejecting requests for lack of quota. Any product needing authentication (Gmail, YouTube, Drive and others) failed for users who needed fresh authentication.
  5. Recovery. Engineers identified the quota problem and restored the quota; service returned after roughly 45 minutes.

Lessons.

  • Automation that can shrink a critical resource needs sanity bounds: never reduce below a floor, reject a sudden drop to zero, and require a human for drastic changes.
  • Shared identity and quota services are single points of failure for everything; give them extra protection and independence.
  • Staged rollout of such changes, and alerts when recorded usage disagrees with real usage.
  • Graceful degradation: let existing authenticated sessions keep working for a while without calling the backend.
Roblox outage (October 2021): what happened? Senior
Say: "Their Consul service-discovery cluster degraded under load, and because nearly every service depended on it, and recovery needed it too, the platform was down for about three days. Lesson: treat core infrastructure as tier zero and avoid circular recovery dependencies."

Background. Roblox ran its platform on HashiCorp Nomad and used Consul for service discovery, health checks and a key-value store. Nearly every service looks others up through Consul.

Timeline (late October 2021, per Roblox's post-mortem as I recall it; numbers approximate).

  1. Contributing change. Roblox had enabled a newer Consul feature (streaming for the cluster's read path). Under unusually high read and write load, this led to heavy contention on a single channel.
  2. Second problem. Consul's storage layer, BoltDB, suffered pathological performance after heavy write churn (a very large freelist), so writes became slow.
  3. Platform-wide impact. Because services could not reliably discover each other or read config, the whole platform degraded and then went down, for around 73 hours in total.
  4. Hard recovery. The systems needed to bring things back up depended on the thing that was broken. Engineers had to rebuild or reconfigure the Consul cluster (including moving to different hardware and disabling the streaming feature), while limited telemetry (also dependent on Consul) made diagnosis hard. The platform was restored gradually.

Lessons.

  • Core infrastructure (service discovery, config, secrets) is a dependency of everything: treat it as tier zero with its own capacity planning, isolation, and rollback.
  • Avoid circular dependencies in the recovery path: the tools to fix it should not need the broken thing.
  • Roll out new features of the most critical components gradually and load test at real scale.
  • Observability must not depend on the failing system; a hard-to-diagnose failure with poor telemetry can last a very long time.
Dyn DNS DDoS (October 2016): what happened? Intermediate
Say: "A huge DDoS, attributed to the Mirai IoT botnet, hit the DNS provider Dyn, so customers who relied only on Dyn could not be resolved. Lesson: do not rely on one DNS provider, and your third-party dependencies are part of your availability."

Background. A domain's authoritative DNS provider answers "what IP is twitter.com?". If it is unreachable, resolvers eventually cannot get an answer and users cannot reach the site, even though the site's servers are running perfectly.

Timeline (21 October 2016).

  1. Botnet. Attackers had compromised many internet-of-things devices (cameras, DVRs, routers) that still used default passwords, forming the Mirai botnet.
  2. Attack waves. They directed floods of DNS traffic at Dyn's infrastructure in several waves through the day.
  3. Impact. Dyn was overwhelmed in some regions, so lookups for its customers failed or were slow. Sites such as Twitter, Netflix, Reddit, GitHub and others became unreachable for many users, particularly in North America and parts of Europe.
  4. Recovery. Dyn mitigated over several hours. Customers who used a second DNS provider could shift or fail over; those with long TTLs benefited from resolver caches.

Lessons.

  • Use multiple authoritative DNS providers for critical domains.
  • DNS TTLs and resolver caching: longer TTLs let some customers ride through the outage (the trade-off is slower changes).
  • Third-party dependencies you do not see in the architecture diagram are part of your availability.
  • Ecosystem security (weak default passwords on IoT devices) is a systemic risk that no single company controls.
Heartbleed (2014): what happened and what does it teach as a systemic lesson? Intermediate
Say: "A missing bounds check in OpenSSL's heartbeat code let anyone read up to about 64 KB of server memory per request, possibly including private keys. Lessons: patch, then revoke and reissue certs and rotate secrets, fund critical open source, and keep a bill of materials."

Background. TLS heartbeat is a small feature letting one side check the other is alive: the client sends a payload and says how long it is, and the server echoes it back.

How the bug worked.

  1. A client sends a heartbeat with a 1-byte payload but claims the length is 64,000 bytes.
  2. The vulnerable server trusted the claimed length and copied 64,000 bytes starting at the payload, which included whatever happened to sit next to it in memory.
  3. It returned them. Adjacent memory could include private keys, session cookies, passwords, and other users' data.
  4. The attacker repeats requests and collects different chunks, leaving little trace in the logs.

The code was written in C, and the fix was a one-line bounds check: a classic buffer over-read. It was disclosed in April 2014 and affected a large share of HTTPS servers using certain OpenSSL versions.

Why the response was big. Because the leak leaves no evidence, you cannot know whether your keys were stolen. So the response had to be broad: patch, then revoke and reissue certificates, rotate keys and invalidate sessions and passwords.

Lessons.

  • Critical open-source infrastructure was underfunded and under-reviewed; this led to funding initiatives.
  • Memory-unsafe languages make this class of bug easy; consider memory-safe languages and fuzzing for security-critical parsers.
  • Keep a software bill of materials so you can answer "where do we use this library?" in minutes. The same question returned with Log4Shell in 2021.
  • Forward secrecy limits how much past recorded traffic an attacker can decrypt after a key leaks.
CrowdStrike (July 2024): what happened? Senior
Say: "A data-only content update caused an out-of-bounds read in kernel-level sensor code, crashing Windows machines at boot, and it was released to everyone at once. Lessons: stage every change including content, make boot-time code defensive, and avoid concentration risk."

Background. CrowdStrike's Falcon sensor runs as a kernel-level driver on Windows machines for security monitoring. It loads at boot, and receives frequent "rapid response content" updates (data files that tell it what to detect) separately from the sensor software itself. Such content updates were not subject to the staged rollout used for sensor releases.

Timeline (19 July 2024, per CrowdStrike's published analysis as I remember it).

  1. Mismatch. A new template type defined 21 input fields, but the sensor's integration code supplied only 20. This mismatch was not caught by earlier testing and validation.
  2. Release. A content update that used the 21st field was pushed to all online customer machines at once, in the early hours UTC.
  3. Crash. When the content interpreter evaluated it, the code read outside the bounds of an array, in kernel mode. A kernel crash means a blue screen.
  4. Boot loop. The faulty file loaded again at every boot, so the machine crashed repeatedly and could not be fixed remotely.
  5. Mitigation. CrowdStrike withdrew the update within roughly an hour, but machines that had already crashed stayed down. Fixing them often needed hands-on work: booting into safe mode or recovery and deleting a specific file, with disk encryption keys (BitLocker) complicating it.
  6. Impact. Airlines, hospitals, banks, broadcasters and others were disrupted worldwide for days.

Lessons.

  • Staged (canary) rollouts for every change, including data or content, with the ability to halt. CrowdStrike afterwards committed to staged deployment and gave customers more control over update timing.
  • Code that runs in the kernel and loads at boot must be extremely defensive: a crash makes the machine unbootable. Fail safe by skipping bad content.
  • Validate content with the same rigour as code, including fuzzing and tests across real configurations.
  • Recovery must be possible at scale without hands-on steps per machine.
  • Concentration risk: one vendor's agent on most of your fleet is a single point of failure.

Cross-cutting lessons from incidents

What patterns repeat across most major outages? Senior
Say: "Incidents are rarely one cause. There is a trigger (the typo, the regex), and several latent conditions (no limits, no canary, untested recovery) that turned it into an outage."

Why look for patterns. Studying each outage as a one-off teaches trivia. Seeing the same seven shapes repeat teaches you what to check in your design review. Ask of any system: "which of these could happen to us?"

The common fix is the same: push to a small slice first, watch health, and stop automatically if it degrades. The fix is independent of the technology involved.

Triggers versus latent conditions. The trigger is what people remember (a mistyped input). The latent conditions are what made it an outage: the tool allowed removing too much, there was no minimum capacity, nobody had restarted the system at its current scale. Removing the trigger (training people not to typo) is hopeless; removing latent conditions is engineering.

Interview use. When you finish a design, add one sentence: "the failure I worry about most is X, and the guard is Y".

How do chaos engineering and game days reduce the chance of these incidents? Intermediate
Say: "They find hidden dependencies, untested restores and unclear ownership before a real incident does, by injecting controlled failure and rehearsing the humans and runbooks."

Chaos engineering means deliberately injecting failure into a running system in a controlled way (kill instances, add latency, drop a dependency, cut a zone) to verify that it degrades as designed. It tests the system.

Game days rehearse the people and procedures: fail over a region, restore from backup, rotate a leaked key, run a mock incident with a time limit and an incident commander. They test runbooks, access and communication.

Why they work. Many bad incidents involved something nobody had ever tried: GitLab's untested restore, S3's cold restart, Facebook's recovery without the network. These exercises surface that kind of gap on a calm Tuesday, instead of during an outage.

How to run one safely, step by step.

  1. Define a steady-state metric (for example, checkout success rate above 99.9 percent).
  2. Form a hypothesis: "if one availability zone goes down, the metric stays within bounds".
  3. Choose a small blast radius and an abort condition (stop if the metric falls below X).
  4. Start in staging, then move to production gradually, during working hours with people watching.
  5. Inject the failure, observe, fix what you learn, and re-run.

Examples of experiments. Kill a random instance; add 300 ms latency to a dependency; block access to the database from one service; expire a test certificate; actually restore last night's backup to a scratch environment and compare.

Pitfall. Chaos without observability just breaks things without learning. Fix the known weaknesses first, and always have an abort switch.

Which design principles would you list as defences against whole-platform outages? Senior
Say: "Limit blast radius, roll out progressively, fail fast and degrade gracefully, keep the data plane independent of the control plane, have an independent recovery path, observe from outside, and fix the human factors."

Each principle answers a pattern from the previous table. Here is what each means in practice.

  • Limit blast radius. Split the system into cells, shards, regions and per-tenant slices so a failure hits a small share. Shuffle sharding gives each customer a random small subset of workers, so a poisonous customer takes out only the few others who share that subset.
  • Progressive delivery for code, config and data: 1 percent, then 10, then 100, with automated health gates and automatic rollback.
  • Fail fast and degrade gracefully. Timeouts, circuit breakers, bulkheads, load shedding and fallbacks mean one broken part reduces functionality rather than killing everything.
  • Static stability. The data plane (the part serving customer traffic) keeps working when the control plane (the part that changes configuration) is down. Example: a proxy that continues routing with its last known config if the config service is unreachable.
  • Independent recovery path. Out-of-band access, tested backups, rehearsed rollback, and recovery tooling that does not need the broken system.
  • Observability. SLO alerting, traces, per-tenant metrics, and synthetic probes from outside your own network.
  • Human factors. Blameless culture, guard rails on dangerous commands (confirmation, limits, clear environment labels), clear on-call ownership, runbooks and practice.

Trade-off. Each principle costs money, complexity or speed. Cells and multi-region cost extra capacity; progressive rollouts slow releases. The decision rule: spend most on the components whose failure would take everything with it (identity, DNS, discovery, deployment, config), and be pragmatic elsewhere.
