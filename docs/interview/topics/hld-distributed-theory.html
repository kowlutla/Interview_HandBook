How to use this page

Each question opens to reveal a model answer. Try to answer out loud first, in about 60 seconds, then open it. Questions are tagged Basic, Intermediate or Senior. In an interview, the pattern that impresses is: state the principle, give a concrete failure example, then name the trade-off you would accept. The theory here is the vocabulary you use when defending any design. Where a number or a product detail varies by version, the answer says so.

1. Why distributed systems are hard

Why is a distributed system fundamentally harder than a single-machine program? Basic

On one machine, a function call either returns or the whole process dies. Across machines you get three extra problems that cannot be removed:

  • Partial failure. Some parts fail while others keep running. The caller often cannot tell what happened.
  • Unreliable networks. Messages can be lost, delayed, duplicated or reordered. There is no upper bound on delay in a real network.
  • No shared clock and no shared memory. Each node only knows its own state and what it has been told, which may already be stale.

Most of distributed systems theory is the set of techniques (replication, consensus, timeouts, retries, idempotency) for building something reliable out of unreliable parts.

What is partial failure, and why is it the root of most distributed bugs? Basic

Partial failure means part of the system is broken while the rest works. The nasty part is ambiguity. You send a payment request and get a timeout. Three worlds are possible and look identical to you:

  1. The request never arrived.
  2. The server processed it, but the response was lost.
  3. The server is still processing it, slowly.

If you retry blindly, in worlds 2 and 3 you charge twice. If you give up, in world 2 you lose money or state. The only safe answer is to design operations so that retrying is harmless (idempotency) or so that you can ask "what happened to request X?" later.

Say: "A timeout does not mean failure. It means I do not know. So every write that crosses a network needs an idempotency key or a way to query its outcome."
What are the fallacies of distributed computing? Basic

A list of eight false assumptions, originally from Peter Deutsch and colleagues at Sun (with the eighth added by James Gosling). Each one produces real bugs.

What is the Two Generals problem, and what does it tell us about acknowledgements? Intermediate

Two armies must attack at the same time, communicating only through messengers who may be captured. General A sends "attack at dawn". To be sure, A needs an acknowledgement from B. But B now needs to know the ack arrived, so A must ack the ack, and so on forever. No finite number of messages gives both generals certainty.

Lesson: over an unreliable channel you cannot get common knowledge with certainty. This is why exactly-once delivery as a network property is impossible, and why protocols settle for "at-least-once plus idempotent receiver". It is also why TCP can only promise that the data was delivered to the peer's kernel, not that the application processed it.

What does the FLP impossibility result say, and does it mean consensus is impossible in practice? Senior

Fischer, Lynch and Paterson (1985) proved that in a purely asynchronous system (no bound on message delay or relative speed), no deterministic algorithm can guarantee consensus if even one node may crash. The reason: you cannot distinguish a crashed node from a very slow one.

Practical systems escape it by relaxing an assumption. Paxos and Raft keep safety (never decide two different values) always, but only guarantee liveness (making progress) when the network is "well behaved enough" for a while. They use timeouts and randomized election delays, which is a form of partial synchrony. So consensus works in practice, but it can stall during bad periods, for example during repeated leader elections under heavy network jitter.

What is the difference between synchronous, asynchronous and partially synchronous system models? Intermediate
  • Synchronous: known upper bounds on message delay, processing time and clock drift. Failure detection is then perfect. Real networks do not give this.
  • Asynchronous: no bounds at all. Very pessimistic, FLP applies.
  • Partially synchronous: the system behaves synchronously most of the time, but bounds can be violated for stretches (GC pauses, congestion, VM stalls). This is the realistic model and the one Raft, Paxos and most production systems assume.

A senior-level point: design for safety under asynchrony and get liveness from partial synchrony.

What is a gray failure, and why is it worse than a clean crash? Senior

A gray failure is when a node is neither fully up nor fully down: it responds to health checks but is very slow, drops some requests, has a failing disk, or has a congested NIC. Health checks say "healthy" while users see errors.

Clean crashes are easy: the node disappears, a failure detector notices, traffic moves. Gray failures slip past binary detection, and because they cause timeouts and retries they can spread load and trigger cascading failure. Mitigations: health checks that exercise real paths, latency-based (not just up/down) outlier ejection, hedged requests, circuit breakers, and accrual-style detectors that output suspicion levels instead of a boolean.

How should timeouts and retries be designed, and what can go wrong? Intermediate
  • Every remote call needs a timeout. Without one, a slow dependency ties up threads and connections and takes you down with it.
  • Retry only idempotent operations, or attach an idempotency key.
  • Use exponential backoff with jitter, otherwise all clients retry in sync and cause a retry storm.
  • Cap total attempts and use a retry budget (for example, retries may add at most 10 percent extra load), so retries cannot multiply load through several layers. Three layers each retrying 3 times means up to 27 attempts at the bottom.
  • Propagate a deadline so downstream work stops when the caller has already given up.

2. CAP and PACELC

State the CAP theorem precisely. Basic

Eric Brewer conjectured it in 2000; Gilbert and Lynch proved a formal version in 2002. For a replicated data store, you cannot simultaneously guarantee all three of:

  • Consistency (C): linearizability. Every read sees the most recent completed write, as if there were one copy.
  • Availability (A): every request to a non-failing node gets a non-error response (no promise it is the latest data).
  • Partition tolerance (P): the system keeps working when the network drops or delays messages between nodes.

The real meaning: when a network partition happens, you must choose between C and A. Since partitions cannot be ruled out in a distributed system, "pick two of three" is misleading. It is "if there is a partition, pick C or A".

Walk through CAP with a concrete example of two nodes. Basic

Two replicas, N1 in Mumbai and N2 in Frankfurt, both hold x = 1. The link between them breaks. A client writes x = 2 to N1. Then another client reads x from N2.

  • Choose C (CP): N2 cannot confirm it has the latest value, so it refuses or times out the read (or N1 refuses the write without a quorum). Correct but unavailable for some requests.
  • Choose A (AP): N2 answers with x = 1. Available, but stale. N1 accepts the write, so replicas diverge and need reconciliation after the partition heals.

When there is no partition, you can have both.

What are the most common CAP misconceptions? Intermediate
  1. "Pick any two." You cannot drop P. A "CA system" only makes sense for a single node or a network you assume never partitions.
  2. "CAP says the system is either consistent or available all the time." The trade-off only bites during a partition, and it can be made per operation (a bank may reject a transfer but allow balance reads).
  3. CAP's C is not ACID's C. CAP consistency means linearizability. ACID consistency means invariants of the application hold.
  4. CAP's A is a strict definition. Every non-failing node must answer. A system that is "up 99.99 percent" in practice is not necessarily "available" in CAP's sense, and a CP system can still be highly available as long as a majority is reachable.
  5. It says nothing about latency and nothing about normal operation, which is why PACELC exists.
  6. Labels are not absolute. Many databases are configurable per query (consistency levels), so "Cassandra is AP" is a default tendency, not a law.
Trap: saying "MongoDB is CP, Cassandra is AP" as if it were fixed. Say "by default, and tunable".

What is PACELC and why is it a better model than CAP? Intermediate

Daniel Abadi (2012) extended CAP: if there is a Partition (P), choose Availability or Consistency (A or C); Else (E), choose Latency or Consistency (L or C).

The key insight is the "else" half. Even with a healthy network, replicating synchronously to get strong consistency costs round trips (latency), while replying after one local write is fast but can be stale. This trade-off exists all the time, whereas partitions are rare.

Is latency part of CAP? How does "slow" relate to "unavailable"? Senior

Formally no, CAP's availability just says "eventually returns a response". In practice a response that arrives after the client timeout is indistinguishable from a partition. Many real "partitions" are just very slow links or paused nodes. This is why Abadi added latency to the model, and why practitioners talk about consistency versus latency day to day (for example choosing quorum reads versus reading from the nearest replica).

Is a single-leader relational database with async replicas CP or AP? Senior

It depends on what you read. Writes and reads that go to the leader are linearizable, so on the leader's side it behaves CP, and if the leader is cut off from clients, those clients get errors. But reads from async replicas can be stale (lag), so reading from replicas is not linearizable even without any partition. And if failover promotes an async replica, acknowledged writes can be lost. So "it is CP" is too coarse. The honest answer: strong on the leader, eventually consistent on followers, and failover semantics depend on sync versus async replication.

3. Consistency models

Explain the spectrum of consistency models from strongest to weakest. Intermediate

What exactly is linearizability, with an example of a violation? Intermediate

Linearizability is a recency guarantee on a single object: once a write completes, every later read, from anyone, sees it or something newer. The system acts as if there is one copy and each operation takes effect atomically at some instant between its start and end.

Violation: Alice updates her profile photo and the call returns success. Alice then tells Bob over the phone. Bob refreshes and sees the old photo, because his read hit a lagging replica. Real-time order (write finished before Bob's read began) was not respected. That is allowed in eventual consistency but not in linearizability.

Where it is truly needed: leader election, distributed locks, unique constraints (one username per account), and anywhere two parties must agree on a single current value.

What is the difference between linearizability and serializability? Senior
  • Linearizability is about single operations on single objects and real-time recency.
  • Serializability is about multi-object transactions: the result equals some serial order of the transactions. That order need not match real time.

A database can be serializable but not linearizable (a transaction may appear to execute "earlier" than one that finished before it started). Strict serializability combines both and is what Spanner provides ("external consistency"). Snapshot isolation is neither, and allows write skew.

How does sequential consistency differ from linearizability? Senior

Both require one global order that every process agrees on and that preserves each process's own order. Linearizability additionally requires that the order respect real time: if operation A finished before B started, A comes first. Sequential consistency lets the system reorder across clients as long as each client's own order is kept. Example: client 1 writes x = 1 and finishes; client 2 then reads x and gets 0. Sequentially consistent is acceptable (the read is placed "before" the write in the global order); linearizability forbids it.

What is causal consistency and why is it attractive? Intermediate

Operations related by "happens-before" are seen in that order by all nodes. The classic example is a comment thread: a reply must never appear before the post it answers. Unrelated posts may appear in any order.

It is attractive because it is the strongest model that can still be provided with high availability under partitions (research results show this), and it matches human expectation of cause and effect. It is implemented with dependency tracking, version vectors or hybrid logical clocks. Some systems offer it (for example MongoDB causally consistent sessions, COPS research systems).

What is read-your-writes consistency and how do you implement it cheaply? Basic

After you write something, you always see it in your own later reads, even if other users might still see the old value. It prevents the "I saved but it vanished" experience.

Implementation options:

  • Read from the leader for data the user just modified, or for a short window (say 10 seconds) after a write.
  • Sticky sessions to one replica.
  • Return a version, LSN or timestamp token with the write; the client sends it on reads and the replica waits until it has caught up to that token.
  • Write-through to a per-user cache.
Trap: with multiple devices (phone and laptop) a session-based approach fails unless the token or leader-read rule is per user, not per connection.
Name the four session guarantees and what each prevents. Intermediate
  • Read your writes: you see your own updates.
  • Monotonic reads: once you have seen a value, you never see an older one later (prevents data "going back in time" when you hit different replicas).
  • Monotonic writes: your writes are applied in the order you issued them.
  • Writes follow reads: a write you issue after reading X is ordered after the write that produced X (so a reply is ordered after the post).
What does "eventual consistency" really promise, and what does it not? Basic

It promises only convergence: if no new updates occur, all replicas eventually hold the same value. It does not say how long "eventually" is (usually milliseconds to seconds, but unbounded in theory), what you read in the meantime, or which value wins when concurrent writes conflict. You must add a conflict resolution rule (last-write-wins, version vectors, CRDTs, application merge).

Good for: counters, feeds, DNS, caches, shopping carts. Bad for: anything where reading a stale value causes a wrong irreversible action (double spend, overselling the last seat).

How do quorum reads and writes (R + W > N) work, and do they guarantee linearizability? Senior

With N replicas, write to W and read from R. If R + W > N, every read set overlaps with every write set in at least one replica, so a read sees the latest acknowledged write (when the replica with the highest version is chosen). Example N = 3, W = 2, R = 2. For latency-sensitive reads you can use W = N, R = 1; for write-heavy, W = 1, R = N.

It does not automatically give linearizability. Known gaps:

  • A write that fails after reaching only some replicas leaves partial state that later reads may or may not return.
  • Sloppy quorums and hinted handoff write to nodes outside the usual replica set, so the overlap guarantee breaks.
  • Last-write-wins with skewed clocks can pick the wrong winner.
  • Concurrent read-then-write races: two clients can both read and overwrite.

Dynamo-style stores therefore need read repair and anti-entropy, and linearizable operations (such as Cassandra lightweight transactions) use Paxos on top.

What is tunable consistency? Give an example. Intermediate

The client chooses consistency per operation. In Cassandra, with replication factor 3, writing at QUORUM (2 replicas) and reading at QUORUM gives overlap, while ONE gives low latency but possible staleness, and LOCAL_QUORUM keeps the quorum inside one datacenter to avoid cross-region latency. DynamoDB offers eventually consistent reads (default, cheaper) and strongly consistent reads (served from the leader of the partition, double the read cost, not available on global secondary indexes). This is a practical form of the PACELC trade-off.

4. Consensus: Paxos, Raft, quorums

What is consensus and why do we need it? Basic

Consensus is getting a group of nodes to agree on a single value (or an ordered sequence of values) even when some nodes crash and messages are delayed. Formal properties: agreement (no two nodes decide differently), validity (the decided value was proposed by someone), termination (every live node eventually decides).

It underlies: electing a single leader, replicating a log so all replicas apply the same commands in the same order (replicated state machine), atomic commit, and configuration or membership changes. Anywhere you need "exactly one winner" without a single point of failure, you need consensus.

What is a replicated state machine? Intermediate

If several nodes start from the same state and apply the same deterministic commands in the same order, they end in the same state. So to replicate any service (a key-value store, a lock service, a metadata database) you only need to agree on the order of the log of commands. A consensus protocol (Raft, Multi-Paxos, Zab) keeps that log identical on a majority of nodes; each node then applies committed entries to its local state machine. Determinism matters: commands must not read the local clock or random numbers differently on each node.

What is a quorum, and why does it need to be a majority? Basic

A quorum is the minimum number of nodes whose agreement makes a decision valid. For consensus it is a majority: floor(N / 2) + 1. Any two majorities of the same group must share at least one node, so two conflicting decisions can never both get a quorum. That shared node "remembers" the earlier decision.

Why do consensus clusters use an odd number of nodes? Basic

Because an even number adds cost without adding fault tolerance. Four nodes need a quorum of 3, so they tolerate only 1 failure, the same as three nodes. Five nodes tolerate 2. Even sizes also make a clean split more likely: with 4 nodes, a 2-2 partition leaves no side with a majority, so the whole cluster stops. With 5 nodes, any split leaves one side with at least 3.

Also, more nodes means slower writes (more acks, more traffic), so typical sizes are 3 or 5; etcd documentation suggests that more than 7 gives diminishing returns. If you need more read capacity, add non-voting learners or read replicas rather than voters.

Explain Paxos at a conceptual level. Senior

Single-decree Paxos (Lamport) decides one value. Roles: proposers, acceptors, learners (often the same nodes).

  1. Phase 1 (prepare/promise): a proposer picks a unique, increasing proposal number n and asks a majority of acceptors to promise not to accept any proposal numbered below n. Each acceptor replies with the highest-numbered proposal it has already accepted, if any.
  2. Phase 2 (accept/accepted): the proposer must propose the value of the highest-numbered accepted proposal reported back; if none, it may use its own value. It sends accept(n, value). Acceptors accept unless they promised a higher number. When a majority accepts, the value is chosen.

Why it is safe: majorities overlap, so a proposer in phase 1 always learns about any value that might already have been chosen and must adopt it. Why it can stall: two proposers can keep pre-empting each other (dueling proposers), so practical systems elect a stable leader.

Multi-Paxos runs many instances for a log, and with a stable leader skips phase 1 for subsequent entries so each command costs one round trip to a majority. Paxos is famously hard to implement fully, which motivated Raft.

Explain how Raft works. Intermediate

Raft (Ongaro and Ousterhout, 2014) was designed to be understandable. It splits consensus into three parts: leader election, log replication, and safety. Every node is a follower, candidate or leader. Time is divided into numbered terms; each term has at most one leader.

  • The leader receives client commands, appends them to its log, replicates them with AppendEntries RPCs, and marks an entry committed once a majority has stored it. It then applies it and replies to the client.
  • Followers only accept entries from the current leader. Heartbeats (empty AppendEntries) keep followers from starting an election.
  • If a follower hears nothing for a randomized election timeout (the paper suggests about 150 to 300 ms), it becomes a candidate and starts an election.
How does Raft leader election work, and how are split votes avoided? Intermediate
  1. A follower's election timer fires. It increments its term, votes for itself, and sends RequestVote to all others.
  2. Each node votes for at most one candidate per term, first come first served, and only if the candidate's log is at least as up to date as its own (compared by last log term, then last log index).
  3. A candidate with votes from a majority becomes leader and immediately sends heartbeats.
  4. If nobody wins (split vote), the timers expire again and a new term starts.

Randomizing the election timeout makes it unlikely that two nodes time out at the same moment, so split votes are rare and resolve in a round or two. Many implementations add a pre-vote phase so a node that was just partitioned away does not bump the term and disrupt a healthy leader when it rejoins.

What is a term (epoch) and why is it important? Intermediate

A term is a monotonically increasing number acting as a logical clock for leadership. Each message carries the sender's term. A node that sees a higher term updates its own and steps down if it was leader or candidate. A message with a stale term is rejected. This makes an old leader that was paused or partitioned harmless: when it comes back, its term is outdated and followers ignore its writes. The same idea appears elsewhere as epoch numbers, generation IDs and fencing tokens.

How does Raft replicate the log, and what happens when follower logs differ? Senior

Each AppendEntries carries the index and term of the entry just before the new ones. A follower accepts only if it has a matching entry at that position (the log matching property). If not, it rejects, and the leader steps back its nextIndex for that follower until it finds the point where the logs agree, then overwrites the follower's conflicting suffix with its own entries.

Entries that were never committed can safely be discarded, because no client was told they succeeded. Raft guarantees leader completeness: any elected leader contains all committed entries, because the voting rule only elects candidates with an up-to-date log, and any majority that voted overlaps with the majority that committed.

A subtle rule: a leader only commits entries from earlier terms indirectly, by committing a new entry from its own term on top of them. This avoids a known scenario where an old-term entry stored on a majority could still be overwritten.

How can a Raft cluster serve linearizable reads without writing to the log every time? Senior

Reading from any follower may be stale. Reading from the leader alone is also unsafe, since a deposed leader may not know it was replaced. Two standard fixes:

  • ReadIndex: the leader notes the current commit index, confirms it is still leader by exchanging heartbeats with a majority, waits until it has applied up to that index, then answers.
  • Leader leases: the leader assumes it is leader for a time window after a majority heartbeat. Faster, but relies on bounded clock drift, so it is less safe if clocks misbehave.

etcd offers linearizable reads (default) and serializable reads (served locally, possibly stale, faster).

What other consensus-like protocols exist (Zab, Viewstamped Replication, EPaxos)? Senior
  • Zab (ZooKeeper Atomic Broadcast): leader-based total-order broadcast, very close in spirit to Multi-Paxos and Raft.
  • Viewstamped Replication: a leader-based protocol from 1988, a direct ancestor of Raft's ideas.
  • EPaxos: leaderless; non-conflicting commands commit in one round trip from any replica, useful for geo-distributed low-latency writes, but more complex.
  • PBFT and its descendants: tolerate Byzantine nodes (see the faults section).
What are the dangers of changing cluster membership, and how does Raft handle them? Senior

If you switch a 3-node cluster to 5 nodes instantly, different nodes may see different configurations at the same moment, so two disjoint "majorities" could exist and elect two leaders in the same term. Raft avoids it either with joint consensus (a transitional configuration requiring majorities of both old and new sets) or with single-server changes (add or remove one node at a time, since majorities of N and N+1 always overlap). Operationally: add a new node as a non-voting learner first, let it catch up, then promote it. Never remove a node and add another at the same time without a safe procedure.

What are the performance limits of consensus-based systems? Senior
  • Every write needs a round trip to a majority plus a durable log flush (fsync), so write latency is bounded by disk and the slowest of the fastest-majority nodes.
  • All writes go through one leader, so throughput does not scale by adding nodes. Scale by sharding into many independent consensus groups (as Spanner, CockroachDB and TiKV do).
  • Across regions, a majority round trip can mean 50 to 150 ms. Designs place the quorum in nearby zones, or keep a leader close to writers.
  • Batching and pipelining entries amortize cost.

5. ZooKeeper and etcd

What do ZooKeeper and etcd do, and why not just use a database? Basic

They are small, strongly consistent, replicated stores for coordination metadata: configuration, service registration, leader election, locks, membership. They run consensus (ZooKeeper uses Zab; etcd uses Raft) and offer primitives that a plain database lacks: watches (get notified when a key changes), ephemeral entries tied to a session or lease (auto-deleted if the owner dies), and ordered, linearizable updates.

They are not general-purpose databases: data is small (etcd has a default storage limit of about 2 GB, recommended up to about 8 GB; ZooKeeper znodes are limited to about 1 MB), kept in memory or small stores, and write throughput is limited by consensus.

What are ZooKeeper's core primitives, and how do you build leader election with them? Intermediate

Data lives in a tree of znodes. Types: persistent, ephemeral (deleted when the client session ends), and sequential (a monotonically increasing suffix is appended). Clients can set watches, one-time notifications on change.

Leader election recipe: each candidate creates an ephemeral sequential znode under /election. The one with the lowest sequence number is leader. Each other candidate watches only the znode immediately before its own (not the leader), so when the leader dies, just one candidate is woken up. This avoids the thundering herd. If the leader's session expires (it crashed or was cut off), its znode vanishes and the next in line takes over.

What are etcd's key features, and who depends on it? Intermediate
  • Key-value store with a flat key space and range reads, multi-version (every change gets a revision, so you can read history and watch from a revision).
  • Leases (TTL): keys attached to a lease disappear when the lease expires without keep-alives. Used for liveness and leader election.
  • Transactions (compare-and-swap style): "if key has version v, then put, else get".
  • Watch streams of changes.

Kubernetes stores all cluster state in etcd; the API server is essentially the only client. If etcd is slow or loses quorum, the cluster cannot change (running pods keep running, but no scheduling or updates).

What are common operational pitfalls with ZooKeeper or etcd? Senior

6. Clocks, time and ordering

Why can't we just use timestamps to order events in a distributed system? Basic

Each machine has its own quartz clock which drifts (commonly tens of parts per million, so a few seconds per day uncorrected) and is periodically corrected by NTP, sometimes jumping backward. Two events on different machines with timestamps 10:00:00.005 and 10:00:00.003 may actually have occurred in the opposite order. Anything that depends on comparing timestamps from different nodes (last-write-wins, lock expiry, cache TTL checked on another host) can break silently.

Say: "I use wall clocks for humans and logs, monotonic clocks for measuring durations on one machine, and logical or hybrid clocks for ordering across machines."
What is the difference between a wall-clock (time-of-day) clock and a monotonic clock? Intermediate
  • Wall clock (for example System.currentTimeMillis, clock_gettime REALTIME): tells the date and time; can jump forward or backward when NTP corrects it, at leap seconds, or if an admin changes it.
  • Monotonic clock (System.nanoTime, CLOCK_MONOTONIC): only moves forward, good for measuring elapsed time, timeouts and rate limits; its absolute value is meaningless and not comparable across machines or restarts.

Common bug: computing "elapsed = now() - start" with a wall clock, then seeing negative or huge durations after a clock step.

What is NTP, how accurate is it, and what can go wrong? Intermediate

Network Time Protocol synchronizes a machine's clock with hierarchical time servers by measuring round-trip delay. Typical accuracy: about 1 ms or better on a good LAN with a local server, and tens of milliseconds (sometimes more) over the public internet. It can be worse during network congestion or asymmetric routes.

Problems: clock steps (backward jumps) when the offset is large, VM pause and resume or live migration causing sudden skew, misconfigured servers, and leap seconds (an extra second inserted at the end of a UTC day). Leap seconds have caused real outages; for example, around the 2012 leap second many Linux hosts hung Java and database processes, and in the 2016 to 2017 leap second Cloudflare saw a bug where a negative time difference crashed parts of its DNS code (as publicly reported). Cloud providers often use "leap smearing", spreading the extra second over many hours to avoid a jump.

How do Lamport logical clocks work, and what do they guarantee? Intermediate

Each process keeps an integer counter L.

  1. Before each local event or send, L = L + 1.
  2. A message carries the sender's L.
  3. On receiving a message with timestamp T, L = max(L, T) + 1.

Guarantee: if event a happened-before b (same process order or via messages), then L(a) < L(b). The converse is false: L(a) < L(b) does not mean a caused b; they may be concurrent. Ties between processes are broken by process ID to make a total order. Lamport clocks give a consistent total order consistent with causality, but cannot tell you whether two events are concurrent.

How do vector clocks work, and how do they detect concurrency? Senior

Each process keeps a vector with one counter per process. On a local event, increment your own entry. On send, attach the vector. On receive, take the element-wise maximum with the incoming vector, then increment your own entry.

Comparison: V1 happened-before V2 if every entry of V1 is less than or equal to V2 and at least one is strictly smaller. If neither dominates, the events are concurrent (a conflict). Example with processes A, B: A writes, giving [A:1, B:0]. B, without having seen it, writes, giving [A:0, B:1]. Neither dominates, so the two writes are concurrent and need merging.

Costs: vector size grows with the number of writers, so real systems prune or use version vectors per replica, or dotted version vectors. Amazon's original Dynamo paper used vector clocks for cart versions.

What is a hybrid logical clock and why do databases use it? Senior

An HLC timestamp is a pair (l, c): l is the largest physical time seen so far (from the local clock or any received message), and c is a logical counter that breaks ties when l does not advance. On a local event or send: l' = max(l, physical now); if l' equals the old l, increment c, otherwise reset c to 0. On receive: take the max of local l, message l and physical now, and bump c appropriately.

It keeps Lamport's causality guarantee, yet stays within the clock-skew bound of real time, so timestamps are human-readable and usable for "read as of 10:00" queries. It is bounded, small and needs no special hardware. CockroachDB and YugabyteDB use HLC-style timestamps (and MongoDB uses a similar cluster time concept for causal consistency). The weakness: without bounded skew it cannot give external consistency, so these systems add uncertainty intervals or retries on reads.

What is Google's TrueTime and how does Spanner use it? Senior

TrueTime is an API that returns not a point in time but an interval, TT.now() = [earliest, latest], guaranteed to contain the true absolute time. Google keeps the uncertainty small (the Spanner paper reports the bound is typically a few milliseconds) using GPS receivers and atomic clocks in each data center, with time masters polled by all servers.

Spanner uses it for commit wait: when a transaction picks commit timestamp s, it waits until TT.after(s) is true (about the uncertainty width) before making the commit visible. Therefore any transaction that starts after that has a later real timestamp, which yields external consistency (strict serializability) across the globe without a central timestamp server. The cost is the added commit latency equal to the clock uncertainty. Systems without such hardware either accept weaker guarantees or handle uncertainty explicitly (CockroachDB uses a configured maximum offset, with read restarts).

What is the happens-before relation, and what does "concurrent" mean? Intermediate

Lamport defined a happens-before partial order: (1) if a and b are on the same process and a comes first, a happens-before b; (2) if a is sending a message and b is receiving it, a happens-before b; (3) it is transitive. If neither a happens-before b nor b happens-before a, they are concurrent. Concurrent does not mean "at the same instant", it means neither could have known about the other. It is a logical, not physical, notion, and it is the basis for causal consistency, version vectors and conflict detection.

What is total order broadcast and how does it relate to consensus? Senior

Total order (atomic) broadcast delivers every message to every node in the same order, and reliably. It is equivalent to consensus: you can build one from the other. With it you get a replicated state machine, and also things like linearizable compare-and-set or unique ID assignment. A single Kafka partition behaves like a total-order log for its consumers. A system without it (just multicast with no agreement on order) leaves replicas diverging.

How can you generate unique, roughly sortable IDs across nodes without coordination? Intermediate

Snowflake-style IDs: a 64-bit integer composed of a timestamp in milliseconds, a machine or worker ID, and a per-millisecond sequence number. Roughly time-sortable, unique if worker IDs are unique, no central call. Risks: clocks moving backward can produce duplicates or errors (implementations refuse to issue IDs until the clock catches up), and worker IDs must be assigned safely (often through ZooKeeper or etcd). Alternatives: UUID v4 (random, not sortable, poor index locality), UUID v7 or ULID (time-ordered), or a database ticket or sequence service with block allocation.

7. Distributed transactions

Why are transactions across services or shards hard? Basic

A local ACID transaction relies on one database engine with one log and one lock manager. When an operation touches two databases or services (debit an account in service A, credit one in service B), no single engine can make both changes atomic. A crash or timeout between the two steps leaves money debited but not credited. You have to choose: a distributed atomic commit protocol (2PC), or give up atomicity and manage consistency with compensations (sagas), or redesign so that the data touched together lives in one shard or service.

How does two-phase commit (2PC) work? Intermediate

A coordinator and several participants (resource managers).

  1. Prepare (voting): the coordinator asks each participant "can you commit?". Each participant does the work, writes it durably to its log, takes locks, and votes yes (promising it can commit even after a crash) or no.
  2. Commit (decision): if all vote yes, the coordinator logs "commit" durably and tells everyone to commit; if any votes no or times out, it logs "abort" and tells everyone to roll back.
Why is 2PC called a blocking protocol? What are its weaknesses? Senior

If the coordinator crashes after participants voted yes but before they receive the decision, those participants are in doubt: they cannot commit (others may have voted no) nor abort (the coordinator may have decided commit). They hold their locks until the coordinator recovers, blocking other transactions touching those rows. Other costs:

  • Latency: two synchronous round trips plus several log forces.
  • Availability: the transaction fails if any participant is down, so availability is roughly the product of the participants' availabilities.
  • The coordinator is a single point of failure unless replicated (Spanner makes the coordinator and participants Paxos groups, so a crash does not block).
  • Heterogeneous participants (a queue, a third-party API) often do not support the prepare phase at all.

Used successfully inside one organization's database tier (XA transactions, Spanner, distributed SQL), rarely across independently owned microservices.

What is three-phase commit and why is it rarely used? Senior

3PC adds a "pre-commit" phase between prepare and commit, plus timeouts, so that participants can make a safe decision on their own if the coordinator disappears: if nobody has received pre-commit, abort; if pre-commit was received, commit. It is non-blocking, but only under strong assumptions: a synchronous network with bounded delays and no partitions. In a real partition, different groups can reach different decisions, breaking atomicity. Because of this and its extra round trip, production systems prefer 2PC with replicated coordinators (consensus-backed) instead.

What is the saga pattern? Basic

A saga (Garcia-Molina and Salem, 1987) breaks a long business transaction into a sequence of local transactions, each committed in its own service. If a step fails, the saga runs compensating transactions for the steps already done, in reverse order, to semantically undo them.

Example, order flow: (1) create order PENDING, (2) reserve inventory, (3) charge payment, (4) mark order CONFIRMED. If the payment fails: release the inventory, mark the order CANCELLED.

No isolation: other transactions can see intermediate states (inventory reserved but order not yet paid). That is the price for avoiding distributed locks.

What makes a good compensating action, and what cannot be compensated? Intermediate
  • Compensation must be idempotent (it may be retried) and must eventually succeed (retry forever, then alert a human).
  • It must be commutative with delayed or out-of-order messages: a "cancel" can arrive before the "reserve" it cancels, so the service must record the cancel and ignore the late reserve.
  • It is a semantic undo, not a database rollback: a payment refund is a new transaction and the customer sees both the charge and the refund.

Some actions cannot be undone: sending an email or SMS, shipping a parcel, calling an external API without a cancel. Order the saga so that the non-compensable (pivot) step comes late, after all steps that can fail, and use "pending" states and reservations first.

What is Try-Confirm-Cancel (TCC)? Intermediate

A pattern where each participant exposes three operations:

  • Try: reserve resources without finalizing (hold 100 on the account, reserve 1 seat for 10 minutes).
  • Confirm: make the reservation permanent. Must be idempotent and must not fail for business reasons.
  • Cancel: release the reservation. Idempotent, and must tolerate being called with no prior Try ("empty rollback") or after a timeout.

A coordinator calls Try on all, then Confirm on all if every Try succeeded, otherwise Cancel on all. Compared to a plain saga it gives better isolation (the resource is explicitly held, not committed), but every service must be written to support the three-step contract. Seen in payment and booking systems.

What is the dual write problem? Intermediate

When a service must update its database and also publish a message (or update another store) there is no atomic operation covering both. Write the DB then publish: if the process dies between, the event is lost. Publish then write: the event goes out but the DB write may fail, announcing something that never happened. Wrapping both in 2PC is usually not available (most brokers do not support XA). The solution is the transactional outbox.

How does the transactional outbox pattern work? Intermediate
  1. In one local database transaction, write the business change (order row) and an event row into an outbox table. Atomic by the DB.
  2. A separate relay process reads new outbox rows (by polling, or by change data capture on the transaction log, for example Debezium) and publishes them to the broker, then marks them sent.
  3. Consumers handle messages at-least-once, so they must deduplicate (by event ID) or be idempotent. The mirror image, the inbox table, records processed message IDs in the same transaction as the consumer's own side effects.

Properties: no lost events; events can be published more than once; ordering per aggregate is preserved if the relay publishes in commit order and uses the aggregate key as partition key. Polling is simpler; CDC has lower latency and no extra load on tables.

What happens if the saga orchestrator crashes midway? Senior

The orchestrator must persist its state (current step, results so far) durably before and after each step, ideally with event sourcing, so a new instance can resume. On resume it may repeat the last command (it does not know whether it was delivered), so every participant command must be idempotent with a stable command ID. Add timeouts per step, a dead-letter path for stuck sagas and monitoring for sagas older than a threshold. Durable execution engines (Temporal and similar) provide this by replaying the workflow history deterministically.

8. Idempotency, delivery guarantees, deduplication

Explain at-most-once, at-least-once and exactly-once delivery. Basic
  • At-most-once: send and do not retry. Messages may be lost, never duplicated. Fine for metrics or logs where a drop is tolerable.
  • At-least-once: retry until acknowledged. Nothing lost, but duplicates happen (an ack was lost, so the sender resends). The default for most reliable systems.
  • Exactly-once: each message has its effect exactly once. Over an unreliable network the delivery itself cannot be guaranteed once (Two Generals). What can be achieved is effectively-once: at-least-once delivery combined with idempotent or deduplicated processing, so the visible effect happens once.
Is exactly-once delivery possible? How do Kafka's "exactly-once" semantics fit in? Senior

True exactly-once delivery between two independent parties over a lossy network is impossible, but exactly-once processing semantics are achievable within a closed system that controls both ends.

Kafka offers: (1) the idempotent producer, where the broker de-duplicates retries using producer ID and per-partition sequence numbers; (2) transactions, which atomically write output records and consumer offsets across partitions, so a consume-transform-produce pipeline within Kafka has exactly-once effect (consumers must read with isolation level read_committed). Flink offers similar end-to-end semantics with checkpoints and two-phase commit sinks.

The guarantee ends at the boundary of the system: if your consumer also sends an email or calls a payment API, you need your own idempotency (a key per business operation), or the effect can happen twice.

Trap: answering "Kafka gives exactly-once so we are fine" for a flow that writes to an external database or an HTTP API.
What is idempotency and how do you make a payment API idempotent? Basic

An operation is idempotent if doing it many times has the same effect as doing it once. "Set balance to 100" is idempotent; "add 10 to balance" is not. GET, PUT and DELETE are idempotent by HTTP definition; POST is not unless you make it so.

Pattern for POST /payments:

  1. Client generates a unique idempotency key (UUID) per logical operation and sends it in a header; retries reuse the same key.
  2. Server, in one transaction, inserts the key with a unique constraint along with the result. If the key already exists, return the stored response instead of charging again.
  3. Store a hash of the request body with the key; if the same key arrives with a different body, return an error.
  4. Handle the in-flight case: the second request arriving while the first is still running should wait or get 409.
  5. Keep keys for a retention window (for example 24 hours) then expire.

This is the approach used by well-known payment APIs such as Stripe's.

What race conditions break naive idempotency checks? Senior

Check-then-act: "SELECT key; if absent, do work; INSERT key" lets two concurrent retries both see "absent" and both do the work. Fix by making the claim atomic: INSERT the key first (unique constraint) and only the winner proceeds, or use SETNX in Redis, or a conditional put. Another gap: key stored but the side effect not yet done when the process crashes. Either store the key and the effect in the same transaction (only possible within one datastore), or record a state (STARTED, DONE) and have the retry resume safely, which demands the downstream call also be idempotent (pass the same key to it).

How do you deduplicate messages in a consumer? Intermediate
  • Unique message ID plus a processed-IDs table written in the same transaction as the side effect (inbox pattern). Exact, cost grows with time, so expire after a window larger than the maximum redelivery delay.
  • Natural idempotency: upsert by key, "set" rather than "increment", conditional update (update where version = 5).
  • Sequence numbers per producer: the consumer remembers the last sequence seen per producer and drops anything lower or equal (as TCP and Kafka's idempotent producer do). Cheap, needs ordering per producer.
  • Bloom filter as a fast first check: a "not seen" answer is certain, but "seen" may be a false positive, so confirm against the exact store before dropping.
  • Time-bounded cache (Redis with TTL) for high-volume, tolerant cases; a duplicate outside the window slips through.
How do retries and duplicates interact with message ordering? Senior

A retry can reorder messages: message 1 fails and is retried after message 2 already succeeded. If order matters, either allow only one in-flight message per key (Kafka's max.in.flight with idempotence keeps order within a partition), or attach version numbers and let the consumer discard stale updates ("apply only if version greater than current"). Also route by key so all messages for an entity go to one partition or queue consumer. Global order is rarely needed and expensive; per-entity order is usually enough.

9. Distributed locks and leader election

When do you need a distributed lock, and what are the alternatives? Basic

Needed for mutual exclusion across processes: only one worker runs the nightly job, only one process writes to a shared file, only one instance processes a given customer's account at a time.

Prefer to avoid it: use a database unique constraint or conditional update (optimistic locking with a version column), a single-partition queue per key (only one consumer per key), or make the operation idempotent so that two runs are harmless. Two types by purpose: efficiency locks (avoid duplicate work; occasional double-run is just waste) and correctness locks (a double-run corrupts data). Correctness locks need much stronger guarantees.

Why are distributed locks tricky? Describe the classic failure. Senior

A lock must be released if its holder dies, so it has a lease (TTL). But a holder cannot know for sure its lease is still valid when it acts, because it may be paused (GC, VM stall, page fault, slow network) between checking the lock and using the resource.

Timeline:
1. Client A acquires the lock with a 30 s lease.
2. A hits a long GC pause (or is partitioned) for 40 s.
3. The lease expires; client B acquires the lock and writes to the storage.
4. A wakes up, still believes it holds the lock, and writes stale or conflicting data.
Result: two holders, lock violated, data corrupted.

You cannot fix it with a better lock service alone: the pause happens in the client, after the lock check. The fix is at the resource: fencing tokens.

What is a fencing token and how does it solve this? Senior

Each time the lock service grants the lock it also returns a number that strictly increases (33, then 34, ...). The client sends the token with every write to the protected resource. The resource remembers the highest token it has seen and rejects any request with a lower token.

In the timeline above, B got token 34 and wrote. When the paused A wakes up and tries to write with token 33, the storage rejects it. Safety now rests on the resource, not on A's belief. Requirements: the resource must be able to check the token (databases with a version column or conditional write; object stores with conditional put or generation numbers), and the token must come from a linearizable source (ZooKeeper zxid or the sequential node number, etcd revision, a Raft term). Fencing is the same idea as Raft terms and epoch numbers.

What is Redlock and what is the debate around it? Senior

Redlock is an algorithm proposed by Redis's author (Salvatore Sanfilippo) for locking with several independent Redis instances (typically 5): acquire the lock on a majority with a TTL, within a time bound that accounts for elapsed time and clock drift; if it fails, release everywhere.

In 2016 Martin Kleppmann published a critique; Sanfilippo replied. The main points of the critique:

  • It does not produce a fencing token, so it cannot protect against the pause scenario.
  • It depends on timing assumptions (bounded clock drift, bounded pauses) that real systems can violate; for example a clock jump on a Redis node can cause the lock to expire early.
  • For efficiency-only locks it is heavier than needed (a single Redis node suffices); for correctness locks it is not safe enough (use a consensus system with fencing).

Balanced takeaway: a single-instance Redis lock with TTL (SET key value NX PX ttl, release via a script that checks the value) is fine for efficiency; for strict correctness use ZooKeeper, etcd or the database itself, with fencing tokens.

How do you implement a safe lock release and renewal? Intermediate
  • Store a unique value (random token) when acquiring. On release, delete only if the stored value equals yours, atomically (a Lua script in Redis; a compare-and-delete transaction in etcd). Otherwise you may delete a lock that someone else acquired after yours expired.
  • Set a TTL always, so a crashed holder cannot deadlock the system.
  • Renew (heartbeat or watchdog thread) at about a third of the TTL while working. If renewal fails, stop work immediately.
  • Make the protected work short and idempotent.
Can you use the database as a distributed lock? Intermediate

Yes, and it is often the safest choice when you already depend on that database for the data. Options: SELECT ... FOR UPDATE on a row; advisory locks (PostgreSQL pg_advisory_lock, MySQL GET_LOCK); an INSERT into a table with a unique key and an expiry column; or optimistic concurrency, UPDATE ... WHERE version = ?. If the lock and the data are in the same database, a transaction gives you atomicity with no separate lock service at all. Caveats: it adds load to the primary, and session-level locks are tied to connection lifetime (watch connection pools).

How can a group of services elect a single leader? Intermediate

Common approaches:

  • Coordination service: candidates try to create an ephemeral node (ZooKeeper) or a key bound to a lease (etcd); the creator is leader and the others watch. Kubernetes controllers use a Lease object that the leader keeps renewing.
  • Built into a consensus protocol: Raft elects its own leader by votes and terms.
  • Database row: a leader row with an expiry that the leader renews using compare-and-swap.
  • Classic algorithms: bully algorithm (highest ID wins, assumes reliable detection) and ring election; mostly textbook, and unsafe under partitions.

Always combine with fencing: an old leader may keep acting for a short time after losing leadership, so downstream resources should check the term or token.

What is a leader lease and what is the risk? Senior

The leader holds a time-limited lease granted by the followers, during which no one else can be elected. It lets the leader serve reads locally without contacting a quorum. Risk: it is only safe if the leader's clock does not run slower than the followers' clocks by more than the allowed bound, and if the leader is not paused past its lease. Clock drift bounds and renewing well before expiry reduce the risk but do not remove it; writes should still go through consensus and carry terms.

What goes wrong when many nodes wait for the same lock or leader key? Intermediate

When the lock is released, all waiters wake at once and stampede the coordination service (herd effect), only one wins, the rest retry. Fix: queue-based locks where each waiter watches only its predecessor (ZooKeeper sequential node recipe), add random jitter to retries, or use a queue with a single consumer. Also be careful with short TTL plus many clients renewing; the lock service itself becomes the bottleneck.

10. Gossip and failure detection

What is a gossip (epidemic) protocol and why is it used? Intermediate

Each node periodically (for example every second) picks a few random peers and exchanges state with them. Information spreads like a rumor or an epidemic: in each round the number of informed nodes roughly doubles, so a cluster of N nodes is informed in about O(log N) rounds. Properties: no central coordinator, robust to node failures and message loss, constant load per node, and scales to thousands of nodes. Downside: only eventual consistency, there is redundant traffic, and convergence time is probabilistic.

Uses: cluster membership and failure information (Cassandra, Consul's Serf, Akka cluster, Dynamo), spreading configuration or schema versions, and aggregating metrics. Variants: push, pull and push-pull; push-pull converges fastest.

How does SWIM-style failure detection work? Senior

SWIM separates failure detection from dissemination. Each period, a node pings one random member. If no ack arrives, it asks k other members to ping the target indirectly (ping-req). If still nothing, it marks the target suspect rather than dead, and gossips this; the suspect gets a chance to refute it (by incrementing an incarnation number) before it is declared failed. This reduces false positives caused by one bad link, and the per-node load stays constant regardless of cluster size, unlike all-to-all heartbeats which cost O(N squared) messages. Membership updates piggyback on the ping messages. Used in Consul/Serf and in many other membership libraries (with extensions).

How do heartbeats and timeouts detect failure, and what is the trade-off? Basic

A node sends "I am alive" messages every T seconds; if the monitor sees nothing for a timeout (often several multiples of T), it declares the node failed. Trade-off: a short timeout detects crashes fast but produces false positives during GC pauses and network blips (and a falsely removed node can trigger needless failover and data movement); a long timeout is stable but leaves clients hitting a dead node longer. A failure detector in an asynchronous network can never be both complete (detects every crash) and accurate (never wrongly suspects) with certainty, since a slow node looks identical to a dead one.

What is the phi accrual failure detector? Senior

Instead of a yes or no, it outputs a continuous suspicion level phi. It keeps a sliding window of recent heartbeat inter-arrival times, fits a distribution, and computes the probability that a heartbeat this late would still arrive. Phi is roughly the negative log (base 10) of that probability: phi = 1 means about 10 percent chance the node is alive and merely late, phi = 2 about 1 percent, phi = 8 about one in 10^8. Applications choose a threshold (Cassandra's default is around 8 and Akka uses the same family of detector), and the detector adapts automatically to the network's normal jitter: on a noisy link the window widens and the same silence yields a lower phi. Different consumers can use different thresholds for different actions (alerting versus failover).

What properties describe a failure detector? Senior
  • Completeness: every crashed node is eventually suspected by every correct node.
  • Accuracy: correct nodes are not suspected (strong form: never; eventual form: after some time).
  • Practical metrics: detection time, false-positive rate, and message load.

Chandra and Toueg showed that consensus is solvable in an asynchronous system with an "eventually strong" failure detector, which is the theoretical basis for timeouts-based leader election in Paxos and Raft.

What are anti-entropy, read repair and hinted handoff? Intermediate

Mechanisms Dynamo-style stores use to heal replicas that missed writes:

  • Read repair: on a read, the coordinator compares versions from several replicas, returns the newest, and writes it back to stale ones.
  • Hinted handoff: if a replica is down during a write, another node stores a "hint" and forwards it when the replica returns (this is what makes a quorum "sloppy").
  • Anti-entropy with Merkle trees: replicas build hash trees over key ranges, compare roots, and descend only into differing subtrees to find and sync mismatched data cheaply. Runs in the background.

11. CRDTs and conflict resolution

Why do conflicts occur, and what are the strategies to resolve them? Intermediate

Conflicts occur whenever two replicas accept writes to the same item without coordinating (multi-leader replication, leaderless stores, offline-capable clients, partition with AP choice). Strategies:

  1. Avoid: route all writes for a key to one leader or region (home region per user).
  2. Last-write-wins (LWW): keep the write with the highest timestamp; simple, can lose data.
  3. Version vectors and siblings: detect concurrency, keep all concurrent versions and let the application or user merge.
  4. Application-specific merge: for example union the shopping carts.
  5. CRDTs: data types designed so that any merge order gives the same result automatically.
  6. Operational transformation (collaborative text editors) as an alternative to CRDTs.
What is last-write-wins and what are its dangers? Intermediate

Each write carries a timestamp; on conflict, the highest timestamp wins and the other write is discarded. It guarantees convergence and is cheap, so Cassandra uses it per cell by default. Dangers:

  • Silent data loss: two concurrent updates to different fields of the same record, if resolved at record level, drop one entire update.
  • Clock skew: a node whose clock is ahead by 5 seconds will win against a later write from a correct node; an earlier causally later write can lose.
  • Ties need a deterministic tiebreaker.

Mitigation: resolve at field level, use server-assigned or hybrid logical timestamps, or only use LWW for data where losing an update is fine (last seen time, cache). Cassandra practice: avoid overwriting the same cell concurrently; use immutable inserts with unique keys.

How do version vectors differ from vector clocks, and how are siblings handled? Senior

Vector clocks track causality between events or processes. Version vectors track versions of a data item per replica: one counter per replica that has coordinated writes to that key. They are used for the same detection: if one vector dominates, the older version is obsolete and discarded; if neither dominates, the two are concurrent siblings.

Example (Dynamo cart): a client reads cart version [A:1] and adds a book, producing [A:2]; another client, via another coordinator, reads [A:1] and adds a pen, producing [A:1, B:1]. Neither dominates, so the store returns both, and the client merges (union of items) and writes the merge back with a vector that dominates both. Known limit: in the "add and remove" case a naive union can resurrect deleted items. The vector grows with replicas that coordinate, so implementations use dotted version vectors or pruning to keep it small.

What is a CRDT and why does it need no coordination? Intermediate

A Conflict-free Replicated Data Type is a data structure whose replicas can be updated independently and concurrently, and always converge to the same state when they have exchanged all updates, with no locking or consensus. The math: states form a semilattice and the merge (join) operation is commutative, associative and idempotent. So it does not matter in what order, how many times, or via which paths updates arrive.

Two families:

  • State-based (CvRDT): replicas send their full state (or deltas) and merge. Tolerates duplicates and reordering, simple network needs.
  • Operation-based (CmRDT): replicas broadcast operations; needs reliable, exactly-once, causally ordered delivery, but messages are small.
Give examples of common CRDTs and how they work. Senior
  • G-Counter (grow-only): a vector with one slot per replica; each replica increments only its own slot; value = sum; merge = element-wise max.
  • PN-Counter: two G-Counters, one for increments and one for decrements; value = P minus N. Cannot enforce a lower bound (stock cannot go below 0), since that needs coordination.
  • G-Set / 2P-Set: add-only set; the two-phase set adds a tombstone set, but a removed element can never be re-added.
  • OR-Set (observed-remove set): each add creates a unique tag; remove deletes only the tags it has seen; so a concurrent add and remove leaves the element present ("add wins"), and re-adding works.
  • LWW-Register: value plus timestamp, highest wins.
  • Sequence CRDTs (RGA, Logoot, Yjs, Automerge) for collaborative text editing.

Used by: Riak data types, Redis Enterprise active-active, Azure Cosmos DB and others (conflict-free multi-region), collaborative editors, and some offline-first mobile sync.

What are the limitations of CRDTs? Senior
  • They cannot enforce invariants that need global agreement (unique usernames, non-negative balance, "only one winner").
  • Metadata growth: tombstones and per-replica or per-element tags need garbage collection, which itself often needs coordination or causal stability knowledge.
  • The merged result is deterministic but may not match user intent (concurrent edits can interleave oddly).
  • Limited set of types; custom ones are hard to design correctly.

Use them when availability and offline writes matter more than strict invariants; otherwise use consensus for the part that has an invariant.

How would you handle conflicting updates in a multi-region active-active database? Senior
  1. First reduce conflicts: pin each entity (user, tenant) to a home region for writes, so most conflicts cannot occur; others read locally.
  2. For data where concurrent writes are expected, pick a rule per data type: counters and sets as CRDTs, free-form fields by field-level LWW with hybrid timestamps, high-value records through a single owner or consensus.
  3. Detect and log conflicts (version vectors) so you can audit and repair.
  4. Make operations commutative where possible (record deltas and events instead of final values).
  5. For invariants (unique, balance), route through one region or use a consensus-backed store for those rows.

12. Fault models, Byzantine faults and split brain

What fault models do distributed algorithms assume? Intermediate
  • Crash-stop: a node works correctly until it halts and never returns.
  • Crash-recovery: a node may crash and restart, keeping only its durable storage. This is what Raft and Paxos assume, hence fsync before replying.
  • Omission: a node fails to send or receive some messages.
  • Timing / performance faults: responses are too late (pauses, slowness).
  • Byzantine (arbitrary): a node may behave in any way, including lying or colluding.

Stronger fault models need more nodes and more messages.

What is a Byzantine fault and when do you need to tolerate it? Senior

A Byzantine node can send conflicting information to different peers, forge data, or collude (from the Byzantine Generals problem, Lamport, Shostak and Pease, 1982). To tolerate f Byzantine nodes you need at least 3f + 1 nodes and a quorum of 2f + 1, because some of the nodes you hear from may be liars. For f = 1, that is 4 nodes. PBFT (Castro and Liskov) is the classic practical protocol; modern blockchain consensus protocols (Tendermint and others) descend from it.

Inside one company's data center, nodes are trusted, so crash-fault tolerance (2f + 1 nodes) is the norm. You need Byzantine tolerance when participants are mutually distrusting (public or consortium blockchains) or in safety-critical hardware where bit flips and software bugs may cause arbitrary behavior (aerospace). Cheaper defenses in ordinary systems: checksums, authentication, signatures and TLS.

What is split brain and how do you prevent it? Intermediate

Split brain is when a network partition (or a falsely detected failure) leaves two parts of a cluster each believing it is the active leader or primary, so both accept writes and the data diverges. Examples: a database with automatic failover where the old primary is merely unreachable, or an old Elasticsearch cluster with two master-eligible nodes in two halves.

Prevention:

  • Majority quorum: only the side with a majority may operate; the minority steps down or goes read-only. Needs an odd node count or a tie-breaker (witness / arbiter node).
  • Fencing: make sure the old primary cannot write after being replaced: fencing tokens at the storage, revoking its access, or STONITH ("shoot the other node in the head", cutting power or network via a management interface).
  • Leases that expire, with clocks assumed bounded.
  • Consensus-based election rather than ad hoc scripts.

Elasticsearch before version 7 needed the setting minimum_master_nodes set to a majority to avoid it; version 7 and later manage the voting configuration automatically.

Describe a real incident caused by a partition and automated failover. Senior

GitHub, October 2018 (as described in its public post-mortem): a brief network connectivity loss, on the order of tens of seconds, between its US East Coast network hub and its primary East Coast data center made an automated failover tool (Orchestrator) promote MySQL primaries in the US West Coast data center. When connectivity returned, both coasts had accepted writes the other lacked, so the data had diverged. Because of cross-country latency, moving back was not possible without losing data, and engineers chose to preserve data integrity, which led to many hours (roughly a day) of degraded service while data was reconciled. Lessons: automated failover across high-latency links can be worse than the original blip; replicas should not be promoted on a short partition; and quorum or consensus-backed topology management helps. (Details above are from memory of the public write-up; verify exact figures before quoting.)

What is a zombie or stale leader and how do you neutralize it? Senior

A node that was leader, got paused or partitioned, and does not know it has been replaced. When it resumes it may keep issuing commands. Neutralize with: term or epoch numbers in every message so followers reject stale terms; fencing tokens at downstream resources; leases checked right before acting (and short enough); and the node stepping down on its own if it cannot reach a quorum (Raft leaders do this with a check-quorum option).

Is a two-node cluster highly available? Intermediate

Not with strong consistency. With 2 nodes the majority is 2, so losing either node loses quorum; and if the nodes stay up but cannot talk to each other, each side cannot tell whether the other died or the link failed. Options: add a third small voter (witness or arbiter, storing no data), or accept availability over consistency (both sides continue and reconcile later), or use a shared external arbiter such as a cloud lock or quorum disk. The "2 nodes plus witness" design is a standard answer.

13. Situational and real-life scenarios

A payment service times out calling the bank API. The customer clicks pay again. How do you avoid charging twice? Intermediate
  1. The first request's outcome is unknown, so treat it as pending, not failed.
  2. Each payment attempt has a client-generated idempotency key that is also forwarded to the bank (if it supports it).
  3. The retry reuses the key; the server returns the stored result or reuses the in-flight attempt.
  4. If the bank offers no idempotency, query the bank by your reference ID to find out whether the first charge happened before retrying, and run a periodic reconciliation job that compares your records with the bank's.
  5. UI: disable the button, show "processing", and poll status instead of submitting again.
You find two instances of a singleton job running at once, both writing. What do you investigate? Senior
  • How is exclusivity enforced: a TTL lock? Was the TTL shorter than a possible pause or long-running step (GC, slow DB call)?
  • Was there a partition between an instance and the lock store, so the first one kept running without renewal checks?
  • Clock issues if expiry relies on wall clocks of different hosts.
  • Fix: add fencing tokens on the target writes, make the job idempotent, have the instance abort when renewal fails, and consider a coordination store with consensus (etcd, ZooKeeper) or a DB row with conditional update instead of a single Redis node.
Users report that after editing their profile they sometimes see the old data. How do you diagnose and fix it? Intermediate

Likely replication lag: writes go to the primary, reads go to async replicas or a cache that is not yet invalidated. Check replica lag metrics and whether the read path uses a load balancer across replicas. Fixes ranked by cost: read your own writes from the primary for a short window after a write; carry a version or LSN token and read from a replica only if it has caught up; sticky routing; invalidate or update the cache on write; as a last resort, move that read to the primary. Explain that you picked read-your-writes rather than global strong consistency to keep read scalability.

Your 5-node etcd or ZooKeeper cluster is split 3 and 2 by a network issue. What happens to each side and to dependent services? Intermediate

The 3-node side has a majority (3 of 5) and keeps electing a leader and serving reads and writes. The 2-node side cannot commit, so it rejects writes and linearizable reads (clients connected there get errors or timeouts). Dependent services connected to the minority side lose coordination: they cannot renew leases, so their locks and leadership expire and move to nodes on the majority side. Clients must have endpoints for all members and fail over. When the partition heals, the minority nodes catch up from the leader's log. No divergent data occurs, which is the point of consensus.

Data written to a Cassandra table keeps getting overwritten by older values. What could be going on? Senior

Cassandra uses client or coordinator timestamps with last-write-wins. If one application server's clock is ahead (NTP broken or a VM clock jump), its writes carry bigger timestamps, so an older write from that server beats a newer write from a correct server, and the "correct" updates look lost. Check clock offsets (chrony or ntp status), fix NTP, compare writetime() values of the cells, and where possible switch to server-side timestamps or lightweight transactions for contested rows, or redesign to avoid overwriting the same cell (append events, version columns).

An order flow spans inventory, payment and shipping services. Payment succeeds but shipping fails. What should happen? Intermediate

Use a saga. Shipping failure triggers compensations in reverse: refund the payment (a new refund transaction, idempotent, retried until it succeeds), release the inventory reservation, mark the order failed and notify the customer. First decide if the failure is retryable: if shipping was temporarily down, retry with backoff before compensating. Keep each step's command idempotent with a stable ID, store saga state durably, and publish events via an outbox so no step silently disappears. Put the least reversible step (actually shipping) last.

A leader node had a 20 second GC pause and the cluster elected a new leader. What are the risks when the old one wakes up? Senior

The old leader may immediately act on stale beliefs (still sends writes, answers reads as leader). Protection: it learns the higher term on its first interaction and steps down; followers and storage reject stale-term or lower-token requests; client reads should go through ReadIndex or quorum rather than the old leader's local lease. Long term: tune GC (smaller heaps, a low-pause collector), set session or election timeouts above the realistic pause, and alert on pauses so it does not look like a network problem.

An interviewer asks: "which consistency would you choose for a like counter, a bank balance, and a chat message order?" Intermediate
  • Like counter: eventual consistency with a CRDT-like or sharded counter; approximate and slightly stale is fine, availability matters.
  • Bank balance: linearizable, serializable transactions on the single account record, a CP store, idempotent transfers; reject rather than guess during partitions.
  • Chat message order: per-conversation order is enough (causal or total order within one conversation, for example a single partition per conversation); there is no need for global order across conversations.

Pattern: choose the weakest model that still preserves the invariants of that data.

Your consumer reads from Kafka and writes to a database; after a crash you see duplicate rows. Why, and how do you fix it? Intermediate

The consumer wrote to the DB but crashed before committing its offset, so on restart the same messages were redelivered (at-least-once). Fix: make the write idempotent (upsert on a business key or the message's topic, partition and offset as a unique key), or store the offset in the same DB transaction as the data and seek to it on startup, or deduplicate with an inbox table. Committing the offset before processing flips the problem into possible loss (at-most-once).
