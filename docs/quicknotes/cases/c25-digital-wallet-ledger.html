Case sheet 25: Design a Digital Wallet and Ledger

One-page cheat sheet: a wallet that holds real money and moves it between users, backed by a double-entry, append-only ledger. Full write-up: Handbook case study 25.

ProblemRequirementsEstimationAPIDataArchitectureDeep divesScalingFailuresImpressQ&ALinks

1. Problem in 3 lines

One line: users keep a balance, top up, pay each other, and withdraw. Every cent must be accounted for, every transfer must happen exactly once, and the history must be provable years later.
  • The money lives inside our system as a stored balance. That is a liability (money we owe the user), backed by cash in bank accounts. In a card payment system the money stays at the bank; here we are the bookkeeper.
  • Core idea to say first: a balance is not a number we edit. It is a summary of immutable ledger entries.
  • Scale is modest, correctness is the hard part.
Clarifying questionAssumed answer
Real money or points?Real money, so regulators want funds safeguarded; we must prove wallet balances do not exceed cash held.
Scale?50M wallets, 20M daily active users, 30M transfers a day, peak 5 times average.
Negative balance?Never for a user wallet. System accounts (bank clearing, fees) may be either sign.
Currencies?Many, but each account holds one. Conversion is a separate, explicit operation.
Consistency?Strong for money (debit and credit together, exactly once). Derived views may lag a few seconds.
Provable history?Yes: the balance on any past date and the entries behind it.
Fees, holds, limits?Yes to all three.
Availability or correctness?Correctness. A "try again" is fine; a lost or invented cent is not.

2. Requirements

Functional

  • Create user accounts (per currency) and system accounts (bank clearing, fees, transit).
  • Top up and withdraw through slow, unreliable banks.
  • Transfer wallet to wallet or to a merchant, with an optional fee.
  • Hold, capture, release: reserve money so it cannot be spent twice.
  • Balance, paged history, statements.
  • Reconcile against bank and processor records.
  • Fix mistakes with a new balanced transaction, never an edit.

Non-functional

  • Exactly-once effect: retried transfers move money once.
  • Atomic: debit and credit together, or neither final.
  • Never overdrawn, even with concurrent spends.
  • Durable: an acknowledged transfer is never lost (zero data loss for the ledger).
  • Latency p99: same-shard transfer under 150 ms, cross-shard under 400 ms, balance read under 50 ms.
  • Availability: 99.95% for money writes, 99.99% for balance reads. If unsure, refuse or delay.
  • Audit: immutable history kept for years (often 7 or more).

Out of scope: the card and bank integrations themselves (behind an adapter), identity checks beyond a hook, fraud-model training, tax, interest and lending, the company's own accounting books.

3. Estimation (same numbers as the handbook)

Traffic: 30M transfers a day / 86,400 s = about 347 per second average, 1,750 per second at peak (5x). Top-ups and withdrawals: 6M a day, about 70 per second average, 350 at peak. Balance reads: 20M users x 10 opens = 200M a day = about 2,315 per second, about 11,600 per second at peak (reads outnumber writes about 6 to 1). History reads: 60M a day, about 700 per second.

  • Hot relational ledger grows about 8 to 9 TB a year, about 25 TB over three years before replication. One primary cannot hold that plus the write rate, so we shard.
  • Balance cache: 50M x about 100 B = about 5 GB. Fits a small Redis cluster.
  • Per shard: a transfer is 6 to 8 SQL statements in one transaction. Plan on about 500 transfers per second per shard. 1,750 / 500 = 3.5, so 4 is the minimum; choose 16 shards (about 110 peak per shard, 1.6 TB each after three years).
  • Cross-shard share: sender and receiver share a shard 1 time in 16, so about 94% of transfers cross shards. This is why the cross-shard design is the heart of the answer, not an edge case.
  • Bandwidth is under 2 MB per second at peak: irrelevant. Disk write latency and correctness matter.
  • Money in flight: average transfer 25 dollars, so about 750M dollars moved a day, about 2B dollars of customer balances. A one-in-a-million error is about 750 dollars lost every day. So we prove invariants instead of trusting "usually right".

4. API

Every money call carries a client-made idempotency key (a unique id for one intent, repeated on every retry). Amounts are integers in minor units (4999 means 49.99); never floating point.

POST /v1/transfers   Idempotency-Key: 5b2f...
  { "from_account":"acc_A1", "to_account":"acc_B7", "amount":2500,
    "currency":"USD", "fee_policy":"sender_pays" }
  -> 201 { "transfer_id", "status":"completed" | "pending", "from_balance_after" }
     409 request_in_progress | 422 key_reused_with_different_body | 402 insufficient_funds
POST /v1/topups | /v1/withdrawals     (Idempotency-Key required)
POST /v1/holds   { account, amount, expires_in }
POST /v1/holds/{id}/capture  (partial allowed)   POST /v1/holds/{id}/release
GET  /v1/accounts/{id}/balance  -> { posted, held, available, as_of }
GET  /v1/accounts/{id}/entries?cursor=&limit=50
GET  /v1/transfers/{id}
  • Honest status: a cross-shard or bank-backed transfer may return pending; the final result comes by event or by reading the transfer.
  • Three balance numbers: posted (sum of completed entries), held (reserved), available = posted minus held. Spending checks available.
  • Cursors, not page numbers, because new entries arrive at the head. Server-made, time-sortable ids.

5. Data model and store choices

Store choice: a relational database for the ledger, because we need multi-row atomic transactions, unique constraints (idempotency is a unique index), check constraints (balance never negative), and strong consistency. Eventually consistent stores only for rebuildable views (history, search, analytics).

  • Signed entries: a wallet is a liability, so a positive entry means "we owe the user more". System accounts (bank clearing, fee revenue, transit) are the other side of every movement. Money never enters or leaves the ledger without a balancing entry.
  • Shard by account_id (hash). An account's entries, balance, holds and sequence live together, so "debit if funds suffice" is one local statement. The price: most transfers touch two shards. We design the protocol for it instead of avoiding it. Idempotency keys and the transfer record live on the sender's shard.

6. Architecture and main flows

Flow A: same-shard transfer (about 6% of transfers)

  1. Client posts the transfer with an idempotency key.
  2. One database transaction begins. Insert the key row (a duplicate key stops here and returns the stored reply).
  3. Lock both accounts in account-id order (fixed order avoids deadlocks between opposite transfers).
  4. Conditional update: debit only if available is at least amount plus fee.
  5. Insert 3 balanced entries (sender, receiver, fee), update cached balances, insert the transfer and an outbox row.
  6. Commit after a synchronous replica has the data. Return 201 with the new balance.
  7. The relay publishes transfer.completed from the outbox, so a crash cannot lose the event.

Flow B: cross-shard transfer (about 94%)

Transfer states: initiated to debited to completed; or initiated to failed (nothing moved); or debited to reversing to reversed. Every change is a compare-and-set on the transfer version. No automatic path leaves completed; undoing a finished transfer is a new refund transfer.

7. Deep dives

7.1 Idempotency and overdraft protection

The network cannot give exactly-once delivery: a timeout does not say whether the server acted. So we build at-least-once delivery plus idempotent effects, which gives exactly-once effects.

Key stateWhat we do
Never seenInsert key as in_progress in the money transaction, do the work, store the reply in the same commit.
Done, same request hashReturn the stored reply byte for byte. No new effect.
In progressReturn 409 with a retry hint. Never start a second run.
Different request hashReject with 422: the client reused a key for a different intent.
Saga pending, process diedRecovery resumes from the saved state; the retry just reads it.

Uniqueness must come from a database unique index, never "check then insert" in code. Keys are scoped per client and kept about 7 days.

Overdraft approachHowTrade-off
Pessimistic lockSELECT FOR UPDATE, check, updateCorrect, simple; needs fixed lock order.
Conditional update (chosen)UPDATE ... WHERE posted - held >= amt; zero rows means insufficient fundsOne statement, atomic; plus a check constraint as a second safety net.
Optimistic version checkUpdate where version matches, retry on conflictCheap when rare, wasteful on hot accounts.

7.2 Double-entry, append-only ledger

Every transaction's signed entries sum to zero, so money is only moved, never created. Example: Alice sends 100.00 to Bob with a 1.00 fee paid by the sender. Entries: Alice -101.00, Bob +100.00, Fee revenue +1.00. Sum is zero.
  • Rules: entries sum to zero per transaction (code check plus database constraint); the app role has no UPDATE or DELETE on entries; mistakes are fixed by a new reversing transaction; unique (transfer, leg) makes a replayed step inert; per-account gap-free sequence.
  • Why immutable: auditors can replay any balance at any date; inserts do not fight like repeated updates to one row; tampering shows up (optional hash chain per account, daily digests stored write-once).
  • Balance is derived: the cached balance is exact at commit, and a nightly job recomputes it from entries (or last snapshot plus later entries). A mismatch pages an engineer. Keep both: the cache for speed, the recompute for trust.
  • Holds reserve money without moving it: create raises held amount after checking available; capture turns it into a real transfer (partial releases the rest); release or expiry lowers it. No ledger entries for the hold itself. Capture after expiry fails, checked under lock.

7.3 Cross-shard transfers

OptionProsCons
A. One big databaseLocal ACID for everythingCeiling at 25 TB and write rate; one failure domain. Fine as phase 1.
B. Two-phase commit across shardsAtomic on paperLocks held across network trips; a coordinator crash leaves in-doubt rows; throughput and availability drop as shards grow.
C. Saga with transit accounts (chosen)Cheap local transactions, no cross-shard locks, resumable, scales linearlyA short visible "in transit" state; needs careful idempotency and compensation.
D. Dedicated ledger engine (for example TigerBeetle)Very high throughput, transfer semantics built inNew system to operate; evaluate at much larger scale.

Why transit accounts work: each shard has a system account for "money that left a wallet here and has not arrived". Every leg balances by itself on its own shard, so each shard sums to zero even mid-saga. Across all shards, TransitOut plus TransitIn equals the value of in-flight transfers, and zero when none are. A checker watches this every few minutes.

Trap: treating a leg 2 timeout as a refusal. If leg 2 actually committed and you compensate, Alice gets her money back and Bob keeps his: you created money. Compensate only on a definite stored refusal or an operator decision; otherwise retry with the same key.

7.4 Event sourcing and derived views

OptionTrade-off
State plus entries (chosen)Balance row updated atomically with immutable entries; events leave through the outbox. Fast, strong spend checks. Two representations to keep in step, guarded by the nightly recompute.
Pure event sourcingOne truth and neat replay, but every spend must fold events under concurrency control and reads are always eventually consistent.
Mutable balance onlyFastest to build; unauditable. Rejected.

Spend decisions read the transactional balance row. History, statements, notifications, analytics and search are projections built from the ordered event stream. Projections lag a second or two, so the app overlays pending items right after a write. Projectors store the last applied sequence per account, so repeats do nothing, and the gap-free sequence lets them notice a missing event. Ordering is per account (one partition keyed by account id), which is enough. Snapshots (a verified balance at sequence N) keep replay short. Any projection can be dropped and rebuilt.

7.5 Top-ups, withdrawals, currencies

  • Top-up: write an external-movement row first, call the provider with a key derived from our movement id, wait for result or webhook. On confirmation one balanced transaction credits the wallet and debits bank clearing. Until then it is pending and not spendable. Bank debits can bounce days later, so for risky users credit after settlement, or credit early up to a limit and claw back with a reversing entry.
  • Withdrawal: reserve first (a hold or a debit into a "withdrawals in flight" account), then instruct the bank, then finalize. Never pay first and debit later: a crash in between gives money away. Large amounts need a second approval.
  • Bank timeout is unknown, not failed. Retry with the same provider key or query by reference, with backoff and jitter, then a "needs review" state.
  • Currencies: each ledger transaction balances per currency. A conversion is a pair of transactions through FX clearing accounts, with the rate, source and time recorded. Use each currency's decimal places (JPY 0, most 2, some 3). Round by a written rule (typically half-even).

7.6 Reconciliation

Reality sometimes disagrees with the books: a bank return we never heard of, a buggy deploy, a manual fix. Reconciliation proves instead of assumes.

HalfWhat it checks
Internal invariants (every few minutes, full run nightly)Sum of all entries is 0; each cached balance equals the sum of its entries; transit totals equal in-flight transfers; no user balance below 0; no gaps in sequences. A violation is an incident, not a ticket.
External matching (nightly)Match bank and processor file lines to our records by provider reference, then amount, date and account. Most important check: total wallet balances do not exceed the safeguarded cash (plus funds in transit).

Exception types name the bug. In the bank file but not ours: a missed webhook or a crash after calling the bank. In ours but not the file: recorded but not happened, or not settled yet. Amount mismatch: fees or currency. Duplicate in the file: a real double movement that beat every earlier defence. Fixes are always new ledger transactions that reference the exception.

  • Consistency: strong inside a shard; across shards, eventual with explicit states (tens of milliseconds in transit, status pending). Read-your-writes for the user who acted. The money path is CP: if a primary is unreachable, refuse rather than guess. Only derived views are AP-like.

10. Things to say to impress

Say this:
  • "A balance is a summary of immutable entries. Everything else protects that ledger."
  • "There is no exactly-once on a network. I build at-least-once plus idempotent effects, with the dedupe key as a unique index in the same transaction as the money."
  • "With 16 shards, 15 of 16 transfers cross shards, so I design the saga first. Transit accounts keep every shard balanced even mid-transfer."
  • "A timeout is unknown, not failed. I never compensate on a timeout."
  • "Spend decisions read the primary; history and statements are projections I can rebuild."
  • "Reconciliation is how I prove the books: entries sum to zero, balances equal entries, transit equals in-flight, wallets do not exceed cash."
  • "Phase 1 is one database with idempotency and double entry from day one. Retrofitting those later is painful."
  • "At 10x (about 17,500 per second at peak) I go to about 128 shards via the bucket map, batch commits, consider a dedicated ledger engine, and move reconciliation to streaming."
Numbers to remember: 347 per second average, 1,750 peak. 16 shards, 94% cross-shard. About 9 TB a year of ledger and transfers. 5 GB balance cache. Same-shard p99 under 150 ms, cross-shard under 400 ms. Stuck transfers: alert at 5 minutes.

Security and cost in one breath: step-up authentication for risky actions; the caller may only debit their own accounts, checked in the service; the app database role cannot update or delete entries; personal data is kept apart from entries so deletion requests do not break legal retention; webhooks are signed and critical state is re-fetched. Infrastructure is modest (a few hundred thousand dollars a year); bank fees, fraud losses and compliance dominate, so batch withdrawals and push cheaper payment rails.

11. Interview Q&A

How do you guarantee a transfer is applied exactly once?

We cannot get exactly-once delivery from a network, so we build at-least-once with idempotent effects. The client sends a unique idempotency key, and the API inserts it into a table with a unique index in the same transaction that moves money. A retry hits the constraint and gets the stored reply instead of a second movement. Inside a saga, each leg has a key derived from the transfer id (T:1 and T:2) with a unique constraint, so recovery re-running a leg changes nothing. Event consumers keep a last-applied sequence number, so duplicate deliveries are harmless.

Why a double-entry ledger instead of updating two balance columns?

Double entry is self-checking: entries sum to zero, so money cannot appear or vanish without a visible imbalance. Because entries are append-only, we can rebuild any balance at any past moment, which auditors need, and inserts avoid the lost-update problem of overwriting one row again and again. Corrections are reversing transactions, so history shows the mistake and the fix. We still keep a cached balance for fast spend checks, but it is derived and verified every night against the entries.

Most transfers cross shards. How do you keep them atomic without two-phase commit?

We split the transfer into two local transactions linked by a saved transfer record. Leg 1 on the sender's shard debits the sender and credits a transit account on that shard, balanced by itself. Leg 2 on the receiver's shard debits that shard's transit account and credits the receiver. Each leg has its own idempotency key and survives crashes because a recovery worker resumes from the stored state. If leg 2 is refused for good, a compensating transaction returns the money. The "pending" state is visible for a short time, but no money is lost or created, and the global check on transit accounts proves it.

Leg 2 times out. Do you compensate?

No. A timeout means the outcome is unknown. If leg 2 actually committed and we compensate, the money is paid twice: once to the receiver and once back to the sender. We retry leg 2 with the same key, which either returns the original committed result or does it once. We compensate only after a definite stored refusal or an explicit operator decision, and meanwhile alert on how long transfers sit in transit. The same rule holds for bank calls: unknown is a state, not a failure.

How do you stop two concurrent spends from overdrawing a wallet?

The balance check and the debit must be one atomic step, otherwise two spends of 80 dollars from a 100 dollar wallet both pass. We use a conditional update that only subtracts when posted minus held is at least the amount, and treat zero updated rows as insufficient funds. The database serializes updates to the same row, so the second spend sees the lower balance. A check constraint that available stays non-negative is a backstop even if application code is wrong. When one transaction touches two accounts we lock in account-id order to avoid deadlocks. The cache is never used for this decision.

How do you handle a very hot account, like the fee account or a huge merchant?

Ledger inserts are cheap; the contention is the single cached balance row that every transaction must update. We split a hot logical account into N sub-accounts (say 16), pick one at random per transaction, and sum them on read, using snapshots to keep that cheap. For fees we go further: the fee goes to a per-shard pending sub-account and a batch job sweeps it to the main account, so the user transaction never touches the global hot row. Normal user wallets stay as single rows, because one person rarely spends concurrently.

What does reconciliation check, and why is it needed if the system is idempotent and transactional?

Idempotency and transactions defend against failures we thought of. Reconciliation catches the rest: a bank return we never received, a bug in a deploy, a manual fix gone wrong. Internally it checks invariants: all entries sum to zero, each cached balance equals its entries, transit totals equal in-flight transfers, no user is negative. Externally it matches our records with bank and processor files by provider reference and checks that total wallet balances do not exceed the safeguarded cash. Differences become exceptions fixed by new balanced entries, which leaves an audit trail. The match rate is itself a health metric, with a target above 99.9%.

Where does event sourcing fit, and what does it cost?

The ledger entries are already an event log, and we publish events through a transactional outbox, so history, statements, notifications and analytics are projections that can be rebuilt by replay. We deliberately do not make the spend decision a pure replay. It reads a transactional balance row, so the check is strong and local, while read models are eventually consistent. The costs are projection lag (hidden by showing pending items right after a write), the need for idempotent projectors using per-account sequence numbers, and snapshots to bound replay time. In return we get new views cheaply and can repair a bad projection without touching the ledger.

How do you add shards without downtime?

Accounts hash into a fixed number of virtual buckets, say 1,024, and a directory maps buckets to physical shards. To add capacity we move some buckets: copy the bucket's rows, stream changes until caught up, pause writes for just that bucket briefly, flip the directory entry, and resume. Account placement by bucket never changes, so nothing is rehashed. In-flight sagas keep their leg keys and look up the current owner on each retry, so a leg retried after a move goes to the new shard.

How do you design a withdrawal so money is never lost or paid twice?

Order matters. First reserve the funds with a hold or a debit into a withdrawals-in-flight account, in a local transaction, so the user cannot spend them again. Then write an external-movement row, then call the bank with an idempotency key derived from that row. If the call times out we retry with the same key or query by our reference, and never assume failure. On confirmation we finalize the ledger, moving money from in-flight to bank clearing. If the bank rejects, we release the hold or reverse the debit. Reconciliation against the bank statement is the last check, and large amounts need a second approval.

Why shard by account and not by transfer id?

Sharding by transfer id spreads writes evenly, but one account's entries end up on every shard, so a simple balance check becomes a distributed query. Sharding by account keeps an account's balance, entries, holds and sequence together, so the most important operation, "debit if funds suffice", is one local atomic statement. The price is that most transfers touch two shards, and we pay it with a saga. Because correctness of the spend check matters more than perfect write spread, account sharding wins.

Production incident: transfers are stuck in the debited state. What do you do?

First check the age and count of stuck transfers and which receiver shard they target, because one slow or down shard is the usual cause. If the shard is down, do not compensate; the recovery sweeper keeps retrying with the same leg keys and will finish them when the shard returns. If leg 2 is being refused, check the reason: permanent refusals (closed account) should already be moving to reversing, so a stuck reversing state points to a bug on the sender shard. Alert humans at the 5 minute mark, check the transit-total invariant to confirm no money is lost, and use an approved manual step only when the stored state proves what happened.

What if the wallet held loyalty points instead of real money?

The architecture stays, because the failure modes (double spend, lost update, audit) are the same, but some requirements loosen. There is no bank reconciliation or safeguarding rule, so external matching shrinks to internal invariants. Multi-currency and FX mostly vanish. Targets can lean toward availability, and active-active across regions with a conflict policy becomes more thinkable for low-value balances, though many programs still need exact accounting. I would still keep the append-only ledger and idempotency, since they are cheap and prevent expensive disputes.

Why not a blockchain or a NoSQL store for the ledger?

A permissioned relational database with an append-only table and a hash chain gives the audit properties we need without the cost of consensus between parties that already trust one operator. The regulator and the bank are counterparties, not peers on a chain. An eventually consistent NoSQL store lacks multi-row atomic transactions and unique constraints, which are our two main safety tools; we would have to rebuild them in application code, which is exactly where money bugs hide. We use NoSQL only for rebuildable read views.
