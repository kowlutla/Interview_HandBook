Chapter 1 · Module 0 · Orientation

How to Approach a Design Interview

A system design interview is a structured conversation, not an exam with one right answer. This chapter explains what the interviewer is really judging, gives you a repeatable eight-step flow with a time budget, shows how to draw and narrate a diagram, and walks the whole framework once on a small, complete example.

1. What a design interview actually evaluates

Most engineers prepare for a system design interview by memorizing architectures: "a URL shortener uses a key-value store and a counter", "a chat system uses WebSockets and a message queue". That preparation helps, but it misses what the interviewer is looking at. The interviewer has one hour and a blank whiteboard (or a shared canvas) and wants to answer a single question: "If I put this person in a room with a vague business problem and a team, would they lead the team to a sound design?"

Real design work is never handed to you as a complete specification. Someone says "we need to let customers share photos with friends", and an engineer has to discover the real requirements, choose which things matter, make tradeoffs under uncertainty, and communicate the result so that other people can build it. The interview is a compressed simulation of exactly that. So the interviewer is watching your process at least as closely as your final diagram.

Most companies score the interview on a handful of signals. The names differ between companies, but the substance is the same:

  • Problem exploration. Do you ask questions that expose what the system must do and how big it must be, or do you start drawing boxes immediately? Strong candidates treat the prompt as deliberately incomplete.
  • Structured thinking. Is there an order to what you do? Can the interviewer always tell where you are in the problem and where you are going next? A candidate who jumps between caching, then the API, then sharding, then back to requirements is hard to follow and hard to score.
  • Technical depth. When the conversation reaches one component (the database, the queue, the cache), do you understand how it really works, including what goes wrong? Depth is shown by explaining mechanisms, not by naming products.
  • Breadth and judgement. Do you know the standard building blocks, and can you choose between them for a stated reason? Saying "I would use Kafka" is weak. Saying "I would use a log-based queue because consumers need to replay events, and the cost is that ordering only holds within a partition" is strong.
  • Tradeoff reasoning. Every design choice gains something and loses something. The interviewer wants to hear both halves, tied to the requirements you agreed on at the start.
  • Communication and collaboration. Can you explain a design clearly, take a hint gracefully, and accept disagreement without becoming defensive? The interviewer is imagining what it would be like to design with you every week.

1.1 There is no single right answer

Because the problem is open ended, two excellent candidates can produce quite different designs for the same prompt. One might choose a relational database and another a wide-column store, and both can pass, provided each can justify the choice against the requirements and describe what the choice costs. Conversely, a candidate who draws a textbook architecture for the wrong problem (for example, a globally replicated system when the requirements say "one office, 200 users") fails, because they did not listen to the requirements they themselves collected.

Key idea You are not being asked "what is the answer?". You are being asked "how do you decide?". Every step of the framework in this chapter exists to make your decision process visible: you gather requirements so that decisions have a basis, you estimate so that decisions are proportionate, and you state tradeoffs so that the interviewer can see you weighed alternatives.

1.2 The interviewer is on your side (usually)

A common misconception is that the interviewer is an adversary who is hiding a trap. In most well-run interviews the interviewer has a rubric, wants you to succeed, and will give hints when you drift. If they interrupt with "what happens if that server dies?", they are usually opening a door for you to show depth, not announcing that you are wrong. Treat questions as invitations. If you disagree, say so politely and explain why; senior interviewers value a candidate who can hold a position with evidence and also change it when the evidence changes.

2. Requirements: functional, non-functional, and how to ask

The first activity in every design is to establish what you are building. Skipping this is the single most common reason strong engineers do poorly: they start designing a solution to a problem they imagined, not the one the interviewer had in mind.

2.1 Functional requirements

Functional requirements describe behaviour: the things users or other systems can do with the product. They are verbs. For a photo sharing service they might be "upload a photo", "view a friend's photos", "follow another user". For a ride-hailing system: "request a ride", "match with a nearby driver", "track the driver on a map", "pay at the end". You want a list that is short and prioritized, usually three to five core features. The interviewer does not want you to design the entire product; a real company's app has hundreds of features, and an hour allows only a few.

Good practice is to say the list aloud, and also state what you are excluding: "I will leave out user registration, payments, and the recommendation algorithm; I can come back to them if time permits." This shows that scoping is a deliberate act.

2.2 Non-functional requirements

Non-functional requirements (sometimes called quality attributes or "-ilities") describe how well the system must behave, rather than what it does. They are the main driver of architecture, because two systems with identical features but different non-functional requirements look completely different. A notes app for ten users and a notes app for ten million users do the same things and share almost no design. The most useful non-functional dimensions are:

DimensionWhat it meansQuestion to ask
ScaleHow many users, requests, and how much data. Usually expressed per second and per day."How many daily active users, and what is the read-to-write ratio?"
LatencyHow fast a single operation must complete, often stated at the 99th percentile (p99): the speed that 99 out of 100 requests meet."Is a response within 200 ms needed, or is a few seconds acceptable?"
AvailabilityThe fraction of time the system works. 99.9% allows about 8.8 hours of downtime per year; 99.99% allows about 53 minutes."If the service is down for ten minutes, how bad is that?"
ConsistencyWhether all users see the same data at the same moment, or whether a short delay is tolerable."If I post and my friend refreshes immediately, must they see it?"
DurabilityWhether data, once accepted, must never be lost."Can we ever lose an acknowledged write?"
Security and privacyWho can see what, and what regulations apply."Is the data sensitive or regulated?"
Cost and operationsBudget limits and how much operational complexity the team can handle."Is this a startup with two engineers or a large platform team?"

Not every dimension matters for every problem, and part of your skill is to pick the two or three that will actually shape the design. A payment system is dominated by consistency and durability. A live video chat is dominated by latency. A log collector is dominated by write throughput and cost.

Interview tip Whenever you ask about a number, offer a default so that the interviewer can simply say "yes". For example: "I will assume around 100 million daily active users, read-heavy at about 100 to 1, and a page load under 300 ms at p99. Does that sound reasonable?" This keeps the conversation moving and shows that you can reason about scale.

2.3 How to ask clarifying questions well

Clarifying questions are a skill with a few rules. First, ask questions whose answers change the design. "Should the system support photos and videos?" changes storage and delivery. "What colour is the logo?" does not. Second, group your questions rather than firing a long list: ask about features first, then scale, then quality. Third, listen for the interviewer's emphasis; if they answer one question at length, that area is probably where they want depth. Fourth, when the interviewer says "you decide", that is not a dodge, it is a test of whether you can make a reasonable assumption and state it.

A reliable set of opening questions to adapt to any prompt:

  1. Who are the users and what is the main use case? Consumers on phones, other services calling an API, internal staff?
  2. What are the three to five features that must exist? And which are explicitly out of scope?
  3. What is the scale? Users, requests per second, data volume, growth.
  4. Is it read heavy or write heavy? This single fact drives caching, storage and replication choices.
  5. What are the latency expectations? Interactive (hundreds of milliseconds), near-real-time (seconds), or batch (minutes to hours)?
  6. What are the consistency and durability needs? Can data be a little stale? Can anything be lost?
  7. Where are the users? One region or worldwide? This introduces multi-region concerns.
  8. Any special constraints? Existing systems to integrate with, compliance rules, device limits.

3. Scoping and stating assumptions

Requirements gathering never ends with a complete picture. The interviewer will answer some questions and say "up to you" to others. What you do with the gaps is itself a signal. The professional move is to write the assumption down and say it out loud, so that it becomes a shared fact that you and the interviewer can both refer back to.

3.1 Why stated assumptions matter

An unstated assumption is dangerous in two ways. If it is wrong, the interviewer may think you misunderstood the problem, when in fact you only guessed differently. And if you silently rely on it, you cannot later justify a decision by pointing at it. A stated assumption turns a guess into a design input. Compare these two sentences:

  • "I will use a cache." The interviewer cannot tell why.
  • "We assumed the feed is read about 100 times for every post written, and slightly stale data is fine, so I will cache the feed for 30 seconds." The interviewer can see the logic and challenge any link in it.

3.2 A practical way to scope

Use a three-bucket method and say it aloud:

  1. Must have (in scope). The core path that defines the product. You will design this fully.
  2. Nice to have (mention, maybe deep dive). Features you will touch on if time allows, such as notifications or search.
  3. Out of scope. Things you explicitly defer, such as billing, admin tools, or machine learning ranking.

Then keep a small "assumptions" list at the corner of your canvas. Add to it as you go. When you reach the wrap-up (section 7), you can read through it, which signals thoroughness and invites the interviewer to correct anything important.

Watch out Do not scope away the hard part. If the prompt is "design a chat system" and you declare group chat, delivery guarantees and offline users out of scope, there is nothing left to discuss. Scope to a size that fits the hour, but keep the part of the problem that makes the problem interesting. If you are unsure, ask: "Is group chat part of what you want to see, or should I focus on one-to-one messaging?"

3.3 Reading the problem type

After requirements, quickly classify the system, because the type points you to a known set of concerns. A read-heavy system (a news site, a product catalogue) leans on caching, replicas and content delivery networks. A write-heavy system (metrics collection, logging) leans on append-friendly storage, batching and queues. A real-time system (chat, multiplayer games) leans on persistent connections and low-latency routing. A data-processing system (analytics, recommendations) leans on batch and stream pipelines. A transactional system (payments, inventory) leans on strong consistency and idempotency. Naming the type aloud is cheap and tells the interviewer that you have a map.

4. A repeatable flow: the eight steps

The most useful thing you can bring into the room is a fixed order of work. When you are nervous, a fixed order frees you from deciding what to do next, so that all your attention goes to the content. The flow below is widely used in one form or another. It is a default, not a law: you can reorder or compress steps when the problem calls for it, but you should always know which step you are in.

4.1 Step 1: Requirements

Covered in sections 2 and 3. The output is a short list of features, the two or three non-functional targets that matter, and a list of assumptions. Take about five minutes. Read your summary back to the interviewer and get a nod before proceeding. That nod is a checkpoint: it makes later disagreement about scope impossible.

4.2 Step 2: Estimation

Estimation, often called back-of-the-envelope calculation, converts the scale requirements into numbers you can design against: requests per second, storage per year, bandwidth, and memory needed for a cache. The goal is not precision. The goal is to learn the order of magnitude, because designs change at orders of magnitude: a system doing 100 requests per second fits on one machine, one doing 100,000 does not, and one doing 10 million needs a fundamentally different structure. Chapter 2 is devoted to this skill, with tables of useful numbers and rounding tricks, so here only the shape is given:

  1. Start with daily active users and actions per user per day to get daily operations.
  2. Divide by about 100,000 (seconds in a day is 86,400, and rounding to 100,000 is close enough) to get average operations per second.
  3. Multiply by a peak factor, commonly 2 to 5, to get peak load.
  4. Multiply object counts by object sizes to get storage; multiply by retention to get total storage.

Say each assumption aloud as you compute. Finish with a conclusion in words ("so this is a few thousand requests per second, which one database cannot absorb for writes but caching can handle for reads"). A number without a conclusion is wasted time.

4.3 Step 3: API design

The API is the contract between the system and its clients. Designing it early forces precision about what the system does and gives you a concrete list of operations to support in the architecture. For each core feature, write one operation: its name or path, the main inputs, and the outputs. A few lines are enough:

POST /v1/photos          body: image, caption       -> 201 { photoId }
GET  /v1/feed?cursor=... (auth user)                -> 200 { items[], nextCursor }
POST /v1/photos/{id}/likes                          -> 204

Mention a few design points: use pagination (cursors rather than page numbers for long lists), idempotency for operations that may be retried (such as payments), authentication, and the choice of protocol (REST for simple resource APIs, gRPC between internal services, WebSockets for push). Chapter 5 covers API design in depth; in the interview you only need the main operations.

4.4 Step 4: Data model

List the main entities and how they relate, then decide what the access patterns are. The access pattern (how data is looked up) matters more than the shape of the data, because it determines the primary key, the indexes, and whether a relational database or a key-value or wide-column store is the right fit. For the photo service: a User entity, a Photo entity with an owner, a Follow relation, and a Like relation. The key access patterns are "get photos by user, newest first" and "get the users that X follows". State which store you would choose and why, in one sentence each: "Metadata in a relational or partitioned store keyed by user; image bytes in object storage, because they are large and rarely modified." Chapter 17 goes into this step in detail.

4.5 Step 5: High-level design

Now draw the architecture: clients, entry points, services, databases, caches, queues, and the arrows showing how a request moves. Walk through each core feature as a numbered sequence ("the user uploads; the request reaches the load balancer, then the upload service, which stores the bytes in object storage and writes metadata..."). Keep it at the level of 5 to 10 boxes. This is the skeleton you will refine; resist the urge to add every possible component now. Section 6 explains how to draw and narrate it.

4.6 Step 6: Deep dives

Choose the two or three parts of the design that are hardest or most important and go deep. Often the interviewer will steer you; if not, choose by asking "where do the non-functional requirements bite hardest?" For a feed, that is the fan-out strategy (do you build each follower's feed at write time or read time?). For a chat, it is message delivery guarantees. For a rate limiter, it is the counting algorithm and where state lives. In a deep dive, explain the mechanism, show alternatives, state their costs, and choose.

4.7 Step 7: Bottlenecks, failure, and scaling

Review your own design critically. Where is the single point of failure? What happens when a database node dies, a cache is cold, a region is lost, or traffic doubles? What is the hot key or hot partition? Which components can be scaled horizontally and which cannot? Naming weaknesses yourself, before the interviewer does, is one of the strongest signals of seniority. Also mention how you would monitor the system: which metrics and alerts show that it is healthy.

4.8 Step 8: Wrap-up

Spend the last minutes summarizing: restate the requirements, the main design decisions with their reasons, the known limitations, and what you would do with more time (for example, "next I would add search, and work out the multi-region story"). This leaves the interviewer with a clean final impression and gives them a chance to point at an area they still want to hear about.

5. Time budgeting for a 45 to 60 minute round

The most common structural failure is running out of time before reaching the interesting parts, by spending 20 minutes on requirements and estimation. The second most common is the opposite: drawing a design with no foundation. A time budget keeps the interview balanced. The numbers below are a guideline for a 45 minute round; multiply by about 1.3 for a 60 minute round, with the extra time going to deep dives.

Step45 min round60 min roundOutput you should leave on the board
1. Requirements and scope5 min5-7 minFeature list, quality targets, out-of-scope list, assumptions
2. Estimation4 min5 minQPS (average and peak), storage, bandwidth, a conclusion
3. API3 min4 min3 to 5 operations with inputs and outputs
4. Data model3 min4 minEntities, keys, store choices
5. High-level design10 min12 minDiagram with main flows narrated

7. Bottlenecks and failure3 min5 minList of risks and mitigations
8. Wrap-up2 min3 minSummary and next steps

5.1 Managing the clock in practice

  • Watch for tarpits. Estimation and database choice are common time sinks. If you notice you have spent three minutes computing something, round it and move on: "about 40 TB per year, close enough".
  • Signpost transitions. Say "I have the requirements and numbers; let me sketch the API and data model, then the architecture." This shows control and lets the interviewer redirect you early if they want to spend time elsewhere.
  • Ask the interviewer to steer. At the end of the high-level design, ask "I can go deep on the feed generation, the storage layer, or failure handling. Which would you like?" This is not weakness; it is a way of aligning your depth with their rubric.
  • Protect the end. If the interviewer is enjoying a deep dive that runs long, spend the last two minutes on a quick summary anyway, even if it means cutting the deep dive short.
  • Keep a visible agenda. Writing the eight step names small at the side of the canvas and ticking them off helps both you and the interviewer.

6. How to draw and narrate a diagram

The diagram is your main shared artifact with the interviewer, and the way you draw and talk through it is evaluated. A good diagram is not decoration; it is a tool for thinking aloud in a form that both people can inspect.

6.1 Drawing conventions

  • Flow left to right (or top to bottom). Put clients on the left, then entry points (DNS, CDN, load balancer, API gateway), then services, then data stores on the right. The eye then follows requests in reading order.
  • Label every box with a role, not just a technology. "Upload service" tells more than "Service A", and "Metadata DB (partitioned by user)" tells more than "DB".
  • Label every arrow with what travels on it, and number the arrows when you narrate a specific scenario. "1. upload photo", "2. store bytes", "3. write metadata".
  • Use consistent shapes. For example, rectangles for services, cylinders for databases, a different shape for queues. Draw dashed arrows for asynchronous flows (a message placed on a queue, handled later) and solid arrows for synchronous calls where the caller waits.
  • Start small. Draw the minimal end-to-end path first (client, server, database), confirm it works, then add components only in response to a requirement ("reads are heavy, so I add a cache here").
  • Leave room. Keep space to add things later. Write neatly. A crowded diagram signals a crowded mind.

6.2 Narrating

Talking while drawing is a skill that needs practice. Three habits make it work:

  1. Say what you are about to draw, then draw it, then say what it does. "Reads are far more common than writes, so I will put a cache in front of the database. It holds recently requested feeds, and a miss falls back to the database." Silent drawing leaves the interviewer waiting; silent thinking looks like being stuck.
  2. Trace a real request. After you finish the sketch, pick one scenario and follow it end to end by pointing: "A user opens the app: the request goes to the load balancer, which picks an app server; the server checks the cache; on a miss it queries the database and stores the result." Then trace a write. This is the quickest way to expose gaps, such as a flow where data has nowhere to go.
  3. Pause for contact. After each major piece, check in: "Does this match what you had in mind?" or "Shall I continue to the write path?" It converts the interview from a monologue to a dialogue and gives the interviewer a chance to steer.
Interview tip If you realize midway that a decision was wrong, say so plainly and fix it: "Actually, with 100 times more reads than writes, polling the database for every feed request will not hold. Let me add a cache." Candidates who correct themselves smoothly score better than those who defend a mistake or hide it.

7. Discussing tradeoffs

Every real design decision gives up something to gain something. The senior habit is to make that exchange explicit. An interviewer cannot see how you think from "I would use Cassandra". They can see it from "I would use a wide-column store because the write volume is high and the access pattern is by key, which it handles well. The cost is that I give up joins and ad hoc queries and accept eventual consistency, which is fine here because a feed can be a few seconds stale."

7.1 A template for a tradeoff statement

Use this four-part pattern until it becomes natural:

  1. Options. "There are two ways to build the feed: compute it when a post is written, or compute it when it is read."
  2. Criteria. "What matters here is read latency and handling celebrity accounts with millions of followers."
  3. Choice and reason. "I would precompute for ordinary users because it makes reads one lookup, but compute at read time for celebrities."
  4. Cost and trigger to revisit. "The cost is extra storage and a more complex read path. If we find most users are inactive, precomputing wastes work and I would revisit."

7.2 Common tradeoff axes

These axes appear in nearly every design. Be ready to place your decision on each and explain it:

AxisOne endOther endTypical deciding factor
Consistency vs availabilityAlways show the latest data; refuse if unsureAlways answer; data may be staleCost of a stale or wrong answer (money versus a like count)
Latency vs throughputHandle each request immediatelyBatch requests for efficiencyInteractive users versus background pipelines
Read cost vs write costPrecompute at write time (fast reads)Compute at read time (cheap writes)Read-to-write ratio

Watch out Avoid false certainty ("X is always better") and also avoid endless hedging ("it depends" with no conclusion). The pattern is: it depends on A and B; here, A is true, so I choose this; if A changed, I would choose that.

9. What senior and lead levels are expected to add

The framework is the same at every level, but the bar for what you do inside each step rises. Interviewers calibrate the expected depth to the role. A rough guide:

9.1 Concrete additions for senior candidates

  • Quantify. Tie every scaling claim to a number. "A single PostgreSQL primary handles a few thousand writes per second comfortably; our estimate is 800, so no sharding yet."
  • Think in failure modes. For each component, state at least one way it fails and the mitigation: replicas, retries with backoff, circuit breakers, timeouts, graceful degradation.
  • Raise the non-obvious. Hot keys, thundering herds after a cache flush, idempotency under retries, data skew in partitions, clock drift in ordering, backward compatibility during deployments.
  • Discuss operability. Metrics, logs, traces, alerts, deploy and rollback strategy, and how you would test the system.
  • Evolve the design. "Version one is a monolith with one database. At ten times the load we shard users; at a hundred times we extract the feed service." Showing a growth path demonstrates experience with real systems.

9.2 Additional expectations for leads

At a lead or staff level, you are also assessed on organizational judgement. That means considering which parts of the system to build and which to buy, how to split the design among teams so that interfaces are clear, how to roll it out safely (feature flags, canary releases, migrations with no downtime), what the cost is per user, and what the biggest uncertainty is and how to retire it early (for example, a prototype or load test). You do not need to lecture on management; folding these considerations naturally into your design decisions is enough.

10. Worked mini example: a page-view counter service

Here is the whole framework applied to a deliberately small problem, so that every step fits on the page. The prompt: "Design a service that counts page views for websites, so that site owners can see how many views each page has had." Think of the small counter widgets and analytics dashboards that sites embed. The text below shows what you would say and write at each step.

10.1 Step 1: Requirements (about 5 minutes)

Questions you would ask, with the answers the interviewer gives:

  • "Who calls the service?" Pages embed a small script that sends one event per view to our service; site owners open a dashboard.
  • "What must owners see?" Total views per page, and views per page per day for the last 30 days. Unique visitors, bot filtering and geography are out of scope.
  • "How big is it?" About 1 billion view events per day across all customers, and about 50 million distinct pages.
  • "How exact must counts be?" Dashboard totals may lag by up to one minute, and small errors (under 1 percent) are tolerable, but we should not lose large amounts of data.
  • "How available must it be?" Event collection must almost never reject events (99.95 percent), because lost events are lost forever. Dashboard availability matters less.

Stated summary:

  • Functional: (1) record a page view; (2) read the total for a page; (3) read daily counts for a page for the last 30 days.
  • Non-functional: write-heavy collection, up to one minute of staleness, approximate counts acceptable within 1 percent, high availability for ingestion, durable once acknowledged.
  • Out of scope: unique visitors, bot filtering, geography, authentication details, billing.

Read this back to the interviewer. The type of system is clear: write-heavy ingestion with a smaller read path, so queues, batching and pre-aggregation will matter.

10.2 Step 2: Estimation (about 4 minutes)

Following the method in section 4.2 (and in detail in Chapter 2):

  • Write rate. 1 billion events per day divided by about 100,000 seconds per day is roughly 10,000 events per second on average. Traffic is uneven over the day, so assume a peak of three times, about 30,000 events per second.
  • Raw storage. Each event stores a page id (8 bytes), a timestamp (8 bytes), and a few other fields, call it 100 bytes. 1 billion times 100 bytes is 100 GB per day, about 36 TB per year. If we keep raw events for 7 days for replay and correction, that is about 700 GB, which is small.
  • Aggregate storage. Per page we store a total and 30 daily counts: 31 numbers of 8 bytes is about 250 bytes, plus the key. 50 million pages times about 300 bytes is roughly 15 GB. That is tiny: it fits in memory on one machine, and certainly across a few.
  • Read rate. If site owners view dashboards rarely, say 1,000 dashboard reads per second at peak, reads are 30 times fewer than writes.
  • Ingestion bandwidth. 30,000 events per second times 100 bytes is 3 MB per second, trivial for the network.

Conclusions, spoken aloud: "The hard part is not storage or bandwidth. It is 30,000 writes per second, and in particular a very hot page that might receive thousands of views per second, which would make a single database row a bottleneck. Aggregated data is small, so reads are easy. The design should absorb write bursts in a buffer and aggregate in batches rather than update a counter row for each view."

10.3 Step 3: API (about 3 minutes)

POST /v1/events/pageview
     body: { siteId, pageId, ts }              -> 202 Accepted

GET  /v1/pages/{pageId}/views                  -> 200 { total }
GET  /v1/pages/{pageId}/views/daily?days=30    -> 200 { points: [{ day, count }] }

Two design points to say aloud. The write returns 202 Accepted, not 200, because the event is queued and processed later; the response means "we have taken responsibility for this event", not "the counter is already updated". And the ingestion endpoint is called by many embedded scripts, so it must be cheap, authenticated by a site key, and rate limited per site.

10.4 Step 4: Data model (about 3 minutes)

Two datasets with different access patterns:

  • Raw events (short term). Append-only, never updated, read only by the aggregation job and for replay. Stored in a durable log (a partitioned queue) for a few days, and optionally archived to cheap object storage. No random access needed.
  • Aggregates (long term). A key-value layout: key (pageId, day) to a count, plus a separate total per pageId. The access pattern is "get a page's counts by page id", which a partitioned key-value or wide-column store serves directly. The data is 15 GB, so even a replicated relational database could hold it; the reason to prefer a key-value store is the high rate of increments, which are simple single-key updates with no joins.

10.5 Step 5: High-level design (about 10 minutes)

Start with the minimal path, the way section 6 suggests: script, server, database. Then say why that fails: at 30,000 events per second, with a hot page, updating total = total + 1 in a database for each event will cause lock contention on popular rows, and a database outage would lose events. So add a buffer and an aggregation stage:

Narrating a write. "A browser sends a view event. The load balancer picks an ingest server. The server validates the site key, appends the event to the log, and returns 202. The log is partitioned by page id, so all events for one page land on one partition and are handled in order by one consumer. Every few seconds the aggregator reads a batch, groups events by page and day, and adds one combined increment per page to the counts store. A page with 5,000 views in those seconds becomes one write of +5,000 instead of 5,000 writes."

Narrating a read. "A site owner opens the dashboard. The query service checks the cache; on a miss it reads the page's total and daily counts from the store, which is a single-key lookup, and caches the result for 30 seconds. This fits our allowance of one minute of staleness."

10.6 Step 6: Deep dives (about 15 minutes)

Pick the parts where the requirements bite. Here there are three.

Deep dive A: the hot page

Partitioning the log by page id keeps the events of one page ordered, but a page with enormous traffic would overload one partition. Because we only need counts, ordering within a page does not matter, since addition is commutative (adding in any order gives the same total). So we can spread a hot page across several partitions by partitioning on (pageId, random suffix) for pages flagged as hot, and let several aggregators each add their partial sums; the store applies increments atomically, so the total is correct. The cost: detecting hot pages (a small in-memory frequency counter on the ingest service works), and a slightly more complex routing rule. Normal pages keep the simple key.

Deep dive B: exactness, retries and duplicates

If the browser retries after a timeout, or the aggregator crashes after writing to the store but before recording its progress in the log, the same events may be counted twice. We said 1 percent error is acceptable, but a repeated crash should not drift counts forever. Options:

  • Accept at-least-once delivery and tolerate rare duplicates. Simple, and within tolerance if crashes are rare.
  • Make batches idempotent. Give each aggregated batch an id (partition plus offset range) and store the id with the increment, so that replaying the same batch is detected and skipped. This costs a small extra write and a conditional update, and gives effectively exactly-once counting.

My choice: the second option for daily counts, because duplicates would otherwise accumulate during incidents, which is exactly when numbers are most scrutinized. See Chapter 32 for idempotency techniques.

Deep dive C: the counts store and late events

The counts store receives roughly one write per active page per aggregation interval, not one per view. With 50 million pages but only a fraction active in any few seconds, say 200,000, and an interval of five seconds, that is about 40,000 writes per second at the extreme and typically far fewer, still comfortably within a partitioned key-value store. Late events (a phone that was offline sends yesterday's views today) are handled by using the event's own timestamp to choose the day bucket, with a rule that events older than seven days are dropped, matching the raw log retention. Daily buckets older than 30 days are deleted by a time-to-live setting on the rows.

10.7 Step 7: Bottlenecks, failure and scaling (about 3 minutes)

  • Ingest servers are down or overloaded. They are stateless, so we add more behind the load balancer, and autoscale on request rate. The browser script retries a few times with backoff and a small local buffer.
  • The event log is the single most important component. Replicate each partition across three machines in different failure zones; acknowledge writes only after at least two copies exist. This is the durability promise.
  • The aggregator falls behind. Consumer lag (how far the aggregator is behind the head of the log) is the key metric. If lag grows, add aggregator instances up to the number of partitions. Because the log keeps events for days, the system recovers by catching up and no data is lost, though dashboards become temporarily stale.
  • The counts store is unavailable. Events keep accumulating in the log; after recovery the aggregator catches up. The read path can serve stale cached values meanwhile and show a "data delayed" notice.
  • Whole region loss. If required, replicate the log to a second region and run a standby aggregator; this can be mentioned as an extension, since the requirements gave us 99.95 percent and one region with multi-zone spread meets that.
  • Monitoring. Track ingest success rate, p99 ingest latency, consumer lag, store write errors, and a reconciliation check comparing the number of events accepted with the total counted.

10.8 Step 8: Wrap-up (about 2 minutes)

"To summarize: we built a write-optimized pipeline. Events are accepted quickly and stored durably in a partitioned log; an aggregator folds them into per-page daily counters in batches, which handles a hot page; and a small cached read path serves dashboards within a minute of freshness. Main tradeoffs: we chose slightly stale counts and at-least-once ingestion with idempotent batches, giving up instant exactness in return for resilience and a cheap store. Known limits: unique visitors need a different, probabilistic approach, which is the natural next feature; and we have not covered multi-region. With more time I would load test a hot-page scenario and define the retention policy for archived raw events."

10.9 What made this walkthrough work

Notice the pattern: the requirements gave us the type (write-heavy); the estimation told us which part was hard (writes and hot keys) and which was easy (storage and reads); the API and data model were small and followed from the features; the architecture was grown from a minimal path by answering "what breaks?"; the deep dives targeted exactly the hard parts; and the end summary named the tradeoffs and limits. At no point did we reach for a component without a stated reason.

12. Interview questions and strong answers

You are given a one-line prompt such as "Design a ride-hailing service". What do you do in the first five minutes?

I treat the prompt as deliberately incomplete and spend the first few minutes on questions that change the design. First I ask about users and core features, and narrow to three to five, for example requesting a ride, matching with a driver, live tracking, and payment, and I state what I am leaving out. Then I ask about scale (riders, drivers, requests per second, cities), latency expectations for matching, and what must be consistent (a driver must not be assigned to two riders). Finally I write a summary of features, quality targets and assumptions on the board and read it back, so the interviewer can correct me before I invest time in the wrong direction.

How do you decide which parts of the design to deep dive into?

I choose the parts where the non-functional requirements are hardest to meet, because that is where design decisions are non-obvious. I look at my estimation: whichever number is uncomfortable (a very high write rate, huge fan-out, tight latency, strict consistency) points to the area. I also offer the interviewer a choice of two or three candidates after the high-level design, since they often have a rubric in mind. Within the chosen area I explain the mechanism, list alternatives, state costs, and decide, rather than just describing the component.

What should you do if the interviewer says "assume whatever you like" about scale?

That is an invitation to demonstrate judgement, not a way to skip the topic. I pick a plausible set of numbers, say them aloud, and ask for a nod: for example, "I will assume 50 million daily users and a 100 to 1 read-to-write ratio." Then I carry the numbers through the estimation and let them drive decisions, explicitly noting where the design would change if the assumption were ten times larger or smaller. This shows that I understand the relationship between scale and architecture and that I can make progress under ambiguity, which is the real-world situation.

You realize halfway through that an earlier decision was wrong. How do you handle it?

I say so directly and fix it, because designs evolve in real work and recognizing a flaw early is a strength. I would state what I assumed, what I now see is wrong (perhaps a database cannot take the write rate I later computed), and what I change, then check whether any other part depended on it. Interviewers generally score smooth self-correction well, while hiding the flaw or defending it against evidence scores badly. I also keep the time budget in mind and avoid redrawing everything; I update only what the change affects.

How much detail is right for estimation, and when is it a waste of time?

The right level is order-of-magnitude with rounded numbers, finishing with a conclusion about the design. For example, 1 billion events per day is about 10,000 per second, with a peak of perhaps 30,000, so a single database for per-event updates is out. Estimation becomes a waste when I compute many figures that do not influence any decision, or chase precision such as 86,400 seconds in a day instead of rounding to 100,000. I stop as soon as I know which resources are the bottleneck (writes per second, storage, bandwidth, or memory) and move on, returning only if a later decision needs another number.

How do you talk about tradeoffs without sounding indecisive?

I use a four-part pattern: name the options, name the criteria from the requirements, choose one with a reason, and state the cost and the condition that would make me revisit. For example, "I choose precomputing feeds at write time because reads dominate; the cost is more storage and a harder path for accounts with huge follower counts, so I would treat those differently." This sounds decisive because there is a decision, and it sounds thoughtful because the cost and trigger are explicit. The trap to avoid is answering "it depends" and stopping, without saying what it depends on or which way I lean in this case.

What distinguishes a senior answer from a mid-level answer to the same prompt?

A mid-level answer produces a reasonable architecture with standard components, usually when prompted. A senior answer drives the conversation, quantifies, and explains why each component is there and what it costs. Senior candidates also volunteer failure modes and mitigations (what happens on a node loss, a cache flush, a hot key), discuss operations such as monitoring and rollback, and describe how the design would evolve as load grows rather than jumping to the final form. Leads add organizational concerns such as which parts to build or buy, how to split the work among teams, and how to roll out safely with the smallest risk.

The interviewer interrupts and suggests a different approach than the one you chose. What do you do?

I treat it as information rather than as a verdict. I first make sure I understand the suggestion by restating it, then compare it with mine against the requirements we agreed, naming what each gains and loses. If theirs fits better, I adopt it and say why; if I still prefer mine, I explain the specific requirement that tips the balance and acknowledge the cost of my approach. Either outcome shows collaboration and sound reasoning. What I avoid is immediate surrender without analysis, or defensiveness, since the interviewer is partly testing how I would behave when a teammate disagrees.

When time is running short and you have not finished the design, what do you prioritize?

I prioritize a coherent end-to-end design over completeness of any one piece. If I am behind, I say so and ask the interviewer which area they most want, then give a concise treatment of it, along with a short list of known risks and the next steps I would take. I would rather leave a sound but incomplete deep dive and a clear summary than finish a detailed component and leave the rest of the system undefined. I also never skip the wrap-up: two minutes of summary of decisions, tradeoffs and limits leaves a stronger last impression than one more detail.

13. A pocket checklist

Keep this list in your head during the interview. It is the framework in one pass:

  1. Clarify. Users, features (3 to 5), scale, read or write heavy, latency, consistency, durability, regions. State assumptions.
  2. Estimate. Requests per second (average and peak), storage, bandwidth, memory. End each calculation with a conclusion.
  3. API. One operation per core feature, with pagination, idempotency and protocol noted.
  4. Data model. Entities, access patterns, keys, store choice with one-sentence reasons.
  5. High-level design. Minimal path first; add components for named reasons; trace one read and one write.
  6. Deep dives. Two or three hardest areas: mechanism, alternatives, costs, decision.
  7. Failure and scaling. Single points of failure, hot spots, degradation, monitoring.
  8. Wrap-up. Decisions, tradeoffs, limits, next steps.

14. Recap

  • The interview evaluates your process and judgement: exploration, structure, depth, breadth, tradeoffs and communication, not recall of one correct architecture.
  • Open every problem with functional and non-functional requirements; ask questions whose answers change the design, offer defaults, and state assumptions and exclusions aloud.
  • Follow a repeatable eight-step flow: requirements, estimation, API, data model, high-level design, deep dives, bottlenecks and failure, wrap-up.
  • Use a time budget (about 5, 4, 3, 3, 10, 15, 3, 2 minutes for a 45 minute round) and signpost transitions.
  • Draw the minimal path first, add components only for named reasons, label arrows, and narrate a real read and write through the diagram.
  • State tradeoffs as options, criteria, choice, cost, and a trigger to revisit.
  • Avoid the common mistakes: drawing too early, name-dropping, over or under engineering, ignoring failure, silence, and fighting hints.
  • Senior and lead levels add quantification, failure thinking, operability, evolution paths and organizational judgement.
  • Test any design by tracing each component back to a requirement, as in the page-view counter example.
