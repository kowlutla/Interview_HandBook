NoSQL Families and Choosing a Store

"NoSQL" is not one technology but five or six different families of databases, each built around one access pattern and willing to give up something a relational database offers in return. This chapter explains what each family is, how it stores and scales data, how to model data for it, where it fails, and how to pick a store in an interview by asking the right questions instead of naming your favorite product.

1. Why NoSQL appeared and what it trades

For decades the default answer to "where do I store my data?" was a relational database such as Oracle, MySQL or PostgreSQL. A relational database gives you tables with a fixed schema, rows linked by foreign keys, a powerful query language (SQL) that can join any tables in ways you did not plan in advance, and ACID transactions (all-or-nothing, consistent, isolated, durable updates; see Chapter 18). Those are real, valuable features, and for most applications a single well-run relational database is still the right choice.

Around 2005-2012, companies running very large internet services hit limits that a single relational server could not handle. Four pressures pushed them to build or adopt something different:

  • Write volume and data size beyond one machine. A single database server can only be made so big. Once you need hundreds of thousands of writes per second or hundreds of terabytes, you must spread data over many machines (sharding, see Chapter 25). Relational databases can be sharded, but the things that made them attractive (joins across any tables, multi-row transactions, global unique constraints) become slow or impossible once the rows live on different machines. You end up building a partial database inside your application.
  • Availability across data centers. Amazon's 2007 Dynamo paper described a shopping cart that must accept writes even when parts of the network are broken. A database that insists on strict consistency refuses writes during a partition; a store designed for availability accepts them and reconciles later.
  • Flexible or evolving data shapes. Product catalogs, user profiles and event payloads vary from record to record. Changing a relational schema on a huge table was painful (see Chapter 26), so teams wanted to store a self-describing blob and let each record carry its own fields.
  • Predictable latency for simple access. Most requests at scale are "get the thing with this key" or "append this event". A system specialized for exactly that can deliver single-digit millisecond answers at any size, because it never has to plan a complicated query.

The result was a wave of purpose-built stores: Google Bigtable (2006), Amazon Dynamo (2007), Cassandra and HBase, MongoDB, Redis, Neo4j and many more. The label "NoSQL" was coined loosely to mean "not only SQL" or simply "not relational". Today many of them even support SQL-like query languages, so the name describes a design philosophy, not the absence of a query language.

1.1 What you give up and what you get

The honest summary of the trade is this. A NoSQL store usually removes one or more of these relational capabilities: arbitrary joins, arbitrary ad hoc queries, multi-row or multi-table transactions, strict normalized schemas, and strong consistency by default. In exchange you get one or more of: near-linear horizontal scaling by adding machines, high availability under failure, very low and predictable latency for the access patterns the store was designed for, a data model that matches your objects or your problem (documents, graphs, time series), and cheaper operations at huge scale.

The crucial point is that the trade is not free and not general. Each family bets on a particular access pattern. If your workload matches the bet, the store is spectacular. If it does not, you will fight the database every day. Choosing a store is therefore the exercise of describing your access patterns precisely, then finding the family whose bet matches.

Key idea Do not start from a product name. Start from the access pattern: what are the exact reads and writes, how often, how fast, and how consistent must they be? Relational databases are flexible about queries but hard to scale out. NoSQL stores are inflexible about queries but easy to scale out. You are choosing which inflexibility you can live with.
Interview tip Never say "I would use NoSQL because it scales". Say what specifically you need: for example "writes of 200k events per second, always read by device and time range, so a wide-column store keyed by device fits". Interviewers listen for the access pattern.

2. Key-value stores

A key-value store is the simplest possible database: a giant dictionary. You give it a key (a string or bytes) and a value, and later you ask for the value by key. The store treats the value as an opaque blob (although some, like Redis, understand richer value types). There is no query on the contents of the value. The supported operations are essentially put(key, value), get(key) and delete(key), plus a few extras such as expiry (time to live) and atomic counters.

That simplicity is the whole point. Because every request names the key, the system can compute a hash of the key and know exactly which machine holds it, with no scanning or planning. Reads and writes stay fast as the cluster grows, and sharding is trivial. The cost is that anything other than "look up by key" (find all carts abandoned yesterday, find all sessions for one user) either needs a second structure you maintain yourself or is impossible.

2.1 Redis

Redis keeps the dataset in memory, which makes operations take well under a millisecond. Its values are not opaque: it offers strings, hashes, lists, sets, sorted sets, streams and more, with atomic operations on each (see Chapter 15 for the deep dive). It is used as a cache (see Chapter 13), for session storage, rate limiting counters, leaderboards with sorted sets, queues and locks. It can persist to disk (snapshots and an append-only log) and replicate, and Redis Cluster shards keys over hash slots, but the dataset must fit in memory (or on a tiering layer), which makes it expensive per gigabyte. Treat it as a fast, small, structured working set, and be careful before making it the only copy of important data.

2.2 DynamoDB as a key-value and document store

Amazon DynamoDB is a managed database that is a key-value store at its core but adds a sort key, secondary indexes, transactions and a document-like item model, so it sits between the families. It is serverless from the customer's point of view: you do not manage machines, you create a table and pay for capacity. Its design is so widely tested in interviews that it gets its own section (section 5).

2.3 Riak and the Dynamo lineage

Riak (and the original Dynamo paper that inspired it, plus Voldemort and the early design of Cassandra) is the pure leaderless key-value design. Data is placed on a consistent hashing ring (see Chapter 57), each key is copied to N nodes, any node can accept a write, reads and writes use a quorum of replicas, and conflicting versions that arise during partitions are reconciled using version vectors or application logic. It is the textbook example of an always-writable, eventually consistent store, which is why it appears in the "design a key-value store" case study (Case study 4). Riak itself is now a niche product, but its ideas live in Cassandra and DynamoDB.

2.4 When key-value is right

  • Access is always by a known key: session by session id, cart by user id, feature flags by name, profile by user id.
  • You need very low latency and very high throughput with simple scaling.
  • The value is read and written as a whole, or with simple atomic updates.

It is wrong when you need to query by anything except the key, join across entities, or run analytics. Teams that start with a pure key-value store and later discover they need "list all orders for a customer in date order" end up building secondary indexes by hand, which is the moment to look at DynamoDB (which has them built in) or a document or wide-column store.

3. Document stores

A document store keeps each record as a self-contained document, typically JSON (or its binary form, BSON in MongoDB). A document can contain nested objects and arrays, and documents in the same collection do not have to share the same fields. Unlike a key-value store, the database understands the structure of the document: you can query by any field, project only some fields, update one nested field in place, and build indexes on fields.

The main products are MongoDB (the most widely used), Couchbase (a document store with a built-in memory cache layer and a SQL-like query language called N1QL, now SQL++), and Firestore (Google's serverless document database with real-time listeners that push changes to mobile and web clients, popular for apps built on Firebase). Amazon DocumentDB is a MongoDB-compatible managed service, and DynamoDB items are also document-like.

3.1 The central modeling question: embed or reference

The defining design decision in a document store is where to put related data. If a blog post has comments, you can embed the comments as an array inside the post document, or you can reference them: store comments in their own collection with a postId field, like a foreign key. This is the document-world version of normalization versus denormalization (see Chapter 17).

A practical rule of thumb:

  • Embed when the relationship is one-to-few (an order and its handful of line items, a user and their addresses), the child is always read with the parent, and the child has no life of its own. You get one read, one atomic write, and great locality because the whole document is stored contiguously.
  • Reference when the relationship is one-to-many with a very large or unbounded "many" (a user and their millions of events), many-to-many (students and courses), or when the child is updated much more often than the parent or is shared by many parents.
  • A common hybrid is to embed a summary and reference the detail: store the 3 latest comments inside the post for the fast page render, and keep all comments in a separate collection.

Embedding implies duplication. If you embed the product name and price inside every order line, a later price change does not alter old orders, which for orders is actually what you want (the order should record the price paid). But if you embed the author's display name inside every post, renaming the author means updating thousands of posts. Decide for each duplicated field whether it is a snapshot (leave it alone) or a live value (reference it, or accept a background fix-up job).

3.2 Indexing in a document store

Without an index, a query on a field makes the database scan every document in the collection. Document stores use B-tree style indexes (see Chapter 19) on fields you choose. The index kinds worth knowing:

  • Single-field and compound indexes. A compound index on (customerId, createdAt) serves queries that filter by customer and sort by date, and also queries by customer alone, but not by date alone. Order matters: equality fields first, then sort fields, then range fields is the usual guidance.
  • Multikey indexes index each element of an array field, so you can query "documents whose tags array contains X".
  • Partial and sparse indexes cover only documents that match a condition (for example only active orders), shrinking the index.
  • TTL indexes automatically delete documents after a time, handy for sessions and temporary data.
  • Text and geospatial indexes give basic search and location queries; heavy search belongs in a search engine (Chapter 22).

Every index is extra storage and, more importantly, extra work on every write: a write must update the document and every index that includes the changed fields. Index only the queries you actually run, and drop the others. Check query plans (MongoDB's explain()) to confirm an index is used.

3.3 How document stores scale

MongoDB scales reads by adding replica set members (one primary accepts writes, secondaries replicate from it and can serve reads that tolerate staleness, with automatic election of a new primary on failure). It scales writes and data size by sharding: you choose a shard key, and the cluster splits data into chunks by hash or range of that key and spreads them over shard replica sets, with a router process directing queries. The shard key choice is the most consequential decision, because a key that grows monotonically (like a timestamp or an auto-increment id) sends all new writes to one shard, and a query that does not include the shard key must be sent to every shard (scatter-gather). See Chapter 25. Couchbase similarly hashes keys into virtual buckets spread over nodes. Firestore shards automatically and hides the mechanics, but still penalizes hot keys and sequential ids.

3.4 Transactions and consistency in document stores

A single-document write is atomic in MongoDB, and since 4.0 multi-document ACID transactions exist (also across shards from 4.2), but they cost performance and are meant as an escape hatch, not the main style. The intended design is to shape documents so that the things that must change together live in one document. Read consistency is tunable through read and write concerns: a write concern of "majority" means the write is acknowledged only after most replicas have it, and a read concern of "majority" avoids reading data that could be rolled back after a failover.

3.5 When a document store is right

  • Records are naturally hierarchical and read as a whole: product catalogs with varied attributes, user profiles, content management, game state, event payloads.
  • The schema evolves quickly and varies between records.
  • You need secondary-index queries on several fields, but not heavy multi-entity joins or strict cross-record transactions.
Watch out "Schemaless" does not mean "no schema". The schema simply moves from the database into your application code, where it is enforced less reliably. Plan for versioned documents (a schemaVersion field), use the database's optional validation rules, and migrate lazily on read or with a background job.

4. Wide-column stores

The wide-column family descends from Google Bigtable (2006). Its relatives are Apache Cassandra (originally from Facebook, mixing Bigtable's data model with Dynamo's distribution), Apache HBase (an open-source Bigtable clone on top of Hadoop's HDFS), Google Bigtable itself (a managed service), and ScyllaDB (a Cassandra-compatible rewrite in C++). The name is misleading: these are not column-oriented analytics stores like a data warehouse (see Chapter 23). Think of them as a two-level map: a distributed map from a partition key to a sorted map of rows, where each row can have its own set of columns.

4.1 The data model: partition key and clustering key

This is the single most important idea to be able to explain. In a Cassandra table, the primary key has two parts:

  • The partition key decides which machines hold the data. Cassandra hashes the partition key and uses that hash to place the whole partition on a set of replica nodes. All rows that share a partition key are stored together, on the same nodes.
  • The clustering key (the clustering columns) decides the sort order of rows within a partition. Rows inside a partition are physically stored sorted by the clustering key, so reading a contiguous range of them (the latest 50 messages, all readings between 9:00 and 10:00) is one fast sequential read.

So a query that supplies the full partition key goes straight to the right nodes and reads a sorted slice from one place. A query that does not supply the partition key has no idea where to look and must contact the entire cluster, which Cassandra refuses unless you explicitly allow filtering. That restriction is deliberate: the database only lets you ask questions it can answer efficiently.

4.2 Query-first modeling

In a relational database you model the entities first (normalize), and then write whatever queries you need. In Cassandra you do the opposite: list the queries first, then design one table per query. Because there are no joins and the only fast access is by partition key plus clustering range, every distinct access pattern gets its own table, even if that means storing the same data several times in different layouts. Storage is cheap; cross-node joins are not. This is a deliberate denormalization, and keeping the copies in sync is the application's job (often with a logged batch or an event pipeline).

The steps a strong candidate follows:

  1. Write down every query the application needs, with the exact filters and the required sort order. ("Show a user's last 50 messages in a conversation, newest first.")
  2. For each query, choose a partition key made of the columns the query always filters by with equality. It must spread load evenly and keep each partition a reasonable size.
  3. Choose clustering columns to give the required sort order and any range filters.
  4. Estimate the partition size and the write rate per partition. If a partition can grow without bound or one key is far hotter than others, add a bucket to the partition key (for example a day or a hash bucket).

4.3 Worked example: a chat message store

Requirements: users open a conversation and see the most recent messages, scrolling back for older ones; users also see a list of their conversations ordered by latest activity. Writes are heavy (millions of messages per minute); there are no cross-conversation analytics queries on this path.

Query 1: get the latest N messages of conversation C, older pages on demand. Partition by the conversation and sort by time descending. But a very busy group chat could accumulate millions of messages in one partition forever. So we add a time bucket (for example a month) to the partition key to cap the size:

CREATE TABLE messages_by_conversation (
  conversation_id uuid,
  bucket          text,        -- e.g. '2026-11' (month bucket)
  sent_at         timeuuid,    -- time-ordered unique id
  sender_id       uuid,
  body            text,
  PRIMARY KEY ((conversation_id, bucket), sent_at)
) WITH CLUSTERING ORDER BY (sent_at DESC);

The double parentheses mark the composite partition key (conversation_id, bucket). The clustering key is sent_at, sorted newest first. To load the chat screen you query the current bucket with LIMIT 50; if fewer than 50 rows come back you query the previous month's bucket. The application knows how to compute the bucket from the current time, so reads still go to a single partition.

Query 2: list a user's conversations, most recently active first. That is a different access pattern, so it is a different table:

CREATE TABLE conversations_by_user (
  user_id          uuid,
  last_activity_at timestamp,
  conversation_id  uuid,
  last_snippet     text,
  PRIMARY KEY ((user_id), last_activity_at, conversation_id)
) WITH CLUSTERING ORDER BY (last_activity_at DESC, conversation_id ASC);

Every new message writes to both tables (and updating "last activity" means deleting the old row and inserting a new one, because the clustering key is part of the row identity; this delete-and-reinsert is a known Cassandra pattern and creates tombstones, covered below).

4.4 Why writes are so fast: the LSM tree

Cassandra, HBase and Bigtable all use a log-structured merge tree (see Chapter 19). A write is appended to a commit log on disk (sequential, fast) and inserted into an in-memory sorted structure (the memtable). When the memtable is full it is flushed to an immutable sorted file (an SSTable). Background compaction merges SSTables. There is no read-before-write and no in-place update, so write throughput is excellent, and it scales linearly with nodes. The price is paid on reads (a read may check several SSTables, softened by Bloom filters) and in compaction work in the background.

This design also explains deletes. You cannot erase data from an immutable file, so a delete writes a tombstone, a marker that hides the older value until compaction removes both after a grace period. Workloads that delete or overwrite heavily (queues implemented in Cassandra, rows constantly re-inserted) accumulate tombstones, and reads must skip over them, which can make a partition painfully slow. This is a classic Cassandra anti-pattern.

4.5 Distribution, replication and tunable consistency

Cassandra has no leader. Every node is a peer. The cluster forms a ring; each node owns ranges of the hash space (using virtual nodes, many small ranges per node, so that load spreads evenly and adding a node takes a little from everyone). Each partition is copied to RF nodes (replication factor, typically 3). Any node can receive a client request and acts as the coordinator, forwarding it to the replicas that own the partition.

Consistency is chosen per query with a consistency level. For RF = 3:

  • ONE: the coordinator waits for one replica. Fastest, can read stale data.
  • QUORUM: wait for a majority, 2 of 3. If both writes and reads use QUORUM, then R + W = 2 + 2 = 4 is greater than RF = 3, so the sets of replicas contacted always overlap in at least one node that has the latest write. This gives strong (read-your-writes) consistency for that key without a leader, as long as clocks and timestamps resolve concurrent writes sensibly.
  • ALL: wait for every replica. Strongest, but a single failed node makes the operation fail.
  • LOCAL_QUORUM: a quorum within the local data center only, avoiding cross-region latency while still replicating asynchronously to other regions.

The rule: if R + W > RF, reads see the latest acknowledged write. If you choose ONE for both, you accept eventual consistency in return for lowest latency and highest availability. Concurrent writes to the same cell are resolved by last write wins using timestamps, so clock skew can silently drop a write (see Chapter 27). Replicas that miss a write are repaired by hinted handoff (a coordinator stores the write and replays it later), read repair (a read that sees disagreement fixes the stale replica) and scheduled anti-entropy repair using Merkle trees (Chapter 33). Quorum mechanics are explained fully in Chapter 24.

4.6 HBase and Bigtable differences

HBase and Bigtable take a different route to distribution. Instead of hashing onto a ring, they keep rows sorted by row key across the whole table and split the key space into contiguous regions (HBase) or tablets (Bigtable) that a master assigns to servers. The storage underneath is a distributed file system (HDFS or Google's Colossus), which holds the replicas, so the servers themselves are not replicas of each other: a region has one active serving node at a time, and consistency is strong by default for a single row. Because rows are globally sorted, range scans over the row key are first-class and efficient; the flip side is that monotonically increasing row keys (timestamps) send every write to the last region, a hot spot. The standard fix is to prefix the key with a salt or to lead with an identifier (device id, then time). Bigtable and HBase suit huge analytics-adjacent workloads (time-series, user event history, ad serving features) with row-level atomicity, and they integrate well with batch frameworks. Cassandra suits globally replicated, always-on, write-heavy services where eventual or tunable consistency is acceptable.

4.7 When wide-column is right, and when it is not

  • Right: very high write throughput, data far beyond one machine, access always by a known key plus a range (time-ordered events, messages, sensor readings, activity feeds), multi-data-center active-active, and a team ready to model per query.
  • Wrong: ad hoc queries, many different filter combinations, strong multi-row transactions, small data (a relational database is simpler), workloads with heavy deletes or updates in place, and teams that cannot accept operating a complex cluster (unless using a managed service).

5. DynamoDB design in depth

DynamoDB is the NoSQL store interviewers raise most often, because it is a managed service with a clear, learnable design model. It descends from the Dynamo paper but is a different system: it uses leader-based replication across three availability zones inside a region, and it is fully managed. You do not see servers, only tables.

5.1 Items, keys and how data is placed

A table holds items (like rows), each a set of attributes (like a JSON document, up to 400 KB per item). Every table has a primary key, in one of two forms:

  • Partition key only (a simple key): each item is found purely by its partition key value, as in a key-value store.
  • Partition key plus sort key (a composite key): the partition key chooses the storage partition (by hashing), and the sort key orders items within it. All items with the same partition key form an item collection, stored together and sorted by sort key. This is the same idea as Cassandra's partition and clustering keys.

With a composite key you can fetch one item (GetItem with both keys), or fetch a range inside a partition with Query using a key condition: partition key equals X and sort key between A and B, or begins with a prefix, with ascending or descending order and pagination. A Scan reads the whole table and is expensive; treat it as a tool for batch jobs and not for requests. The core rule of DynamoDB design is that everything the application does online should be a GetItem or a Query.

5.2 Secondary indexes: GSIs and LSIs

Often you must also read the data by a different attribute. DynamoDB provides two kinds of secondary index:

  • A Global Secondary Index (GSI) has its own partition key and optional sort key, completely independent of the table's. DynamoDB copies the items (all attributes or a chosen projection) into the index and keeps it updated automatically. It has its own provisioned capacity. GSI updates are asynchronous (eventually consistent): a read from a GSI may briefly miss a write you just made, and you cannot ask for a strongly consistent read on a GSI. You can add a GSI at any time, up to a quota of 20 by default. If a GSI cannot keep up with writes, DynamoDB throttles writes to the base table.
  • A Local Secondary Index (LSI) has the same partition key as the table but a different sort key. It lets you sort the same item collection a second way. It must be created when the table is created, supports strongly consistent reads, and imposes a 10 GB limit on the size of any one item collection. Because of that limit and the creation-time restriction, most designs use GSIs and avoid LSIs.

5.3 Hot partitions and key design

DynamoDB spreads items over partitions by hashing the partition key. If your key values are evenly distributed and traffic is spread across many of them, throughput scales almost without limit. If traffic is concentrated on a few key values (a celebrity's profile, today's date as a partition key, status = "PAID"), one partition gets too much load and requests are throttled even though the table as a whole has plenty of unused capacity. Each partition can serve roughly 3,000 read capacity units and 1,000 write capacity units per second (approximately). Adaptive capacity and automatic splitting by load help move capacity toward hot items, and a single item hammered by writes still tops out at one partition's limits, so you cannot rely on them for a persistent hot key.

The cures are the same as in every partitioned store:

  • Choose a high-cardinality, evenly accessed partition key (user id, device id, order id), not a date or status.
  • Write sharding: append a random or computed suffix (PAID#0 to PAID#9) to spread one logical key over several physical ones, then read all shards and merge. This trades write throughput for a more expensive read.
  • Cache hot reads in front (DynamoDB Accelerator, or Redis), see Chapter 14 for hot keys.
  • Aggregate counters in memory or a stream and write batches, instead of one write per event to the same item.

5.4 Capacity modes: on-demand versus provisioned

Read and write throughput is measured in capacity units. One read capacity unit (RCU) is one strongly consistent read per second of up to 4 KB (an eventually consistent read costs half, and a transactional read costs double). One write capacity unit (WCU) is one write per second of up to 1 KB (a transactional write costs double). Items larger than the unit cost multiples.

  • Provisioned mode: you declare RCU and WCU (optionally with auto scaling that follows utilization). It is cheapest when traffic is steady and predictable. If traffic exceeds the provisioned amount (beyond a short burst allowance), requests are throttled.
  • On-demand mode: you pay per request and the table adapts instantly to traffic (up to roughly double the previous peak quickly). It suits spiky, unpredictable or new workloads, and costs more per request at steady high volume.

A common pattern is to start on-demand, and move stable high-volume tables to provisioned with auto scaling once the traffic shape is known.

5.5 Single-table design

Because DynamoDB has no joins, an application that needs an order together with its customer and its line items would need several round trips if each entity lived in a different table. Single-table design stores several entity types in one table, using generic key attributes (PK, SK) and carefully chosen key values so that data that is read together shares a partition key and can be fetched with one Query:

PK = "CUST#c77"   SK = "PROFILE"                 (customer record)
PK = "CUST#c77"   SK = "ORDER#2026-11-17#8841"   (an order)
PK = "ORDER#8841" SK = "ITEM#1"                  (a line item)
PK = "ORDER#8841" SK = "ITEM#2"

Querying PK = "ORDER#8841" returns the order's items together, in one call, from one partition. Prefixes in the sort key allow begins_with to select one entity type. GSIs with overloaded keys serve the other access patterns. The benefits are fewer round trips and fewer tables to manage; the costs are a steep learning curve, data that is hard to read by eye, difficulty changing access patterns later (every new pattern may need a new index or a backfill), and awkward analytics. Many teams use a middle path: one table per service or bounded context, a few entity types each. Single-table design is a technique, not a requirement; do not recommend it blindly in an interview. It shines when access patterns are well known and stable, and it is a poor fit when the product is still discovering what queries it needs.

5.6 Transactions, consistency and other features

  • Consistency: reads are eventually consistent by default (cheaper), and you can ask for a strongly consistent read on the base table (not on GSIs). Writes are acknowledged after being durably stored on a majority of the three replicas.
  • Conditional writes (ConditionExpression) make a write succeed only if a condition holds, such as "item does not exist" or "version equals 7". This is the building block for optimistic locking and idempotency (Chapter 32).
  • Atomic counters and update expressions change an attribute in place without reading it first.
  • Transactions (TransactWriteItems, TransactGetItems) give all-or-nothing ACID semantics across up to 100 items, potentially in different tables, within one region. They cost twice the capacity of normal operations, so use them for the few operations that need them (transfer money between two items), not as the default.
  • Streams publish a time-ordered change log of every item modification, used to trigger functions, maintain materialized views, or feed other stores (section 11).
  • TTL expires items by a timestamp attribute, deleting them in the background at no write cost.
  • Global tables replicate a table across regions in active-active fashion with last-writer-wins conflict resolution (see Chapter 26).
  • Backups: point-in-time recovery and on-demand backups.
Watch out Do not quote exact current quotas (the number of GSIs, transaction size, partition throughput) as if fixed; AWS adjusts them. In an interview say "approximately" and focus on the design consequence, such as "one key value is bounded by one partition's throughput".

6. Graph databases

Some data is mostly about the relationships between things: who follows whom, which accounts share a device, which products are bought together, which router connects to which. A graph database stores data as nodes (entities) and edges (relationships between them), and is built to follow those edges quickly. The dominant model is the property graph: nodes and edges both carry a type label and a set of key-value properties, and edges are directed. Example: a node (:Person {name: "Asha"}), an edge -[:FOLLOWS {since: 2024}]->, and another person node. The main products are Neo4j (with the Cypher query language), Amazon Neptune (supports Gremlin and openCypher, plus the RDF triple model with SPARQL), JanusGraph and TigerGraph.

6.1 Why graphs beat joins for some questions

Consider "find friends of friends of Asha who like jazz and are not already her friends". In SQL you join a friendship table to itself, once per hop. Each join is a lookup in an index over the whole friendship table, whose cost grows with the table's total size, and the intermediate result explodes as hops increase. Three or four hops over millions of rows becomes slow or unusable.

A native graph database uses index-free adjacency: each node stores direct references to its neighbours. To expand from a node you follow its own references, which costs time proportional to that node's number of neighbours, not to the size of the whole graph. A four-hop traversal touches only the part of the graph reachable from the start node. That is why deep, variable-length traversals ("is there any path of length up to 6 between these two accounts", "shortest path") are the sweet spot.

6.2 Where graphs are used

  • Social networks and recommendations: friends of friends, "people you may know", paths used for collaborative filtering.
  • Fraud and security: rings of accounts sharing devices, cards or addresses; attack paths through a network.
  • Knowledge graphs and master data: entities and their typed links, identity resolution.
  • Dependency and topology: service dependencies, impact analysis, routing, permission inheritance.

6.3 Limits and scaling

Graphs are the hardest family to scale horizontally. A traversal wants all connected data close together, but any partitioning of a highly connected graph cuts many edges, so a traversal that crosses machines becomes slow. Neo4j classically scales reads with replicas (each holding the full graph) and writes through one leader cluster; sharding very large graphs is possible but is a design project of its own. Systems at massive scale, such as Facebook's TAO, build a purpose-made graph layer on top of sharded relational stores with heavy caching, restricting queries to one or two hops. Supernodes (one node with millions of edges, such as a celebrity) make traversals expensive and need special handling, such as filtering edge types or capping fan-out.

Interview tip Most "friends" problems in interviews (followers, feeds, mutual friends) do not need a graph database. A follower table keyed by user in a sharded store handles one-hop queries, and mutual friends is a set intersection. Reach for a graph database only when the requirement is multi-hop or path-finding over relationships, such as fraud rings or shortest path.

7. Time-series databases

A time-series database is specialized for data that arrives as a stream of (timestamp, value) points identified by what they measure: CPU usage per host, temperature per sensor, price per stock symbol, request count per endpoint. The access pattern is distinctive: writes are almost always appends of new data, data is almost never updated, reads ask for a time range ("last 6 hours") often with aggregation ("average per minute"), and old data matters less than new data.

7.1 The model: series, labels and points

A series is identified by a metric name plus a set of labels (also called tags): http_requests_total{service="cart", region="eu", status="500"}. Each distinct combination of label values is a separate series, and each series holds an ordered list of (timestamp, value) points. Prometheus, InfluxDB, TimescaleDB (a PostgreSQL extension that partitions tables by time), VictoriaMetrics, OpenTSDB and Amazon Timestream follow this shape. They compress time data heavily: timestamps are stored as deltas of deltas and floating-point values with XOR-based encoding, so well-behaved series typically cost only a byte or two per point.

Cardinality is the main hazard. The number of series is the product of the label value counts. Using a user id, request id or full URL as a label creates millions of series, bloating the index and memory and slowing every query. Labels must have a bounded, modest set of values; high-cardinality identifiers belong in logs or traces, not in metric labels.

7.2 Downsampling and retention

Keeping raw per-second data for every metric forever is expensive and rarely needed. Two techniques manage this:

  • Retention policy: raw data is kept for a window (for example 15 days), and older partitions are dropped wholesale. Because storage is partitioned by time, dropping a day means deleting a file, not millions of row deletes.
  • Downsampling (rollups): a background job aggregates raw points into coarser ones (min, max, average and count per 5 minutes, then per hour) and stores them as separate, longer-retained series. A dashboard over the last year reads the hourly rollup (about 8,760 points per series) rather than 31 million raw points. The trade is lost detail: you cannot recover a spike shorter than the rollup interval, so keep max and percentile approximations in the rollup, not only the average.

Storage is usually an LSM tree or a time-partitioned block layout, sharded by series identity or time range, with writes batched in memory then flushed. This chapter treats time-series stores only at the level needed to choose among stores; engine internals and the neighbouring OLAP and vector stores are covered in Chapter 23, and how metrics feed alerting is in Chapter 54.

Wide-column stores (Cassandra with a device-and-day partition key, or Bigtable with a device-then-time row key) are also common time-series back ends when you need massive write rates and simple range reads, particularly for IoT where each device's history is read on its own. A purpose-built time-series database wins when you want rich aggregation functions, downsampling, retention and a time-aware query language out of the box.

Two patterns recur. First, the unit of atomicity is the unit of partition: a single row, document, item collection or partition is cheap to update atomically because it lives on one machine. The moment an operation spans partitions, you either pay for a distributed transaction (two-phase commit, slow, see Chapter 31) or you design around it with sagas (Chapter 48). Second, stronger guarantees cost latency and availability: waiting for a quorum or a cross-region round trip adds milliseconds, and during a network partition you must choose between refusing requests and serving possibly stale data (CAP and PACELC, Chapter 28).

Key idea When you cannot avoid a multi-entity invariant (never oversell stock, a balance must not go negative), make sure the data involved shares a partition key so one atomic operation covers it, or keep that part of the system in a relational store. Pushing such invariants into application code on top of an eventually consistent store is the most common cause of subtle money and inventory bugs.

10. Choosing a store: the decision framework

Choosing a database is not a ranking of products; it is matching a workload to a design. Ask these questions, roughly in this order, and write the answers down before naming anything.

  1. Access pattern: exactly what are the top 3 to 5 reads and writes? By key only? By key and range? By several attributes? By relationships? Aggregated over time? This question eliminates most candidates.
  2. Consistency and transactions: what must be correct immediately (payments, inventory, uniqueness) and what can be slightly stale (view counts, feeds, recommendations)? Do you need multi-entity atomic updates?
  3. Scale: data size now and in two years, reads and writes per second at peak (see Chapter 2), read-to-write ratio, growth rate. A few hundred GB and a few thousand requests per second fits on one PostgreSQL server; do not over-engineer.
  4. Latency: is the target single-digit milliseconds at the 99th percentile (a key-value or in-memory store), or is 100 ms acceptable (almost anything)? Is it a user request path or a batch job?
  5. Query flexibility: will product requirements add unknown queries (favoring relational or document stores with rich indexes, or a search engine), or are the queries fixed (favoring partition-key-first stores)?
  6. Availability and geography: single region or several? Must writes succeed during failures and in multiple regions (leaderless or global tables), or is a single leader with failover enough?
  7. Team skill and operations cost: does the team know this system? Is a managed service available (DynamoDB, Atlas, Keyspaces, Bigtable), or will you run it? A well-understood PostgreSQL beats an exotic system your team cannot debug at 3 a.m.

11. Polyglot persistence and keeping stores in sync

Polyglot persistence means using several different databases in one system, each for the job it does best. An e-commerce platform might hold orders and payments in PostgreSQL, the product catalog in MongoDB, search in Elasticsearch, sessions and carts in Redis, clickstream in Kafka plus a warehouse, and images in object storage. Each store is chosen for its access pattern, which is the premise of this whole chapter.

The cost is that data now lives in several places, and they must agree. A product edit in the catalog must reach the search index. A new order must update statistics. There are three good ways to keep stores in sync, and one to avoid.

  • Change data capture (CDC): a tool such as Debezium reads the database's own transaction log and publishes every committed change as an event to a log like Kafka (Chapter 34). Downstream consumers update the search index, cache or warehouse. Because the log is the database's own, no change is missed and no application code has to remember to publish. See Chapter 36.
  • Native change streams: DynamoDB Streams, MongoDB change streams and Cassandra CDC give a similar ordered feed per partition, typically consumed by a function that updates other stores.
  • Transactional outbox: the service writes the business change and an "event to publish" row in the same local transaction, and a relay publishes the outbox rows. This gives reliable publishing without CDC on the whole database (Chapter 31).
  • Avoid dual writes: having the application write to database A and then to search B in one request. If the second write fails or the process crashes between them, the stores disagree indefinitely, and two racing requests can apply their writes in different orders. Use CDC or an outbox instead.

Derived stores are eventually consistent, so the user experience must tolerate a short delay (a new product appears in search a second later). Monitor lag, make consumers idempotent (Chapter 32) and keep the ability to rebuild a derived store by replaying the log or re-reading the source. In microservices each service owns its store (Chapter 46), which is polyglot persistence at the organizational level. The warning: every added database is another system to deploy, back up, secure, monitor and staff. Add one only when a real access pattern justifies it.

12. Common mistakes

12.1 Using MongoDB like SQL

The classic failure is normalizing data into many collections and joining them in application code or with $lookup, because that is what the team knows. The result has none of the benefits of a relational database (cheap joins, constraints, a mature planner) and none of the benefits of documents (one read for a whole aggregate). A variant is dumping data in without indexes and then wondering why queries scan millions of documents. The remedy is to model around the application's read patterns, embed what is read together, and index the queries that matter. If the data is truly relational and join-heavy, use a relational database.

12.2 Hot partitions

A hot partition arises when a disproportionate share of traffic hits one partition key: a time-based key where all current writes share today's value, a celebrity or very large tenant, a low-cardinality attribute such as status or country, or a sequential id under range partitioning. The partition's node saturates and throttles while the rest of the cluster idles, and adding nodes does not help because the hot key cannot be split. Prevent it with a high-cardinality key, key salting or write sharding, bucketing (user plus day), caching reads, and monitoring per-partition metrics rather than cluster averages. See Chapter 25.

12.3 Unbounded partitions

In Cassandra, DynamoDB and similar stores, a partition that grows forever is a time bomb. In Cassandra, partitions beyond roughly 100 MB (or hundreds of thousands of rows) typically make reads, compaction and repair slow and memory hungry. In DynamoDB, a table with an LSI caps each item collection at 10 GB, and any single partition key holding an enormous list becomes hard to read and cannot be split. In MongoDB, an array that grows without bound pushes a document toward the 16 MB limit and rewrites it repeatedly. The fix is to bound growth with time or hash buckets in the key (the (conversation_id, bucket) pattern in section 4.3) and to archive or expire old data with TTL.

12.4 Secondary index costs

Secondary indexes feel free until they are not. In DynamoDB each GSI duplicates data, uses its own write capacity and multiplies write cost: with three GSIs one item write may cost four writes, and an undersized GSI can throttle writes to the base table. In Cassandra, native secondary indexes are local to each node, so a query using one without the partition key contacts many nodes and scales poorly; prefer a query table. In MongoDB every index slows every insert and update that touches indexed fields and uses RAM that the working set also needs. In all of them a low-cardinality index (a boolean) rarely helps. Index deliberately and review unused indexes.

12.5 Other frequent mistakes

  • Choosing by hype: picking a distributed store for 50 GB of data. Operational simplicity has real value.
  • Ignoring tombstones: using Cassandra as a queue, or deleting heavily, which causes slow reads.
  • Scans in production: a DynamoDB Scan or a Cassandra ALLOW FILTERING query on a hot path.
  • Assuming consistency you did not configure: reading from a GSI, a replica or at ONE and expecting the latest value.
  • Last-write-wins surprises: concurrent updates silently losing data; use conditional writes, versions or CRDTs (Chapter 59) where it matters.
  • No plan for new queries: a query-first model is brittle to new requirements; know the cost of a new access pattern (a new table or index plus a backfill).

13. Migration considerations

Moving between stores, or from relational to NoSQL, is a project in its own right, and the principles of Chapter 26 apply. Points specific to NoSQL:

  • Remodel, do not port. Copying relational tables one-to-one into a document or wide-column store carries the old shape into a system that rewards a different shape. Start from the access patterns, design the new model, then write the transformation.
  • Migrate with dual running, not a big bang. A common order: (1) backfill historical data with a batch job; (2) capture ongoing changes with CDC so the new store stays current; (3) run shadow reads, comparing results between old and new; (4) shift reads gradually to the new store; (5) switch writes with the old store following for a rollback window; (6) retire the old one. Application dual writes are risky for the reasons in section 11.
  • Plan for lost features. Foreign keys, unique constraints beyond the key, joins and multi-row transactions must be replaced by application logic or a different design. Inventory every query and constraint the old system provided.
  • Schema evolution inside NoSQL. With flexible documents old and new shapes coexist; version them and migrate lazily on read or by a background job. In Cassandra adding a column is cheap, but changing the primary key means a new table and a backfill.
  • Capacity during the migration. Bulk loads can throttle a live DynamoDB table or flood a Cassandra cluster with compaction work; rate-limit the backfill and consider temporarily raising capacity.
  • Verification. Compare counts and checksums per partition, not only totals, and test failure and rollback paths before cut-over.
  • Migrating out of NoSQL is just as common (teams discovering they needed joins). The same dual-running method works in reverse, and a replayable change log makes either direction easier.

14. Interview walkthroughs: pick the database

The pattern for each: state the access patterns, estimate scale, choose, justify against alternatives, then note what you give up and how you would mitigate it.

14.1 Scenario A: a shopping cart for a large retailer

Requirements: 20 million active users, peak 50,000 cart operations per second, cart read by user id, always available (a cart that errors during a sale loses money), items added and removed frequently, carts expire after 30 days of inactivity.

Access pattern: get the cart by user id, replace or update it, delete it after expiry. No cross-user queries, so key-value with TTL fits. Choice: DynamoDB (managed, partition key = user id, TTL attribute for expiry, on-demand capacity for sale spikes, global tables if multi-region), or Redis with persistence if carts are treated as short-lived and latency is paramount. A cart is one aggregate read whole, so a key-value or document shape works. Consistency: a user must see their own latest cart (read-your-writes), so use strongly consistent reads on DynamoDB or route to the leader in Redis; a second of staleness across devices is acceptable. Checkout converts the cart into an order that lives in a relational store with transactions and inventory constraints, so this is polyglot: key-value for carts, relational for orders. Risks: a hot key if a bot hammers one user id (rate limit, Chapter 16) and cart growth (cap items per cart).

14.2 Scenario B: chat message history

Requirements: 500 million users, 30 billion messages per day, always read as "latest messages in a conversation, then older on scroll", retained for years, multi-region users.

Access pattern: append-heavy writes and a sorted range read inside one conversation. 30 billion per day is about 350,000 writes per second on average, far above one relational primary. At roughly 200 bytes per message that is about 6 TB per day before replication, well over 2 PB per year. Choice: a wide-column store (Cassandra, ScyllaDB, HBase or Bigtable) with partition key (conversation, time bucket) and clustering by message time, exactly the table from section 4.3. Use LOCAL_QUORUM within a region and asynchronous cross-region replication. Add-ons: a per-user conversations table for the inbox, Redis for presence and unread counters (Chapter 6), object storage for attachments (Chapter 21), and Elasticsearch if message search is required. Risks: huge group chats creating hot partitions (smaller buckets, cap very large rooms), tombstones from deletes (use TTL or soft-delete flags), and last-write-wins on edits (store edits as new versions).

14.3 Scenario C: fraud detection on payments

Requirements: flag a transaction if its card, device, address or IP is linked, through shared attributes, to accounts previously marked fraudulent within 3 hops, with an answer inside 100 ms at checkout.

Access pattern: multi-hop traversal over relationships between accounts, cards, devices and addresses. In SQL this means repeated self-joins on large tables whose cost grows with every hop. Choice: a graph database (Neo4j or Neptune) for link analysis, populated from the payments system of record (relational) by CDC. The payment itself stays in the relational ledger because it needs ACID. Precompute risk features asynchronously (size of the connected component, number of known-fraud neighbours) into a key-value store such as Redis or DynamoDB, so the online path does a key lookup instead of a live deep traversal, and run the live traversal only for borderline cases. Risks: supernodes (a shared public Wi-Fi IP linking thousands of unrelated accounts, so cap or down-weight them), graph freshness (CDC lag), and write scaling.

14.4 Scenario D: a metrics platform for 50,000 hosts

Requirements: every host reports about 200 metrics every 10 seconds; dashboards show recent and historical charts; alerts evaluate within a minute; raw data kept two weeks, trends for two years.

Numbers: 50,000 hosts × 200 metrics gives 10 million series; one point per series every 10 s gives 1 million points per second. At roughly 1.5 to 2 bytes per compressed point that is about 130 to 170 GB per day raw, so two weeks is around 2 TB before replication. Choice: a time-series database (a Prometheus-compatible system such as VictoriaMetrics or Mimir, or InfluxDB or Timescale), sharded by series, with two replicas and long-term blocks in object storage. Downsample to 5-minute and then hourly rollups (min, max, average, count) for the two-year view. Label discipline: host, service and region are fine; a request or user id must never be a label. Alternatives considered: Cassandra with device-and-day partitions would store the data, but you would rebuild aggregation, downsampling and a query language; a single relational database would not sustain a million points per second. Risks: cardinality explosions (enforce per-tenant series limits), query fan-out over long ranges (serve from rollups), and alert evaluation depending on the store's availability (run a highly available pair).

15. Interview Q&A

Why would you choose NoSQL over a relational database, and when would you not?

I choose NoSQL when a specific requirement exceeds what a single well-tuned relational database offers, and I can name it: write volume or data size beyond one node, a need for multi-region active-active writes, a data model that naturally fits documents or graphs, or a fixed access pattern needing predictable millisecond latency at any size. I do not choose it just because it sounds scalable. If the data is a few hundred gigabytes with varied queries and transactional invariants, PostgreSQL is simpler, cheaper to operate and more flexible. I would also avoid NoSQL when the product is still discovering its queries, since query-first stores make new access patterns expensive.

Explain partition key versus clustering (sort) key, and why it matters for performance.

The partition key is hashed to decide which nodes hold a piece of data, so all rows sharing it live together. The clustering or sort key orders rows within that partition on disk. A query that names the partition key goes to the right replicas and reads a sorted contiguous slice, which is fast and bounded. A query without it must contact every node. So the partition key should spread load evenly and keep partitions a manageable size, while the clustering key encodes the read order and range filters. A wrong partition key yields hot or unbounded partitions that no amount of added hardware can fix.

What is query-first modeling and how does it differ from relational modeling?

In relational design you model entities and relationships once in normalized form, and then any query can be answered with joins. In Cassandra or DynamoDB you list the application's queries first and design a table or index for each, with the partition key matching the equality filters and the clustering key matching the sort order. The same data may be stored several times in different layouts, so the application or a pipeline must keep the copies consistent. You gain predictable latency and linear scale, and you lose flexibility, since a new query often means a new table plus a backfill.

How does tunable consistency work in Cassandra, and what does R + W > N mean?

Each partition is replicated to N nodes, say 3. For each operation the client picks how many replicas must respond: W for writes and R for reads. If R + W is greater than N, every read set intersects every write set in at least one replica holding the latest acknowledged write, so reads see it. With QUORUM for both on N = 3, that is 2 + 2 = 4 > 3. Using ONE for both gives the lowest latency and highest availability but only eventual consistency. Higher levels cost latency and reduce availability when replicas are down. Concurrent writes still resolve by timestamp, so last-write-wins can lose an update unless you use conditional writes.

What is a hot partition in DynamoDB and how do you avoid it?

DynamoDB spreads items across partitions by hashing the partition key, and each partition handles only limited throughput, roughly 1,000 writes and 3,000 reads per second. If traffic concentrates on a few key values, such as a date, a status or a celebrity user, that partition throttles while the table's total capacity sits unused. To avoid it I pick a high-cardinality, evenly accessed key, add a random or computed suffix to spread one logical key over several (write sharding) and merge on read, cache hot reads, and batch counter updates instead of writing the same item per event. I also watch per-partition throttling metrics rather than only table totals.

GSI versus LSI in DynamoDB: what are the differences?

A global secondary index has its own partition and sort key, can be added at any time, has separate capacity, and is updated asynchronously, so reads from it are eventually consistent only. A local secondary index shares the table's partition key and offers an alternative sort key, must be defined when the table is created, supports strongly consistent reads, and limits each item collection to 10 GB. Because of those restrictions most designs use GSIs. Both multiply write cost, since each indexed write also updates the index, so I add only indexes that serve known queries.

When would you embed versus reference in MongoDB?

I embed when the child data is bounded, read with its parent and belongs to it, such as order line items; this gives one read and an atomic write. I reference when the many side is large or unbounded, shared by several parents, or updated independently, such as a user's events or many-to-many relations, because embedding would grow documents toward the 16 MB limit and force heavy duplication. A hybrid works well: embed a small summary, like the latest few comments, and keep the full set in another collection. The decision is driven by read patterns and growth, not by an abstract normalization rule.

Do graph databases scale horizontally, and when are they worth using?

They scale reads well with replicas but struggle to partition writes, because a highly connected graph cannot be cut without severing many edges, and traversals that cross machines are slow. They are worth using when the core requirement is multi-hop relationship queries or path-finding, such as fraud rings, recommendations over several degrees, or dependency impact analysis, where SQL needs expensive repeated self-joins. For one-hop queries like follower lists, a normal sharded table is simpler and scales better. Large companies often build custom graph layers over sharded stores with caching and limited hops.

How do you keep a search index or cache in sync with your primary database?

I make the primary database the single source of truth and derive the other stores from its change log. Change data capture reads the database's transaction log and publishes each committed change to a stream such as Kafka, and consumers update Elasticsearch or the cache. A transactional outbox gives a similar guarantee without CDC. I avoid dual writes from the application, because a crash or failure between the two writes leaves the stores inconsistent, and concurrent requests can interleave. Consumers should be idempotent, lag should be monitored, and the derived store must be rebuildable by replay, accepting that it is eventually consistent.

How would you migrate a service from a relational database to DynamoDB or Cassandra with no downtime?

First design the new model from the access patterns instead of copying the tables. Then backfill historical data in rate-limited batches to avoid throttling. Capture ongoing changes with CDC so the new store tracks the old one. Run shadow reads that compare responses to find mismatches, then shift a percentage of reads and finally all reads to the new store. Switch writes with a rollback path, keeping the old store updated for a while, and only then decommission it. Throughout, replace lost features such as joins, constraints and multi-row transactions in application logic, and verify with per-partition counts and checksums rather than only totals.

What problems do many tombstones cause, and how do you avoid them?

In LSM-based stores like Cassandra, a delete writes a tombstone marker instead of removing data, and the marker persists until compaction after a grace period. A read has to scan over tombstones in the partition before reaching live data, so a partition with many deletions, such as a queue implemented as a table or rows repeatedly overwritten in a clustering range, becomes slow and can even fail queries after a tombstone threshold. I avoid modeling queues in Cassandra, use TTL with time-bucketed partitions so whole buckets expire together, avoid frequent overwrites of clustering keys, and monitor tombstone counts per read.

16. Recap

  • NoSQL is a set of families, each a bet on an access pattern, trading away joins, ad hoc queries, multi-row transactions or strong consistency to gain horizontal scale, availability, latency or a better-fitting model.
  • Key-value stores (Redis, DynamoDB, Riak) serve lookups by key; document stores (MongoDB, Couchbase, Firestore) hold nested records with rich indexes, where embed versus reference is the key decision; wide-column stores (Cassandra, HBase, Bigtable) use a partition key to place data and a clustering key to sort within it, and are modeled query-first.
  • Cassandra scales through consistent hashing, leaderless replication and tunable consistency (R + W > N for strong reads); HBase and Bigtable partition by sorted key range.
  • DynamoDB design revolves around partition key, sort key, GSIs and LSIs, avoiding hot partitions, choosing on-demand or provisioned capacity, optionally single-table design, and using transactions sparingly.
  • Graph databases win on deep traversals and struggle to shard; time-series stores optimize labeled append-only points with downsampling and retention, and must guard against label cardinality.
  • Atomicity usually ends at the partition boundary; keep invariants inside one partition or in a relational store.
  • Choose by asking about access pattern, consistency, scale, latency, query flexibility, availability, team skill and ops cost; default to relational unless a specific requirement says otherwise.
  • Polyglot persistence needs one source of truth and CDC or an outbox for derived stores, never dual writes.
  • Common mistakes: SQL-style modeling in MongoDB, hot and unbounded partitions, careless secondary indexes, scans on hot paths, and big-bang migrations.
