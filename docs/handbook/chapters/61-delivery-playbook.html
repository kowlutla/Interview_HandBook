Interview Delivery Playbook

Knowing the right architecture is only half of a system design interview. The other half is delivery: how you ask questions, structure your time, draw, argue tradeoffs, handle failure, and react when you are pushed or stuck. This chapter explains what interviewers actually score and gives you phrases, timelines, and a full annotated transcript so you can practise the behaviours, not just the content.

Chapter 1 gave you the step-by-step framework (requirements, estimates, API, data model, high-level design, deep dives, wrap-up). This chapter assumes you know those steps and focuses on how to perform them out loud, under time pressure, in front of another person. Content knowledge lives in the other chapters; Chapter 60 collects the recurring patterns, and the cheat sheets Numbers and Decision Tables give you quick reference during practice.

1. What interviewers actually assess

A system design interview has no single correct answer, so interviewers cannot grade it like a coding test with passing and failing cases. Instead, most companies give interviewers a rubric of several dimensions, and the interviewer writes down evidence for each one while you talk. The final decision comes from that written evidence. Understanding the dimensions tells you what to make visible. A strong idea that you did not say out loud produces no evidence, and no evidence means no credit.

The six dimensions below appear under different names in different companies, but nearly every rubric contains all of them.

1.1 Problem navigation

Problem navigation means moving through an open-ended problem without getting lost. The interviewer watches whether you begin by clarifying scope, whether you build a rough end-to-end design before polishing any single part, and whether you notice which part of the design is hardest and spend time there. A candidate who spends twenty minutes choosing the exact database schema for a URL shortener, and never reaches the redirect path, has failed to navigate even if every schema decision was correct. Evidence the interviewer writes down: "had an agenda", "got to a complete design by minute 20", "chose a sensible deep dive".

1.2 Technical depth

Depth means you can go one level below the box on the diagram. Saying "we use a cache" is breadth. Saying "we use a cache-aside Redis cluster, keys are the short code, TTL of 24 hours with jitter, and on a node failure about one sixth of keys remap, so the database must absorb a spike" is depth. The interviewer is looking for internal mechanics, sensible numbers, and awareness of what breaks. Depth is not name-dropping; it is being able to answer the second and third "why" about anything you wrote.

1.3 Tradeoff reasoning

Every design decision gives something and costs something. Interviewers want to hear that you know both sides. The signal is not that you picked the same technology they would pick; it is that you compared options against the stated requirements and could say what would change your mind. Section 8 gives language templates for this.

1.4 Communication

Communication covers voice, structure, and the whiteboard. A design may be excellent yet unscorable if the interviewer cannot follow it. Good communication means short signposted chunks ("first the write path, then the read path"), defined terms, a diagram that agrees with what you say, and thinking aloud rather than in silence.

1.5 Collaboration

The interview simulates working with a colleague on a design review. The interviewer asks themselves, "Would I enjoy designing with this person?" A collaborator asks questions, checks that the interviewer agrees before moving on, takes hints gracefully, and pushes back with reasons rather than defensiveness. A candidate who talks at the board for forty minutes without a single check-in, or who treats every interviewer question as an attack, scores low here no matter how good the architecture.

1.6 Handling ambiguity

The prompt is deliberately vague ("design a news feed"). The skill being tested is converting vagueness into decisions: ask the questions that matter, state assumptions for the rest, and proceed. Equally important is remaining flexible when the interviewer changes a requirement halfway through. Section 11 covers this.

Key idea Interviewers can only score what they hear and see. Make your reasoning visible: say the question you are answering, say the options, say the choice and why. Thinking that stays in your head earns nothing.

2. The rubric by level

The same answer can pass at one level and fail at another. A design that is fine for a mid-level engineer, "put a cache in front of the database and shard when it gets big", is thin for a staff engineer, who is expected to explain how the team would roll it out, what could go wrong in production, and how it fits the organisation. Interviewers calibrate against the level you are applying for, so know where the bar sits.

2.1 What "lead" adds that "senior" does not

A senior engineer is expected to design a system that works. A lead or staff engineer is expected to design the path to that system from where the organisation is today. In practice this appears as four extra behaviours. First, you describe phases: what ships in the first month, what waits, and what you deliberately do not build yet. Second, you talk about people: which team owns which service, where the boundary between teams lies, and how a change by one team can break another. Third, you discuss risk: how you would roll out safely, how you would detect failure, and what the rollback is. Fourth, you make decisions about build versus buy with a cost argument, not a preference. Section 14 collects these into a checklist.

3. Clarifying questions that matter

The first few minutes set the quality of everything after. A clarifying question is worthwhile only if the answer changes the design. "Should it be fast?" changes nothing, since the answer is always yes. "Is a read allowed to be a few seconds stale?" changes the whole caching and replication story. Before asking any question, check that you can say how each possible answer would alter your design. If not, skip it.

Group your questions into six families so you do not forget one and do not wander.

3.1 Sample opening scripts

Here is how a clear opening sounds for the prompt "Design a news feed". Notice that it is short, structured, and finishes by confirming scope instead of waiting for permission.

"Before I start, I'd like to spend two or three minutes on scope.
 On functionality: I'm assuming users can post, follow others, and
 see a ranked home feed. Is that the core, and can I leave out
 ads, search, and notifications?
 On scale: could I assume about 100 million daily users, each
 reading the feed around ten times a day and posting once?
 On consistency: is it acceptable if a new post takes a few seconds
 to appear in followers' feeds?
 On latency: I'll aim for the feed to load in under 300 ms at p99.
 If that sounds right, I'll proceed with those and flag anything
 that changes as we go."

And a second example for a more infrastructure-flavoured prompt, "Design a rate limiter".

"A few questions that decide the design. Is this limiter inside one
 service, or a shared service for many? Do we limit per user, per
 IP, per API key, or all three? When the limiter itself is down, do
 we fail open and let traffic through, or fail closed? And is
 approximate counting acceptable, for example off by a few percent,
 or must limits be exact?"
Watch out Do not ask ten questions in a row like an interrogation, and do not ask questions whose answers you are only going to ignore. Five or six good ones, grouped, followed by a summary of what you will assume, is the right shape. Equally, do not ask zero questions: jumping straight to boxes signals that you build before you understand.

3.2 When the interviewer says "you decide"

Often the interviewer answers a question with "whatever you think is reasonable". This is not a trap; it is the ambiguity test. Pick a value, say it aloud, and give a one-line reason: "I'll assume 100 million daily users, because that is a large consumer product but not the very largest, and I'll note that if it were ten times that, I'd revisit the storage layer." You have turned a missing fact into a recorded assumption and shown the sensitivity of your design to it.

4. Stating assumptions explicitly

An assumption is a fact you are choosing to treat as true so that you can move forward. Stating it aloud does three things. It protects you: if the interviewer disagrees, they will say so now, not after you have designed around the wrong number. It shows maturity, because real design work always rests on assumptions that later turn out wrong. And it gives you a place to return to when the interviewer changes a requirement, since you can say, "That breaks assumption three, so here is what changes."

A practical way to do this is to keep a small assumptions list in a corner of the whiteboard and number each item as you add it. For example:

Assumptions
 A1. 100M daily active users, 10 feed reads per user per day
 A2. Read-heavy: roughly 100 reads for every write
 A3. Feed may be seconds stale; own posts must appear immediately
 A4. Single primary region first; second region later
 A5. Posts are text plus an image URL; media stored elsewhere

The phrase pattern is: "I'm going to assume X, because Y; if X is wrong, then Z changes." The last clause is the valuable part, because it demonstrates that you understand which parts of the design depend on which facts. Keep assumptions few. A list of fifteen assumptions looks like stalling. Five to eight that cover scale, consistency, and scope are normal.

Good habit Whenever you catch yourself about to say "obviously" or "of course", stop and ask whether the interviewer has agreed to that fact. If not, it is an assumption and deserves a sentence.

5. Driving the conversation instead of following it

There are two bad extremes. At one extreme, the candidate waits to be told what to do next, answering each question narrowly. The interviewer ends up designing the system by questioning. At the other extreme, the candidate monologues for twenty minutes without letting the interviewer in. The target is in the middle: you set the direction, and the interviewer steers by exception. Three tools make this possible: agenda-setting, signposting, and check-ins.

5.1 Agenda-setting

After clarifying questions, spend ten seconds telling the interviewer your plan. It costs almost nothing and shows structure immediately.

"Here's how I'd like to use the time. First, a quick estimate so we
 know the scale. Then the API and data model. Then a high-level
 design that handles the main flows. After that I'd like to deep dive
 into the part I think is riskiest, which I suspect is feed
 generation, and finish with failure modes and scaling. Does that
 work for you, or is there somewhere you'd rather I spend more time?"

The final question matters. It invites the interviewer to redirect early, which is much better than discovering at minute 35 that they wanted to hear about the ranking service.

5.2 Signposting

Signposts are short phrases that tell the listener where you are in the story. They let the interviewer relax and follow instead of guessing. Useful ones:

  • Opening a topic: "Now I'll look at the write path." "Let me switch to the read path."
  • Summarising: "So far we have a stateless API tier, a sharded store, and a cache. That covers the happy path."
  • Parking an issue: "Notification fan-out is a big topic; I'll note it and return to it if we have time, because the feed itself is the harder part."
  • Transitioning: "That finishes the basic design. I want to stress-test it next: what happens if the cache goes down?"
  • Marking a decision: "This is a key decision, so let me compare two options."

5.3 Check-ins

A check-in is a short question at a natural pause: "Does this match what you had in mind?", "Should I go deeper here or move on?", "Is there a part you would like me to justify more?" Check in at the end of each major phase, not after every sentence. Roughly four to six times in an interview is right. Each check-in gives the interviewer a cheap way to give a hint or redirect without it feeling like a correction, which is also a collaboration signal.

Interview tip If the interviewer interrupts with a question, answer it directly and briefly, then return to your thread with a signpost: "Good point, short answer is X. Back to the read path, where I was about to..." Interruptions are not failures; they are the interviewer showing you where they want evidence.

6. Time boxing a 45 and a 60 minute interview

Most interviews are 45 or 60 minutes of which the first few are introductions and the last few are your questions for the interviewer, so the real design time is typically 35 to 50 minutes. Without a plan, candidates run out of time in the same predictable way: they spend too long on estimates and the first diagram, then rush the deep dive and skip failure handling entirely. A time budget prevents that. The proportions below are guides, not rules; adjust them to the prompt and to the interviewer's interest.

6.1 What happens in each box

  • Clarify (5 to 6 minutes). Scope, scale, consistency, latency, constraints. End with a stated list of assumptions and a one-line agenda.
  • Estimate and API (7 to 8 minutes). Back-of-the-envelope requests per second, storage, bandwidth (see Chapter 2), then the two or three core endpoints and the main entities. Keep the arithmetic short; the goal is to find which resource is the bottleneck, not to be exact.
  • High-level design (12 to 15 minutes). Draw the simplest design that meets the requirements, walk the main flows with numbered arrows, then name the first obvious bottleneck.
  • Deep dives (13 to 18 minutes). Choose the riskiest component or the one the interviewer points to. Go into data structures, algorithms, protocols, and tradeoffs (section 8).
  • Failure and scaling (about 8 minutes). Walk through what dies, slows, or lies (section 9), plus a short capacity and growth check.
  • Wrap (2 to 5 minutes). Summarise the design in three sentences, list what you would do next or what you deliberately left out, and ask whether there is anything to revisit.
Watch out Keep an eye on the clock without staring at it. A useful rule is the halfway checkpoint: when half the time is gone, you should have a complete end-to-end design on the board. If you do not, stop adding detail and finish the whole path first. A complete shallow design can be deepened; an unfinished detailed one cannot be rescued.

6.2 Adapting on the fly

Interviewers sometimes jump straight to a deep topic ("Let us talk about how you would shard this"). Go with it, since it is the thing they want evidence on, but briefly say what you are skipping: "I'll take that now; I'll come back to the API and the failure story if there is time." Your agenda is a default, not a contract.

7. Whiteboard and diagramming technique

The diagram is your shared workspace with the interviewer and the evidence they will look at when writing notes. A clean diagram makes a mediocre explanation survivable; a messy one makes a good explanation hard to credit. Whether the tool is a physical whiteboard, a shared drawing canvas, or a document, the same principles apply.

7.1 Layout

  • Flow left to right. Clients on the left, then edge and API layers, then services, then data stores on the right. Readers expect this and will follow it without help.
  • Keep a reserved corner for the assumptions list and the estimates, so the main area stays for the architecture.
  • Leave room. Do not fill the board from the first minute. You will add caches, queues, and replicas later, and cramming them in makes the diagram unreadable. Leave at least a third of the space empty after the first version.
  • One diagram per question. If a component needs its own detail (for example, how the feed ranking pipeline works), draw it separately next to the main diagram and label it, instead of overloading the main one.

7.2 Naming

Give every box a name that says what it does, not what product it is: "Feed Service", "Post Store", "Fan-out Queue". Product names can be added in a smaller second line when you choose them ("Post Store: sharded SQL"). Names matter because you will refer to them aloud many times, and consistent names let the interviewer track your story. Avoid generic labels such as "Service A", "DB", and "Cache 2", which force the listener to remember what each one is.

7.3 Numbering the flow

When you explain a request, put numbers on the arrows in the order the steps happen, and speak those same numbers: "One, the client sends the request. Two, the load balancer picks a server. Three, the service checks the cache." The numbers turn a drawing into a story and also prove that your diagram and your words agree. Use one number sequence per flow, and write the flow name beside it ("Read path", "Write path").

7.4 Evolving the design in iterations

Do not draw the final architecture in one go. Draw it in iterations, and say why each one appears. This is both easier to follow and a demonstration of good judgement, because each addition is justified by a named bottleneck. The method is: start with the simplest thing that could work, find where it breaks under the estimated load, add exactly one mechanism to fix that, and repeat.

7.5 Starting simple, then scaling by bottleneck

The first version of your design should be almost embarrassingly simple: clients, one service, one database. Say so: "I'll start with the simplest thing so we have something correct, and then scale it where the numbers say it breaks." Then use the estimates from earlier to find the first breaking point. For example, if peak load is 20,000 reads per second and one database node handles about 5,000, you have a measured reason to add a cache and replicas. If writes are 2,000 per second and one primary handles 10,000, you do not need sharding yet, and saying so is a strong signal: it shows you do not add machinery without evidence. Cheat sheet: Numbers has the typical per-node figures to quote.

7.6 Showing data flow and data at rest

Besides request flows, mark where data lives and how it moves between stores. Use solid arrows for synchronous calls, dashed arrows for asynchronous ones (queue, stream, replication), and label stores with their main key and size: "Post Store, key = post_id, about 5 TB". When data moves asynchronously, note the expected delay: "replica lag typically under a second". This one habit prevents most consistency questions from catching you off guard.

Interview tip Talk while you draw, but not only while you draw. Writing silently for thirty seconds is dead air. Say what you are about to draw, draw it, then turn to the interviewer to say why it is there.

8. Tradeoff language and deep-dive selection

8.1 The tradeoff sentence

Tradeoffs are the single most important thing to verbalise, and many candidates make decisions silently. A reliable template has three parts: what the option gives, what it costs, and why you still choose it for this problem.

"X gives us ..., costs us ..., and I'd pick it because ..."

Examples:

  • "Asynchronous replication gives us fast writes and cross-region reach, but costs us possible lost writes on failover, a few seconds of staleness at worst. For a feed, that is acceptable, so I'd pick it."
  • "A cache in front of the store gives us single-digit-millisecond reads and cuts database load by about 90 percent at an expected 90 percent hit rate. It costs us invalidation complexity and stale data. I'd pick it because the workload is 100 to 1 reads to writes and stale data for a few seconds is fine."
  • "Sharding by user id gives us even write distribution and keeps one user's data together; it costs us cross-user queries, which become scatter-gather. Because our queries are almost always per user, I'd pick it."

8.2 Quantify the choice

Numbers turn an opinion into an argument. When comparing two options, attach even a rough number to at least one dimension: latency, throughput, storage, cost, or operational effort. "Redis" versus "database" is a preference; "about 0.5 ms versus about 5 ms per read, so for a page that needs 20 lookups it is 10 ms versus 100 ms" is reasoning. The numbers do not need to be exact. They need to be the right order of magnitude and clearly derived. If you are unsure, say "roughly" and show the arithmetic.

8.3 Name the alternative and why you rejected it

For each major decision, name at least one alternative and give the reason it loses here. This is what separates "I know one tool" from "I chose among tools". Keep it to one sentence: "I considered a graph database for follows, but at our scale a simple table keyed by user id with an index handles it, and the team already operates a relational store." Also say what would flip the decision: "If we needed multi-hop traversals like friends of friends, I'd revisit that." That final sentence is the staff-level touch.

Weak phrasingStrong phrasing
"We'll use Kafka.""I'll use a durable log such as Kafka here because we need replay and several independent consumers; a simple queue would be lighter but loses the replay we need for rebuilding the search index."

8.4 Choosing the deep dive

After the high-level design, the interviewer often says, "Pick an area to go deeper on." The best choice is the riskiest component: the one that is hardest, most likely to fail, or most specific to this problem. Do not pick the easy one to look good. For each candidate component ask three questions: Is this where the scale pressure is highest? Is this where correctness is hardest (ordering, duplicates, consistency)? Is this unique to this prompt, rather than a generic piece found in every design?

  • URL shortener (Case study 1): unique ID generation and the read-heavy redirect path, since everything else is generic.
  • News feed (Case study 6): fan-out on write versus on read, and the celebrity problem.
  • Chat: ordering, delivery guarantees, and presence at connection scale.

Say it aloud: "The riskiest part here is fan-out for accounts with millions of followers, so I'd like to go deep on that." Naming the choice and the reason is itself evidence of problem navigation.

8.5 Going deep without losing the thread

A deep dive can swallow the whole interview and leave the interviewer unsure whether you can still see the whole system. Use a stack discipline: announce the dive ("I'm going down into the fan-out service"), explain it to a satisfying depth, then explicitly come back up ("So that is fan-out; in the whole system it sits between the post store and the feed cache, and the rest of the diagram is unchanged"). Keep a finger on the main diagram and point at where the component sits when you leave it. If the interviewer drags you deeper than you can follow, say what you know and where your knowledge ends (section 11.3), rather than bluffing.

9. Failure walkthroughs: die, slow, lie

The most reliable way to show senior judgement is to walk through failure before the interviewer asks. A weak candidate treats failure as one bullet at the end ("we'd add redundancy"). A strong candidate takes each component on the diagram and answers three questions: what happens when it dies (stops responding), when it slows (still responds, but late), and when it lies (responds with wrong, stale, or duplicate data). These are three different failures with three different remedies, and slowness is usually the most dangerous of them because it does not trigger simple failover.

9.1 A worked example

Take the read path from section 7: client, load balancer, feed service, feed cache, post store. A spoken walkthrough might sound like this.

"Let me stress the read path. If the cache dies, the feed service
 falls through to the post store. The store is sized for the
 miss traffic, not for all reads, so I'd protect it with request
 coalescing and a rate limit on cache-miss reads, and the feed
 degrades to slower but still works. If the cache slows, that is
 worse, because requests pile up waiting. So the service uses a
 50 ms timeout on cache calls, and after a few timeouts a circuit
 breaker skips the cache entirely for a short while. If the cache
 lies, meaning it holds a stale feed after a post is deleted, the
 deletion path writes a tombstone and the read path filters
 deleted ids, so the worst case is a brief delay, not showing
 removed content."

That is about thirty seconds of speech, covers all three failure modes for one component, and ends with a concrete, bounded outcome. Do the same, more briefly, for the queue, the database, and the load balancer. You will not have time for every component, so choose the two or three whose failure hurts most, say so, and give the others one line each. Chapter 40 explains timeouts, retries, breakers, and bulkheads in detail; Chapter 41 covers replication, failover, backup, and recovery objectives (RPO and RTO). The failure checklist cheat sheet is a handy prompt list.

Key idea Always state the blast radius and the recovery of a failure, not only the mitigation. "If this node dies, one shard of users sees errors for about 30 seconds until failover, and no acknowledged writes are lost" is a complete, scoreable statement.

10. Consistency, data, capacity, and the "extras"

10.1 Consistency and data discussions

Whenever data is stored in more than one place (a cache and a database, a primary and a replica, two regions), say what consistency you promise and how. You do not need to quote the CAP theorem in every answer. It is enough to be concrete: "Reads from the replica can lag by up to a second. For the user's own posts I read from the primary for a few seconds after they write, which gives read-your-writes consistency. For everyone else's view, eventual consistency is fine." Mention the unit of data that must be correct together (an order and its line items) and use a transaction or a single-partition write for that. Where two systems must agree and a distributed transaction is too costly, name the alternative (an outbox table with an idempotent consumer, or a saga, section 12).

10.2 Capacity checks

Return to your estimates at least twice: once after the first design to confirm that each component can hold its load, and once after scaling to confirm the numbers still work. A capacity check is a two-line calculation: "Peak is 30,000 reads per second. A cache node handles about 100,000, so three nodes give headroom for loss of one. The database sees only the 10 percent misses, or 3,000 per second, within one primary plus replicas." This shows that your boxes were sized, not decorated. Chapter 2 and the Numbers cheat sheet are the toolkit.

10.3 Security, privacy, cost, and operability without padding

These topics are often listed on rubrics but become noise when recited as a generic list. The right approach is to mention each one only where the design makes it relevant, in one sentence, with a specific hook:

  • Security: "The upload endpoint is public, so it needs authentication, a size limit, and a content-type check before anything reaches the store."
  • Privacy: "Messages contain personal data, so I would encrypt at rest, restrict access by role, and make deletion requests propagate to caches and search indexes."
  • Cost: "Storage dominates cost here, about 500 TB a year, so I'd tier old posts to cheaper storage after 90 days."
  • Operability: "I'd expose three dashboards: request rate and error rate per endpoint, queue depth and age, and replication lag, with alerts on the last two since they warn before users notice."

Four specific sentences are better than a paragraph of "we would also consider security, privacy, scalability, and monitoring". If the interviewer wants more, they will ask.

11. Hints, pushback, not knowing, mistakes, and curveballs

Much of the real evaluation happens in the unscripted moments, because that is where candidates cannot rely on memorised templates. Each moment below has a good response.

11.1 Handling hints

A hint is a gift. When an interviewer asks, "What about the case where two users write at the same time?" they are almost always pointing at a gap. Do not treat it as criticism. Respond in three steps: acknowledge ("Good catch, I hadn't covered concurrent writes"), solve out loud, and incorporate it into the diagram or assumptions. Taking a hint quickly scores better than needing none but ignoring one. Ignoring a hint, or arguing past it, is a serious collaboration red flag.

11.2 Handling pushback

Sometimes the interviewer challenges a decision: "Why not just use a relational database here?" There are two kinds of pushback. One is a genuine flaw they have spotted; the other is a probe to see whether you can defend a sound choice. You often cannot tell which, so treat both the same: restate the concern to confirm you understood, evaluate it honestly against the requirements, then either defend with evidence or concede with a revised plan.

Defend:  "Fair question. At 50,000 writes per second a single
          relational primary becomes the bottleneck, so I'd need
          sharding anyway. That's why I leaned to a partitioned
          store. If the write rate were ten times lower, I'd agree
          with you and keep it relational."
Concede: "You're right, that breaks under the bursty case I hadn't
          considered. Let me change the design: ..."

Never become defensive, and never cave instantly to every challenge either; caving without reasons suggests you had none for the original choice.

11.3 Handling not knowing

You will be asked about something you do not know well. The wrong responses are bluffing and freezing. The right response has three parts. Admit the boundary: "I haven't operated that system, so I won't claim details." Reason from first principles: "But I know what it must achieve: ordered delivery per key and durability. A design that does that would partition by key, replicate each partition, and let consumers track offsets. Is that close to what you have in mind?" Offer to verify: "In real work I'd check the vendor documentation for the exact guarantees before committing." Interviewers value honesty plus structure over false confidence, and they are good at detecting bluffs.

11.4 Recovering from a mistake

Everyone makes errors in a live design: a wrong number, an inconsistent diagram, a decision that violates an earlier assumption. What counts is the recovery. Say it plainly, fix it, check what else it affects, and move on without apologising at length.

Example: "I said 10 GB per day, but 100 million posts at 1 KB is 100 GB per day, so about 36 TB a year. That is ten times my earlier figure, so a single database node is out, and I will shard sooner than I planned." You have corrected the figure and followed the consequence in one breath.

11.5 Handling curveballs and new requirements

Interviewers often change the problem midway: "Now the product must work in three regions", "Now posts must be searchable", "Now one customer sends ten times the traffic". They want to see how flexible your design is and how you reason when the ground shifts. A reliable response sequence:

  1. Restate the new requirement to confirm: "So we now need cross-region reads under 100 ms for users in Europe and Asia?"
  2. Map it to your assumptions list: "That invalidates A4, single region."
  3. Identify which parts change and which do not: "The API tier is stateless and copies easily; the data tier is what changes."
  4. Propose the smallest sufficient change, with the tradeoff: "Async replicate to read replicas in each region; writes still go to the home region, which costs write latency for remote users but keeps consistency simple."
  5. Say what you would defer and what the next step would be if requirements grow again.

The mistake to avoid is redesigning from scratch, which suggests your original design was not grounded in requirements, or stubbornly insisting nothing needs to change.

12. Microservices-specific talking points

Many prompts end with "how would you split this into services?" or involve an existing monolith. Interviewers use this to probe whether you know that microservices are a cost you accept for a benefit, not a default. Strong answers touch these points, each briefly.

  • Why split at all. Independent deployment, independent scaling, isolation of failures, and team autonomy. The cost is network calls, distributed data, harder debugging, and operational overhead. If the team is small or the domain is unclear, a modular monolith is often the right start. Chapter 44 covers migration strategies such as the strangler pattern.
  • Where to draw boundaries. Along business capabilities and data ownership, not technical layers. Each service owns its data; no shared database. Chapter 45 explains decomposition by domain.
  • Synchronous versus asynchronous calls. Use synchronous calls where the user waits for the answer, and events where work can happen later. Say what happens if the downstream service is slow (timeouts, breakers; section 9).
  • Distributed transactions. There are no cheap cross-service transactions. Mention sagas with compensating actions and the outbox pattern for reliable events. Chapter 48 goes deeper. Be ready to say what compensation means for each step, for example refunding a payment when shipping fails.
  • Cross-cutting concerns. Service discovery, an API gateway, centralised authentication, tracing with a correlation id, and per-service SLOs. One sentence each is enough unless asked.
  • Ownership. Name the team that would own each service and what the contract is (API versioning, backward compatibility). This is a lead-level signal.

Also be ready to argue against splitting: "I would keep payments and ledger in one service because they need strong consistency; splitting them forces a saga for something that should be a single transaction." Willingness to not split is a mark of experience. The Microservices checklist summarises these items.

13. Describing real production experience credibly

Behavioural-adjacent questions appear inside design interviews: "Have you built something like this?", "What went wrong the last time you ran a queue in production?" The interviewer wants to separate real experience from textbook knowledge, and they do it by probing for specifics that only someone who did the work would know.

A credible story has a clear shape: context, your role, a decision, the result, and what you learned. Be precise about your role. "We migrated to Kafka" invites the question "What did you personally do?" Say instead: "I designed the partition keying and led the cut-over for the order events; another engineer owned the consumers."

If you only have partial experience, say so and connect it: "I have not run a multi-region database, but I ran cross-zone failover, and the lessons were...". Where it helps, link your story to the design: "In my last system we had exactly this fan-out problem, and what worked was..." This bridges the gap between design and delivery and is especially powerful at senior and lead levels.

14. Level-specific tips for senior and lead candidates

At senior and lead level, interviewers assume you can draw a competent design. They are looking for judgement about how the design lives in the real world. Weave these into the design instead of leaving them for a closing monologue.

14.1 Strategy and phasing

Present the design as phases. Phase 1 is the smallest version that meets the core requirement and can ship quickly. Phase 2 adds scale or reliability features once data justifies them. Phase 3 handles the long tail (multi-region, advanced ranking). For each phase, state the trigger: "We add sharding when the primary exceeds 60 percent write capacity." This shows you can sequence work, avoid over-building, and tie investment to evidence.

14.2 Migration plans

When there is an existing system, the design is a migration, and migration is where projects fail. Describe the steps: run the new system in shadow mode, comparing outputs; migrate a small percentage of traffic; use dual writes or change-data capture with reconciliation; define the cut-over and the rollback. Always say how you would verify correctness before turning off the old path. Link to Chapter 44 for the strangler approach.

14.3 Team and ownership boundaries

Identify who runs each component. A design with six services and one team is a different design from six services and six teams. Mention API contracts, on-call responsibility, and what happens when a dependency's team changes behaviour. Boundaries that match teams reduce coordination cost; boundaries that cut across teams create it.

14.4 Build versus buy

Treat this as an economic decision. Compare total cost (licence or managed fees, versus engineering time to build and operate), time to market, fit to the requirements, lock-in, and the team's skill. A good statement: "A managed queue costs more per message but removes a team's worth of operational burden. Unless we need features it lacks, I'd buy it and spend our effort on the domain logic that differentiates us." Mention the exit path if the vendor fails you.

14.5 Risk and rollout

Describe how a change reaches production safely: feature flags, canary releases to one percent of traffic, automatic rollback on error-rate or latency regressions, and a pre-agreed list of what you would watch. Name the riskiest step and how you reduce it. For data migrations, say that rollback must be possible for as long as practical, and say what the point of no return is.

14.6 SLOs and measurement

Convert vague goals into service level objectives: "99.9 percent of feed reads under 300 ms over 30 days", which implies an error budget of about 43 minutes of full unavailability a month. Say which indicators you would measure, where you would alert, and how the objective constrains the design (a 99.99 percent target pushes you to multi-zone or multi-region and active redundancy, with matching cost). Tie each SLO to a user-visible outcome, not an internal metric.

Interview tip At lead level, finish with a short "what I would worry about" list: two or three real risks, such as a hot-key problem, a migration step, or a staffing dependency, each with its mitigation. It leaves the impression of someone who has been responsible for the outcome.

17. Practice plan guidance

Delivery is a skill like playing an instrument: it improves through repetition with feedback, not through reading. Because your schedule will differ, this section describes how to practise, not when.

17.1 How to practise

  • Practise out loud, standing or at a real board. Silent mental design is far easier than speaking and drawing at once. If you only think a design through, you will discover on the day that you cannot narrate it.
  • Time yourself against the boxes in section 6. Use a visible timer and note which phase overran.
  • Record yourself (audio is enough) and listen for silences, filler words, missing tradeoff sentences, and unexplained jargon.
  • Practise parts separately: ten minutes on only the clarifying opening, or only failure walkthroughs, or only one deep dive. Isolated drills build fluency faster than full runs.
  • Reuse a small library of problems (the case studies in this handbook) and vary the requirements each time, so you practise adapting rather than reciting.

17.2 Mock interviews

At least some of your practice should be with another person, ideally someone who has interviewed candidates. Ask them to follow the rubric of section 1, to interrupt with hints and pushback, and to introduce a curveball. Afterwards, ask for specific evidence: "Where did I lose you? Which decision was unjustified? Did I check in enough?" Swap roles: interviewing someone else is one of the fastest ways to learn what evidence a rubric needs. If no partner is available, a recording plus a self-review is the next best thing.

17.3 Self-review checklist

After each practice run, answer these questions honestly:

  1. Did I ask clarifying questions that changed the design, and state assumptions with numbers?
  2. Did I give an agenda and signpost each phase?
  3. Did I reach a complete end-to-end design by the halfway point?
  4. Did I start simple and add each component for a named bottleneck?
  5. Did every major decision have "gives, costs, because" and a named alternative?
  6. Did I quantify at least three choices?
  7. Did I choose the deep dive deliberately and return to the main diagram afterwards?
  8. Did I cover die, slow, and lie for my two or three most important components?
  9. Did I check in at least four times, and respond well to interruptions?
  10. Did I wrap up with a summary and open risks?

Pick the two lowest-scoring items and make them the sole focus of the next session. Trying to fix everything at once improves nothing. For content gaps, use Chapter 60 to find the pattern you missed and the relevant component chapter to learn it.

18. Day-of checklist

18.1 Before

  • Test your tools: video, audio, the shared drawing canvas or a marker that works, water, and a pen and paper as a fallback.
  • Re-read the Numbers and Decision Tables cheat sheets and the framework in Chapter 1. Do not learn new topics on the day.
  • Prepare two or three real production stories with numbers (section 13) and one question to ask the interviewer.
  • Warm up by talking through one familiar design aloud for five minutes.

18.2 During

  • Clarify for five minutes; state assumptions; give the agenda.
  • Estimate, then draw the simplest design; number the flows.
  • Name the bottleneck before each addition; say "gives, costs, because" for each major choice.
  • Pick the riskiest area for the deep dive and say why.
  • Reserve eight minutes for failure and scaling; do not skip it.
  • Check in after every phase; accept hints with thanks.
  • If lost, say so, reason from first principles, and ask a focused question.

18.3 After

  • Summarise the design in three sentences and list the open risks.
  • Ask a real question about the team's systems; it shows curiosity and ownership.
  • Write down what you would improve within an hour while the memory is fresh.

19. Interview Q&A

How do I decide which clarifying questions to ask, and how many?

Ask only questions whose answers would change the design. Group them into scope, scale, consistency, latency and availability, users, and constraints, and ask about five or six in total, finishing with a summary of the assumptions you will use. The test for any question is: "If the answer were different, would I build something different?" If not, skip it. Too few questions suggests you build before you understand; too many suggests you cannot make decisions under uncertainty. Spend about five minutes in total.

What do I do if the interviewer will not answer my clarifying questions?

Treat "you decide" as the real exercise. Pick reasonable values, say them aloud with a one-line justification, and note how the design would change if the value were different, for example "I'll assume 100 million daily users; at ten times that I'd shard the feed store earlier". Write the assumptions on the board and ask whether they look sensible. This demonstrates that you can make progress without perfect information and know which parts of the design depend on which facts.

How do I drive the conversation without seeming to ignore the interviewer?

Set an agenda in the first few minutes, signpost each transition, and check in at the end of each phase with a question such as "Does this match what you had in mind, or should I go deeper here?". Driving means you hold the direction; it does not mean you stop listening. When the interviewer interrupts, answer directly, then return with a signpost. If they redirect you, follow them and say what you are postponing. The interviewer should feel that they steer by exception, not that they have to pull the design out of you.

How should I split time in a 45-minute interview?

A reasonable split is about 5 minutes of clarifying, 7 of estimates and API, 12 for the high-level design, 13 for one or two deep dives, and 8 for failure handling, scaling, and wrap-up. In a 60-minute session, extend the clarifying, high-level design, and deep dive portions modestly and keep failure and wrap-up separate. Use the halfway checkpoint: by then you should have a complete end-to-end design. If you do not, finish the whole path at low detail before going deeper anywhere.

How do I pick which component to deep dive?

Choose the riskiest one: where scale pressure is highest, where correctness is hardest, and which is most specific to the prompt rather than generic infrastructure. For a news feed that is fan-out and the celebrity problem; for a URL shortener it is unique code generation and the redirect read path. Say the choice aloud with the reason, so the interviewer can redirect you early if they care about something else. Avoid choosing the easy part just because you know it well, since that avoids the evidence they want.

What is the best way to talk about tradeoffs?

Use a repeatable sentence: "X gives us this, costs us that, and I'd pick it because of the requirement." Attach a number where possible, name at least one alternative, and say why it lost for this problem and what would make you switch. For example, asynchronous replication gives write speed but risks losing the last second of data on failover, which is acceptable for a feed but not for a payment ledger. This shows you know the options, you decide by requirements, and you understand the limits of your own choice.

How do I do a failure walkthrough efficiently?

For each important component, answer three questions: what happens when it dies, when it slows, and when it lies. Give the blast radius, the detection mechanism, and the recovery, for example timeouts and circuit breakers for slowness and idempotency keys or reconciliation for wrong data. You will not cover every box, so pick the two or three whose failure hurts most and give the rest a single line. Doing this before the interviewer asks is one of the clearest senior signals. See Chapter 40 and Chapter 41.

What if the interviewer asks about a technology I have never used?

Say so plainly, then reason from first principles about what the technology must provide, such as ordering, durability, or partitioning, and sketch a design that provides it. Ask whether that matches their understanding and offer to check the exact guarantees in documentation in real work. Bluffing is easy to detect through follow-up questions and does more damage than a clear boundary. Honesty combined with sound reasoning usually scores better than a memorised but shallow answer.

How should I react when I realise I made a mistake or the interviewer changes the requirements?

For a mistake, say it plainly, correct the board, state what else the correction affects, and carry on; the whole thing should take about fifteen seconds and it shows composure. For a new requirement, restate it, map it onto your assumptions list, identify what stays the same and what changes, and propose the smallest sufficient change with its tradeoff. Avoid both defensiveness and a full redesign. Interviewers use curveballs to see whether your design was grounded in requirements and whether you stay flexible.

How do I talk about microservices without sounding like I just want to split everything?

Frame microservices as a cost you accept for specific benefits: independent deployment and scaling, failure isolation, and team autonomy. Draw boundaries around business capabilities and data ownership, and say that no service shares another's database. Address distributed transactions with sagas and the outbox pattern, and be ready to argue that two pieces belong together, such as a payment and its ledger, because the consistency need outweighs the split. See Chapter 44, Chapter 45, and Chapter 48.

What extra should a senior or lead candidate show beyond a correct design?

They should show the path and the ownership: phased delivery with triggers for each phase, a migration plan with shadow traffic and rollback, team and API boundaries, a build versus buy argument with total cost, a rollout approach with canaries and flags, and service level objectives with an error budget. They should also name the two or three biggest risks and how they would be mitigated. This demonstrates responsibility for the outcome, not only the whiteboard diagram.

How do I describe my real production experience without sounding rehearsed or inflated?

Use a simple shape: context with real numbers, your specific role, the decision and why, the result, and what you would change. Include a failure or a regret, because real systems have them. Be exact about what you did versus what the team did, and do not invent figures, since follow-up questions will expose them. Where your experience is partial, say what you have done that is close and reason about the difference. Linking a story to the current design, such as "we hit this same hot-key problem and salted the key", makes it relevant.

20. Recap

  • Interviewers score six things: problem navigation, technical depth, tradeoff reasoning, communication, collaboration, and handling ambiguity. Only what you say and draw is scored, so make your reasoning audible.
  • Expectations widen with level: mid-level owns a component, senior owns a whole system and its operation, staff and lead own the path, the people, and the risk.
  • Ask clarifying questions that change the design, grouped as scope, scale, consistency, latency, users, and constraints; state the rest as numbered assumptions with "if wrong, then" consequences.
  • Drive with an agenda, signposts, and check-ins. Time box deliberately: clarify, estimate and API, high-level design, deep dive, failure and wrap-up, with a complete design by the halfway point.
  • Draw left to right, name by function, number the flows, leave space, and evolve the design in iterations, starting from a single box and scaling only at a named bottleneck.
  • Say "X gives us, costs us, I'd pick it because"; quantify; name rejected alternatives and what would change your mind.
  • Deep dive into the riskiest component and return to the main thread. For every important component, walk through die, slow, and lie, with blast radius and recovery.
  • Handle hints, pushback, not-knowing, mistakes, and curveballs calmly and with a method. Mention security, privacy, cost, and operability only where the design makes them relevant.
  • Avoid the classic anti-patterns: tech-name jumping, buzzwords, over-engineering, ignoring requirements, silent thinking, no numbers, no tradeoffs, no failure handling.
  • Practise out loud, timed, with mock interviews and the self-review checklist, working on two weaknesses at a time.
