Service Decomposition

Deciding where one service ends and the next begins is the most consequential and least reversible decision in a microservice architecture. This chapter explains the main ways to find boundaries, how to judge whether a boundary is good, which smells reveal a bad one, and how to fix boundaries after the fact, with two worked examples.

1. Why boundaries matter more than technology

Once a company has decided to split a system into services (Chapter 44 covers when that is wise), the next question is how to cut. The choice of language, framework, message broker or orchestrator can be changed service by service. A boundary, the line that says "this data and this behaviour belong inside one service, everything else is outside", is far harder to change, because once other teams call your API and read your events, moving the line means coordinating many teams and migrating live data.

A good boundary has a simple consequence: most changes touch exactly one service. A bad boundary has the opposite consequence: a single feature, such as "let customers pay with a gift card", requires coordinated edits to five services, five deployments in the right order, and a rollback plan that nobody trusts. When that happens, you have paid the full price of a distributed system (network failures, partial failure, harder debugging, operational overhead) and received almost none of the benefits (independent deployment, independent scaling, team autonomy). This failure mode has a name, the distributed monolith, and almost every decomposition mistake in this chapter eventually produces one.

There is no formula that outputs the correct boundaries. Decomposition is a design activity guided by several lenses, each of which highlights a different kind of force. Experienced architects apply more than one lens and look for the places where the lenses agree. Where they disagree, that is where the interesting trade-off lives.

Key idea A service boundary should enclose things that change together and must be consistent together, and should cut where things change independently and can tolerate being consistent eventually. Every technique in this chapter is a different way of finding that line.

2. Decomposition approaches

Six lenses are in common use. They are not rival methods, they are different questions you ask of the same system.

2.1 By business capability

A business capability is something the business does to create value, described by what rather than how: "manage product catalog", "take payment", "fulfil shipments". Capabilities are remarkably stable. An online retailer was selling goods, taking payment and shipping parcels long before it had microservices, and will still do so after the next re-platforming. That stability is what makes them good boundaries. A capability map is a hierarchy that starts with the broad capabilities of the organisation and breaks each into smaller ones. Each leaf, or small cluster of leaves, becomes a service candidate.

Capability-based decomposition has a strong advantage: you can derive it with business people, without looking at any code, and it maps onto how the company is organised. Its weakness is that capabilities are described at the level of business vocabulary, so they can hide the fact that a single capability such as "order management" actually contains several distinct models (a cart, an order, a return). It tells you where to start but not always where to stop.

2.2 By subdomain and bounded context (domain-driven design)

Domain-driven design (DDD, introduced in Chapter 39) sharpens the capability idea. It splits the business domain into subdomains: core subdomains (where the company differentiates itself and invests the best engineers), supporting subdomains (necessary but not differentiating), and generic subdomains (solved problems you should buy or use off the shelf, such as payments processing or email delivery). Inside the solution space, a bounded context is a boundary within which every term has one precise meaning and one model.

The classic example is the word "product". To the catalog team a product is a rich description with images, attributes and categories. To inventory it is a SKU with a quantity at a location. To billing it is a line item with a price and tax code. To shipping it is a weight and dimensions. Trying to build one shared "Product" model that serves them all produces a bloated object that every team is afraid to change. DDD instead gives each context its own model, and defines how contexts translate between them. A bounded context is the most natural candidate for a microservice boundary because it already encloses a consistent vocabulary and a consistent model. A useful heuristic is that when two experts from different parts of the business use the same word to mean different things, you have probably found a context boundary.

2.3 By data ownership and transaction boundaries

Every piece of data should have exactly one service that owns it, meaning that only that service reads and writes the underlying store, and everyone else goes through its API or its events (Chapter 46 covers the data patterns). This lens asks two questions. First, which entities are always changed together in one atomic step? If an operation must either fully succeed or fully fail across two pieces of data, and you want that guarantee from a simple database transaction, the two pieces belong in the same service. Second, which entities can tolerate a delay in agreeing with each other? If a few seconds of inconsistency is acceptable (for example, the shipping service learning that an order was paid), the pieces can be in different services connected by events.

Transactional boundaries are the most honest lens because they are physical. A single-database ACID transaction cannot span two services' databases, and the alternatives (sagas, covered in Chapter 48) are much more work. If your decomposition forces a saga for something the business thinks of as one indivisible action, such as "add item and update the cart total", you have probably cut in the wrong place.

2.4 By volatility (rate of change)

Some parts of a system change every week, such as promotion rules, recommendation algorithms and checkout experiments. Others change once a year, such as tax calculation structure or core account data. Putting a fast-changing piece and a slow-changing piece in the same deployable means the stable code is redeployed, retested and put at risk every time the volatile code changes. The volatility lens says: isolate the parts that change often and for different reasons, so their frequent releases do not drag the stable parts along. This is the microservice version of the Single Responsibility Principle, where a "responsibility" is a reason to change, and it explains why pricing and promotions are often split from the basic product catalog even though both deal with products.

2.5 By scaling and performance profile

Different parts of the system face different load. Product search might receive 50,000 requests per second at peak and is read-heavy and cache-friendly, while payment capture might be 300 per second but requires strong consistency and careful security. Video transcoding is CPU- and GPU-heavy and batchy. If they share one deployable, you must scale the whole thing to the needs of the hungriest part, and a hot spot in one can starve the rest. The scaling lens separates components whose resource shape (CPU, memory, I/O, read versus write ratio, latency target, availability target) differs sharply. It also isolates blast radius: a flood of search traffic should not take down checkout.

Use this lens with care. Scaling profile alone is rarely a reason to split, because most scaling problems can be solved by running more copies of the same service or by adding a cache. It becomes decisive when the profiles are truly incompatible, such as an interactive API next to a heavy batch job, or when compliance requires isolating one piece (for example cardholder data, which is why payment is usually its own tightly controlled service).

2.6 By team

Conway's Law says systems end up mirroring the communication structure of the organisation that builds them. The practical advice, sometimes called the inverse Conway manoeuvre, is to design the team structure you want and let it shape the architecture. A service should be ownable by one team (typically five to nine people, the size that can be fed by two pizzas, in the usual joke) that can understand all of it, deploy it, and be on call for it. If a boundary requires two teams to edit the same service constantly, they will collide in the code and in the release schedule. If one team owns twelve tiny services, it spends its time on plumbing. Team topology is therefore both an input to and a check on the other lenses.

3. Granularity: how big should a service be?

Granularity is the size of a service, and there is no correct number of lines of code or endpoints. The right size depends on what the boundary protects. Too coarse and you keep the problems of a monolith. Too fine and you create a different set of problems, because every extra network hop adds latency, failure modes, versioning work and operational cost.

3.1 Signals that a service is too coarse

Look for several teams repeatedly merging into the same service, so that every release needs coordination. Look for a long build and test cycle, because a large codebase has a large test suite. Look for unrelated parts with different load, such as a read-heavy browsing API living beside a write-heavy ingestion pipeline. Look for the scenario where a memory leak in a rarely used report takes down checkout. Finally, look for internal modules with their own clear vocabulary and little coupling to the rest; they often want to be extracted.

3.2 Signals that a service is too fine

Look for features that always need coordinated changes in several services, which means the cut is through a cohesive unit. Look for deep synchronous call chains: if serving one user request requires service A to call B to call C to call D, then end-to-end latency is the sum of four calls, and availability is the product of four availabilities (four services each at 99.9 percent give about 99.6 percent, because 0.999 to the power of four is roughly 0.996). Look for services that are trivially small and exist only to wrap a single table (anemic services, section 5), and for teams that spend more time on pipelines, dashboards and contract tests than on features. Look also for the pattern where nobody can say what the service owns, only what it calls.

3.3 Rule-of-thumb tests

Four tests are quick to apply to any proposed service. Passing all four is a strong sign, and failing any one deserves an explicit explanation.

  1. Can one team own it? One team should be able to hold the whole service in their heads, run its on-call rotation and make decisions about it without negotiating with another group.
  2. Can it change independently? Think of the last ten features. How many needed edits only inside this service? If most of them also needed edits elsewhere, the boundary is in the wrong place.
  3. Can it deploy independently? You should be able to release it on a Tuesday without releasing anything else, and a rollback should not require rolling back a neighbour. Backward-compatible API changes (Chapter 47) are what make this true.
  4. Does it own its data? It should be the only writer of its tables. If another service reads or writes them directly, the boundary exists only on the diagram.
Watch out "Micro" is a misleading prefix. Optimising for small size leads to nanoservices. A better question is not "how small?" but "what is the smallest unit that satisfies the four tests?". Many successful systems begin with a few coarse services or a modular monolith and split further only when pressure appears.

4. Coupling and cohesion

Cohesion is how strongly the things inside a boundary belong together. Coupling is how strongly things across a boundary depend on one another. The aim of decomposition is high cohesion inside and low coupling between. Coupling is not one thing, and naming its kinds precisely helps you diagnose which one is hurting you.

The distinction between temporal and the others is important when you choose between synchronous and asynchronous communication (section 10). Domain coupling is the most expensive and the hardest to remove, because it is a symptom of a wrong boundary rather than a technical choice. Data coupling is the sneakiest, because it works perfectly well until someone changes a column.

Cohesion has levels too. The best kind is functional cohesion: everything in the service contributes to one well-defined purpose. The weakest kinds are logical cohesion (grouped because they are similar, like a "utilities" service) and coincidental cohesion (grouped for no reason). A service whose name ends in "Manager", "Helper" or "Common" is a warning sign. Cohesion and coupling interact: if you cut through a cohesive unit, you convert internal calls into cross-service calls and manufacture coupling, which is why wrong cuts show up as chattiness.

5. Boundary smells

You cannot know a boundary is right in advance, but you can recognise a wrong one by its symptoms. These smells show up in design reviews and in production incidents.

5.1 Chatty calls

If one logical operation triggers many small calls between the same two services, they are probably one service pretending to be two. Every call pays for serialisation, a network round trip (often 1-5 ms inside a data centre) and a new chance to fail. Forty calls at 2 ms each is 80 ms of pure waiting, before any real work. Remedies are to coarsen the API (accept a batch of items rather than one), to cache stable data, or to merge the services.

5.2 Shared tables

When two services read or write the same table, the schema becomes a private API with no versioning, no owner and no contract tests. A column rename in one service breaks the other at runtime. It also destroys the ability to deploy independently, since each change must be checked against every other reader. The fix is to give the table one owner and expose the data through an API or events (Chapter 46).

5.3 Distributed transactions everywhere

Some cross-service workflows are unavoidable and can be handled with a saga. But if most business operations need a saga, with compensating actions and careful ordering, the services were cut through the middle of the transactional units. Sagas are a tool for a few cross-boundary processes (placing an order across payment and inventory is the textbook case), not the normal way for two pieces of the same concept to stay consistent.

5.4 Lockstep releases

If the release notes say "deploy A, then B, then C within ten minutes of one another", the services are deployment-coupled. This is the clearest operational sign of a distributed monolith, and it is measurable: count how many releases in the last quarter involved more than one service.

5.5 The god service

One service accumulates many unrelated responsibilities, usually because it was the first one built, or because "it already has the data". Everything depends on it, so it is a bottleneck for change and a single point of failure. Typical culprits are "User", "Order" or "Core" services that every feature touches. The cure is to find the separate models inside and peel them off one at a time.

5.6 Anemic services

The opposite problem: a service that is nothing but CRUD over one table, with no business rules, while the real logic lives in a separate orchestrating service that calls five of these. The orchestrator knows everything and the "services" know nothing, which means every business change lands in the orchestrator and the boundaries add cost without carrying meaning. A healthy service owns not just data but the rules that keep the data valid.

5.7 Circular dependencies

If A calls B and B calls A (directly or through a chain), neither can be built, started or reasoned about alone. Startup order becomes fragile, and a failure in one propagates around the loop. Cycles nearly always indicate that a concept has been cut in two or that a shared third concept is missing and should be extracted.

6. Dependency analysis

Smells are most reliable when you look at the whole dependency graph: nodes are services, and a directed edge from A to B means "A needs B to do its job". You can build the graph from design documents, but in an existing system it is better to derive it from evidence such as service-mesh telemetry, distributed traces, API gateway logs, and shared-database access logs, because real dependencies differ from the documented ones.

Useful things to compute from the graph:

  • Cycles: any strongly connected component of more than one service is a design bug to fix, either by inverting one edge with an event or by merging the members.
  • Fan-in (afferent coupling): how many services depend on this one. High fan-in means the service has many customers, so its API must be stable and carefully versioned. Identity and catalog legitimately have high fan-in. A business-logic service with huge fan-in is a god service.
  • Fan-out (efferent coupling): how many services this one depends on. High fan-out means it is fragile, because its availability is the product of its dependencies' availabilities. Aggregators such as checkout and a web backend-for-frontend naturally have high fan-out; a domain service should have low fan-out.
  • Depth: the longest synchronous chain from a user request to a leaf. More than three or four hops usually means latency and availability trouble.
  • Co-change frequency: from version control, which services are modified in the same pull requests or the same release? Two services that always change together are one service in disguise.

The co-change analysis is especially useful because it measures what you actually care about (domain coupling) rather than what is easy to measure (call counts). A rule of thumb used by many teams is to look at the pairs of services with the highest co-change ratio and ask whether the boundary between them deserves to exist.

7. Aggregates and transactional consistency inside a service

In DDD an aggregate is a cluster of objects that must be consistent with each other at all times, treated as one unit for changes. One object, the aggregate root, is the only entry point. For example, an Order aggregate contains the order and its line items; you never change a line item without going through the order, because the order enforces rules such as "total equals the sum of the lines" and "a shipped order cannot be edited". The database transaction boundary matches the aggregate boundary: one aggregate is changed in one local transaction.

Aggregates give you a precise tool for decomposition. The rule is: an aggregate must live entirely inside one service, never split across two. A service can contain several aggregates, but between aggregates (and therefore across services) you accept eventual consistency and reference other aggregates only by identity, not by holding object references. This leads to design guidance that is easy to state:

  • Keep aggregates small. A giant "Customer" aggregate that includes all orders and addresses makes every update contend on one row and one lock.
  • Refer to other aggregates by ID. An order holds a customerId, not a customer object.
  • If a business rule spans two aggregates (for instance "a customer's total open orders may not exceed a credit limit"), ask whether it truly needs immediate consistency. If it does, they probably belong in the same service and perhaps the same aggregate. If it does not, enforce it with an event and a later compensation, or with a reservation model.

Inside a service, you still have the full power of the local database: ACID transactions, joins, constraints. This is a reason to keep strongly related data together; every pair you separate converts a one-line transaction into a distributed workflow. Within a service, use one local transaction per aggregate and publish events about what happened (using the outbox pattern from Chapter 48 so that the database write and the event cannot diverge).

Key idea Strong consistency lives inside aggregates and therefore inside services. Eventual consistency lives between them. Choosing a service boundary is choosing which invariants you will enforce instantly and which you will enforce eventually.

8. Shared concepts: reference data, identity, and shared libraries

8.1 Reference data: duplicate, share, or look up

Almost every service needs some data that it does not own: currency codes, country lists, product names, a customer's display name, tax rates. There are three strategies, and each is right sometimes.

  • Look up (call the owner at request time). Always fresh, simple. But it adds latency, couples availability (temporal coupling), and the owner must handle the load. Good for data that is rare to need and must be current, such as "is this account blocked right now?".
  • Duplicate (keep a local copy fed by events). The consumer subscribes to the owner's change events and stores the fields it needs. Reads are local and fast, and the service survives the owner's outage. The price is staleness (typically seconds), storage, and the need to handle events that arrive late or out of order. Good for data read very frequently, such as product name and price shown in an order history.
  • Share (a common database or a shared library with the data). Appropriate only for tiny, static, universally agreed data such as ISO country codes, shipped as a versioned library or configuration file. Sharing a live table is the shared-database smell.

A very important subtlety: a copy is often not the same concept as the original. An order must record the price the customer actually paid at the time, which is a fact about the order, not a live reference to the catalog price. Catalog price changes tomorrow, and the historical order must not. Copying values into an order as a snapshot is therefore not duplication to be eliminated; it is correct modelling. Ask whether you need "the current value" (look up or subscribe) or "the value at that moment" (snapshot).

8.2 Identity of entities across services

When the same real-world thing appears in several services, they need a common way to refer to it. Design choices:

  • The owner generates the ID and others store it as an opaque reference. The order service holds customerId; it never interprets it. This is the default and keeps ownership clean.
  • Use globally unique IDs (UUIDs, or time-ordered ones such as ULIDs or snowflake-style 64-bit IDs; see the ID generation material in the foundations chapters). Auto-increment integers are fine within one service but leak ordering and collide if databases are ever merged. Generating the ID in the client or the first service lets you create a record before the other service replies, which helps idempotency.
  • Do not use natural identifiers as keys where they might change or be sensitive, such as email address or national ID. Keep them as attributes with a unique index in the owning service.
  • Correlation IDs for processes. A business process spanning services (an order and its payment and shipment) needs an identifier carried in every message so you can trace it and so consumers can deduplicate. The order ID usually serves, and a separate request ID covers a single call chain.

If two services both believe they own the same entity's identity (for example both create "customer" records and reconcile them later), you have a boundary problem, not an ID problem. Resolve who is the system of record.

8.3 Shared libraries and their pitfalls

Teams are tempted to put shared code in a library to avoid duplication: DTOs, domain models, validation, database entities. This is the most common way to rebuild a monolith with a network in the middle. If a library contains the domain model and every service imports it, then changing the model forces every service to upgrade together, and you are back to lockstep releases. Good rules:

  • Share technical, domain-free code: logging, tracing, authentication helpers, HTTP clients with retry policies. Keep these thin and backward compatible.
  • Do not share domain entities or business rules. Prefer a small amount of duplication; the cost of copying a ten-line DTO is much lower than the cost of coupling releases (a view often summarised as "duplication is cheaper than the wrong abstraction").
  • Share contracts, not implementations: publish an OpenAPI or protobuf schema, and let each consumer generate or write its own client. Tooling that checks compatibility (Chapter 47) replaces shared model code.
  • Never let a library make a runtime call to a shared database. Version libraries semantically, and do not require consumers to upgrade at the same moment.

9. Reporting and queries across services

Splitting data across services breaks the thing relational databases are best at: joins. "Show the ten best-selling products this month with their category and stock level" touches order, catalog and inventory data that are now in three stores. Resist the instinct to reconnect them through a shared database. The standard solutions (explained in depth in Chapter 46) are:

  • API composition: a gateway or backend-for-frontend calls several services and merges the answers in memory. Fine for screens that show one customer's data. Poor for large sorted or filtered sets across services, because you cannot join a million rows across a network.
  • CQRS read models: a dedicated query service subscribes to events from several services and builds a denormalised view shaped exactly for the question, for example a search index or a table with product, category and stock together. The read model is eventually consistent, which is usually fine for browsing.
  • Analytical store: for reports and business intelligence, stream events or change data capture into a warehouse or data lake, and query there. This keeps heavy analytic queries off operational databases.

Plan for reporting at decomposition time. If the business needs cross-cutting reports that must be exact and real-time, check whether the boundaries put the needed data close together. Reporting needs are a valid input to boundary choice, though they are usually satisfied by read models rather than by moving boundaries.

10. Synchronous versus asynchronous communication and its effect on boundaries

The communication style between services (Chapter 47 covers it in full) is tied to boundary quality. In a synchronous call, the caller waits for the callee's reply; in an asynchronous interaction, the caller publishes a message or event and moves on. Synchronous calls are simple to reason about and give immediate answers, but they create temporal coupling: the caller's availability is limited by the callee's, and latency adds up.

How this affects boundaries:

  • Asynchronous events let you draw a boundary where you would otherwise need a transaction, because the services agree eventually rather than instantly. That makes finer boundaries viable, but it also pushes complexity into saga design and into the user experience ("your order is being processed").
  • A boundary crossed by many synchronous calls is a coupling hotspot. Converting a call into an event can remove a cycle, for example by having inventory react to OrderPlaced rather than the order service calling inventory, which in turn calls order to look up details.
  • Queries want synchronous replies. Commands that change state across boundaries are the best candidates for async. A common pattern is to keep user-facing reads synchronous (served from local data or read models) and make cross-service writes asynchronous.
  • Events carry a contract just like APIs. An event that exposes the producer's internal model couples consumers to it. Publish events in the vocabulary of the business ("OrderPlaced") with only the data consumers need.
  • Asynchrony is not free: you need idempotent consumers, ordering strategy, a dead-letter path and monitoring of consumer lag. Do not use it to hide a bad boundary; a chatty pair of services connected by a queue is still chatty.

11. Decomposition workshop techniques

Boundaries should be found by people who understand the business, not by engineers alone looking at code. Two lightweight workshop formats are widely used.

11.1 Event storming

Event storming is a collaborative modelling session where developers, product people and domain experts stand around a long wall of paper and use coloured sticky notes. The steps run roughly as follows.

  1. Big picture: everyone writes domain events (orange notes, past tense, such as "Order Placed", "Payment Captured", "Parcel Dispatched") and arranges them left to right in time order. Disagreements about wording or order reveal different mental models.
  2. Add commands and actors: blue notes for commands ("Place Order") that cause events, and notes for the people or systems who issue them.
  3. Find aggregates: yellow notes for the aggregate that handles each command and emits each event. Events and commands that cluster around the same aggregate belong together.
  4. Find policies and hot spots: lilac notes for reactions ("when payment is captured, reserve stock"), and red notes for confusion and conflicts.
  5. Draw boundaries: look for where the vocabulary shifts, where pivotal events (for example "Order Paid") hand control from one group of aggregates to another, and where different experts take over. Those lines are bounded context candidates.

The result is a shared picture in a few hours, and an early list of the cross-context policies that will become events or sagas.

11.2 Domain storytelling

Domain storytelling has a domain expert narrate a concrete scenario ("a customer orders two books and one is out of stock") while a moderator draws it as a simple pictogram sentence: actors, the work objects they handle, and numbered activities. Because each story is one concrete scenario, it surfaces real exceptions (backorders, partial refunds) that abstract diagrams miss. Stories from different scenarios are compared to see where the same concept recurs, which reveals terms and boundaries.

Other useful inputs: context mapping to name relationships between contexts (customer-supplier, conformist, anti-corruption layer, shared kernel), capability mapping with business leaders, and analysis of existing code (co-change history, call graphs, database foreign keys) to see where the current system already has seams. The best results combine the workshop view of "what the business believes" with the data view of "what the code actually does".

12. Evolving boundaries and merging services

The first decomposition will be wrong in places, because you learn the domain by building it. Treat boundaries as hypotheses and plan to revise them. Some practices make revision cheaper:

  • Start coarser. Splitting a service in two is easier than merging two that have grown apart in data and API, since splitting follows a seam you can see in the code while merging requires reconciling two schemas and two sets of consumers.
  • Build a modular monolith first (Chapter 39), with strict module boundaries and no shared tables between modules. Modules can later become services along lines the code already respects.
  • Hide the boundary behind a stable API. If consumers use a well-designed API and events, you can move the implementation behind it, splitting or merging, without them noticing.
  • Use the strangler fig (Chapter 44) to migrate behaviour gradually, with routing that sends traffic to old or new implementation and is rolled back if needed.

12.1 Splitting a service

The usual steps: separate the code into modules inside the service first; separate the data (give the module its own schema or tables, remove cross-module joins, replace them with calls or replicated data); then extract the module as its own deployable, often running both versions in parallel behind a router while data is synchronised (dual writes with verification, or change data capture). Only then remove the old code.

12.2 Merging services

Merging is legitimate and underused. Signs you should merge: two services always change together; their API is chatty; they form a cycle; they need a saga for something the business sees as one step; a single team owns both and pays double the overhead. The steps mirror splitting in reverse: align the data models, move one service's logic into the other as a module, redirect consumers, then retire the empty shell. Treat it as a normal refactor and not as a failure. Amazon Prime Video's well-known move of one video-monitoring pipeline from distributed components back into a single process reduced cost significantly; the lesson is that the right granularity depends on the workload, not on fashion.

Interview tip Saying "I would merge these two" shows maturity. Interviewers often probe whether you can reverse a decision when evidence shows the boundary is wrong.

The details of that saga are in Chapter 48.

13.3 Typical wrong splits and their fixes

  1. Splitting by technical layer. A "web service", a "business logic service" and a "data access service" means every feature changes all three, they must deploy together, and none owns any business meaning. Cut vertically by capability, so each service contains its own API, logic and data.
  2. One shared "Product" service for everyone. Catalog descriptions, prices with promotions, and stock quantities have different owners, change rates and consistency needs. A single product model becomes a god service that every team is afraid to change. Separate catalog content, pricing, and inventory, and let the others keep only an ID and snapshots.
  3. Splitting Order from OrderLine. An order and its lines form one aggregate that must be consistent (totals, state). Putting lines in another service forces distributed transactions for every edit and chatty reads for every display. Keep them together.
  4. Putting stock checks inside the cart or order service reading inventory's table. That is a shared table. Inventory owns stock and exposes a reservation API; order refers to it by event or call.
  5. Over-splitting notification into email, SMS and push services. They share templates, preferences and rate limiting, and all react to the same events. One notification service with several channel adapters is enough until load or team structure demands more.
  6. Putting payment into Order for convenience. It brings card-data compliance scope into the whole order codebase and ties order releases to PSP integration changes. Keep payment separate, even though it adds a saga step, because the compliance and external-vendor volatility justify it.
  7. Making Cart a thin wrapper over Order. A cart is ephemeral and high-churn, an order is a durable record. Keeping them apart lets the cart use a fast key-value store and be lost without legal consequence.

13.4 Numbers behind the choices

Suppose a sale event generates 20,000 catalog reads per second, 2,000 cart updates per second, 200 orders per second and 200 payment captures per second. In the monolith, everything must be provisioned for the 20,000 read peak plus the rest. With the split, catalog scales to dozens of small instances behind a cache, cart sits on a key-value store, and order and payment need only a handful of nodes with strong databases. If each checkout makes one synchronous call to the order service and the rest are events, a payment outage delays confirmation but does not stop browsing or carts, and the order backlog drains when payment recovers.

14. Second example: food delivery or ride hailing

A food-delivery platform (the same shape fits ride hailing) shows how real-time and geographic forces change the cuts. The main capabilities are: restaurant and menu management, customer accounts, order placement, dispatch (matching an order with a courier), courier location tracking, delivery pricing and surge, payment and payout, ratings, and notifications.

14.1 Why these cuts

  • Location tracking is separate because its scaling profile is extreme: with 200,000 active couriers sending a position every 4 seconds, that is 50,000 writes per second of data that is useless after a minute. It belongs in an in-memory or specialised geo store with no need for durability or transactions, and must not share a database with orders.
  • Dispatch is separate from Order because it is an algorithmic, fast-changing capability (volatility lens): matching rules, batching, and machine-learned estimates are tuned weekly, while the order record is stable. Dispatch owns "offers" and their timeouts, and Order only knows that a courier was assigned.
  • Pricing and surge are separate from Order and Menu, because surge depends on live supply and demand per area, a different data shape and change rate. Order snapshots the price quoted, so later surge changes do not alter it.
  • Menu and Restaurant serve a read-heavy catalog with restaurant-side editing and availability toggles. It is analogous to catalog in the retail example.
  • Payment and payout are two sides of one money domain: charging customers and paying couriers and restaurants. They are kept together because ledger consistency matters, and separated from Order for compliance.

14.2 Wrong splits here and their fixes

  • One "Delivery" service containing order, dispatch and tracking. The location write storm then shares fate with order placement, and a bad deploy of matching logic risks taking down checkout. Split by scaling profile and volatility.
  • Separate "Courier" and "CourierLocation" and "CourierStatus" services. Dispatch must read all three on every decision, producing chatty calls and inconsistent snapshots (a courier shown free in one but busy in another). Dispatch needs a single consistent view, so keep availability and position together in one component that owns courier state.
  • Dispatch calling Order synchronously for details, and Order calling Dispatch to assign. This is a cycle. Break it with events: Order publishes OrderReady with the data dispatch needs, and Dispatch publishes CourierAssigned.
  • A shared database for customers, couriers and restaurants. Three kinds of user have different data, privacy and verification needs. Use a shared identity capability for login only, and give each role its own profile service or module.

The transferable lesson: when one part of a domain has a very different real-time, volume or latency shape, give it its own service and, usually, its own specialised store. When two things need a single consistent decision, keep them together even if they sound like different nouns.

15. Checklists

15.1 Before you draw a boundary

  • Have domain experts named the capabilities, and does the vocabulary stay consistent inside the proposed boundary?
  • Which invariants must hold instantly, and are all the data for each inside one service?
  • Who will own it, and can that team deploy and operate it alone?
  • What is the expected load shape and availability target, and is it compatible with its neighbours in the same deployable?
  • How often will it change, and for what reasons?
  • What reference data will it need, and will it look up, subscribe or snapshot?
  • Which reports and queries will cross it, and how will they be served?

15.2 After the first deployment

  • Does each service own its data, with no foreign readers or writers?
  • Is the dependency graph acyclic, and how deep is the longest synchronous chain?
  • What percentage of releases touched more than one service in the last quarter?
  • Which service pairs have the highest co-change ratio?
  • Is any service called by nearly everyone, or calling nearly everyone, without being a gateway?
  • Does each service have contract tests and backward-compatible API changes?
  • Do we have a measurable trigger for splitting (build time, team collisions, load) and for merging (always change together, saga for single business step)?

16. Interview Q&A

How do you decide where to draw service boundaries in a new system?

I start with business capabilities, because they are stable and understandable to non-engineers, then refine them with bounded contexts, looking for places where the same word means different things. Next I check each candidate against transactional boundaries, asking which invariants must be enforced atomically, and against team ownership and scaling profile. I accept a boundary only if one team can own it, it can change and deploy alone, and it owns its data. For a new product I prefer to start with fewer, coarser boundaries or a modular monolith, since splitting later is cheaper than merging.

What is a distributed monolith and how do you recognise one?

It is a system split into separately deployed services that are still coupled so tightly that they must change and release together. You recognise it by lockstep releases, features that require edits in many services, shared databases, deep synchronous call chains and cycles in the dependency graph. It has the costs of a distributed system (network failures, latency, operations) without independent deployment or scaling. The fix is usually to redraw boundaries around cohesive business capabilities, give each service its own data, and replace synchronous chains with events or merge services that always change together.

Two services always change together. What do you do?

Co-change is evidence of domain coupling, meaning the boundary cuts through one cohesive concept. First verify with data, such as pull request history and release records. Then consider merging them into one service, perhaps keeping them as two modules inside it, because that removes the network hop, the contract versioning and any saga. Splitting a service later when real pressure appears is cheaper than maintaining a wrong split now. The exception is when the split is justified by something else such as compliance or very different scaling, in which case I would reduce the coupling instead.

How do you handle a business rule that spans two services?

First ask whether it truly needs immediate consistency. If yes, that is a signal that the data belongs in one service and one transaction, so I would reconsider the boundary. If the business can accept brief inconsistency, I enforce it with events and a saga, using reservations or compensation to repair violations, for example reserving stock and releasing it if payment fails. I make steps idempotent and design the user experience around the intermediate state. The key is to decide per rule, and not to default to distributed transactions.

Should services share a library of domain models?

Generally no. A shared domain model library means every change forces every consumer to upgrade, which recreates deployment coupling and lets teams couple to internals. Share technical, domain-free libraries such as logging, tracing and HTTP clients, and share contracts like OpenAPI or protobuf schemas instead of implementation code. A little duplicated DTO code is cheaper than synchronised releases. Where a model must be common, such as an event schema, version it and keep it backward compatible so consumers upgrade at their own pace.

How do you run reports that need data from many services?

I avoid reconnecting the databases. For screens that show one entity's data, a backend-for-frontend or gateway can compose several API calls. For filtered lists or search across services, I build a read model that subscribes to events from the owning services and stores a denormalised view in a suitable store such as a search index. For analytics, I stream events or change data capture into a warehouse. These views are eventually consistent, which is acceptable for most reports. If a report must be exact and real-time, I check whether the boundaries put that data too far apart.

When would you choose async messaging over synchronous calls between services?

I choose async for commands that cross a boundary and do not need an immediate answer, such as publishing OrderPlaced so inventory, shipping and notification can react independently. It removes temporal coupling, absorbs bursts and lets consumers fail and retry without blocking the user. I keep synchronous calls for reads that need an immediate answer, preferably from local data. The cost is complexity in idempotency, ordering, monitoring consumer lag and explaining intermediate states to users, and a chatty pair does not become healthy just because a queue sits between them.

How do you identify entities across services, for example a customer?

The service that owns the entity generates its identifier, usually a globally unique ID such as a UUID or time-ordered ID, and other services store it as an opaque reference. They do not interpret it or share the owner's table. Natural keys such as email stay as attributes with a unique index because they can change. For processes that span services, I propagate a correlation identifier such as the order ID so messages can be traced and deduplicated. If two services both create the entity, that signals unclear ownership and needs to be resolved first.

How do you know a service is too small?

Symptoms include features that need coordinated changes in several services, synchronous call chains six hops deep, services that are only CRUD over one table with the logic sitting in an orchestrator, and teams that own more services than they can operate. Availability suffers, since four services in a chain at 99.9 percent give about 99.6 percent overall. I check the rule-of-thumb tests: one team can own it, it changes independently, deploys independently and owns its data. When they fail, I merge services, often into modules within one deployable.

How would you split an existing god service?

I begin by finding the separate models inside it through event storming or by analysing which tables and code change together. Then I separate the code into modules, remove cross-module joins by replacing them with explicit calls or replicated data, and give each module its own schema. Only then do I extract one module as a service, using the strangler fig approach with routing and parallel run, so I can roll back. I extract the module with the clearest boundary and highest pain first, such as the most volatile or most load-heavy part, and repeat, keeping the system working throughout.

17. Recap

  • A boundary is the hardest decision to reverse; a good one means most changes touch one service, a bad one yields a distributed monolith.
  • Use several lenses together: business capability, bounded context, data and transactions, volatility, scaling profile and team.
  • Right size is judged by four tests: one team can own it, it changes independently, deploys independently and owns its data.
  • Know the coupling types (temporal, deployment, domain, data, semantic) and aim for functional cohesion inside each service.
  • Boundary smells: chatty calls, shared tables, sagas everywhere, lockstep releases, god services, anemic services, cycles.
  • Analyse the dependency graph for cycles, fan-in, fan-out, depth and co-change.
  • Aggregates stay inside one service: strong consistency inside, eventual consistency between, references by ID.
  • For shared concepts, look up, subscribe or snapshot; owners generate IDs; share contracts and technical code, not domain models.
  • Cross-service reporting uses composition, read models and an analytical store, not shared databases.
  • Async events permit finer boundaries but add idempotency and ordering work and do not fix bad cuts.
  • Use event storming and domain storytelling to find boundaries; treat them as hypotheses, and split or merge as evidence arrives.
