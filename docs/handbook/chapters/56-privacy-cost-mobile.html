Chapter 56 · Module 8 · Cross-cutting concerns

Privacy, Cost and Client/Mobile Concerns

Three concerns decide whether a design that works on a whiteboard can survive in the real world: the law and ethics of personal data, the monthly cloud bill, and the fact that your users run your code on unreliable phones. This chapter treats each as a first-class design requirement, with concrete mechanisms, numbers, and the questions an interviewer will ask.

1. Why these three belong together

Most system design discussion is about the happy path: requests arrive, servers answer, data is stored. Three things are easy to forget during a forty-five minute interview, and each causes real production pain. The first is privacy: some of the data you store is about real people, laws regulate what you may do with it, and "we never delete anything" is a legal liability. The second is cost: every box and arrow on your diagram has a price, and a design that scales technically but costs more than the revenue it earns is a failed design. The third is the client: a large share of your traffic comes from phones on bad networks with small batteries, running app versions you cannot force anyone to update.

They share a theme. Each one constrains the design from outside the "functional requirements" list, so each one is a non-functional requirement that a senior engineer raises before being asked. They also interact. Deleting a user's data across seven stores costs money. Keeping data in a European region for residency raises cost. Syncing a phone cheaply means sending deltas, and deltas make deletion harder because you must also sync the deletion itself. We will see these interactions repeatedly.

Key idea Treat privacy, cost, and client behaviour as requirements with numbers, exactly like latency and availability. "We retain raw logs for 30 days, PII lives only in one vault, a delete request completes within 30 days, and an average active user costs us 0.4 cents per month" is a design statement. "We will be careful with data" is not.

The chapter has three parts. Part A (sections 2 to 9) covers privacy. Part B (sections 10 to 17) covers cost. Part C (sections 18 to 26) covers client and mobile concerns. Sections 27 and 28 give two worked examples that combine them, and the chapter ends with questions and a recap.

2. Part A: what counts as personal data

You cannot protect data you have not identified. PII (personally identifiable information) is any information that identifies a person directly or can be combined with other information to identify them. European law uses the broader phrase personal data, which includes things many engineers do not think of as personal: IP addresses, device identifiers, cookie IDs, advertising IDs, precise location, and anything that "relates to" an identifiable person such as purchase history or messages they wrote.

2.1 A practical classification

A classification scheme is just a small number of labels attached to every field in every schema, so that code, pipelines, and reviewers can treat them differently. A common four-level scheme works well in interviews.

ClassExamplesTypical handling
PublicProduct names, public posts the user chose to publishNo special controls beyond integrity.
InternalRequest counts, anonymous aggregate metrics, feature-flag stateNormal access controls; may be broadly available to engineers.
Personal (PII)Name, email, phone, address, IP address, device ID, precise location, order historyEncrypted at rest, access logged, retention limited, deletable, kept out of logs and analytics by default.
SensitiveGovernment ID numbers, payment card numbers, health data, biometrics, passwords, data about race, religion, sexual orientation, childrenStrongest controls: isolated vault, field-level encryption, explicit consent, tight and audited access, often a regulatory regime of its own (PCI DSS for cards, HIPAA for US health data).

The key engineering move is to tag at the schema level. Every column or event field carries a classification annotation. Then a logging library can refuse to print fields tagged Personal, a data-warehouse export job can drop or hash them, and a deletion pipeline can discover automatically which tables hold a user's data. Without tagging, you rely on people remembering, and they will not.

2.2 Where PII hides

The database column called email is obvious. The places that cause breaches and failed deletion requests are the ones nobody listed: application logs that print a request URL containing an email, error-tracking tools that capture request bodies, analytics events with a user ID, search indexes holding a copy of the profile, caches, message queues that retain events for days, database backups, data-warehouse copies, files in object storage, and screenshots in a support tool. A mature design starts with a data map: an inventory of every system that holds each class of data. This is also literally a legal requirement under GDPR (a "record of processing activities"), so the inventory is not optional paperwork; it is the input to the deletion design in section 5.

3. GDPR and CCPA as design requirements

You do not need to be a lawyer in an interview. You need to translate the main legal rights into engineering requirements, because each one produces a concrete mechanism. The GDPR (General Data Protection Regulation) is the European Union's law, applying to anyone processing data about people in the EU, wherever the company is based. The CCPA and its extension the CPRA (California) give California residents similar rights. Other regimes exist (Brazil's LGPD, India's DPDP Act, and so on) and they rhyme. Treat the following as a checklist of requirements.

The engineering answer is that these laws all reduce to the same few capabilities: a data map, per-purpose consent, export, deletion, retention, residency, and audit. Build those once as platform features and every regime becomes configuration, not a rewrite.

4. Why deletion is the hardest requirement

Storing data is easy and every system is designed to keep it safe. Erasure fights against the grain of almost every technique in this handbook. Immutable logs (Chapter 36) are designed to never change. Backups are designed to preserve exactly what existed. Caches (Chapter 13) copy data to many places. Search indexes (Chapter 22) denormalize it. Analytics warehouses aggregate it. Each is a place the user's data may live after the main record is gone.

5. Designing deletion across every store

5.1 Soft delete, then purge

A soft delete marks a record as deleted (a deleted_at column or a tombstone flag) instead of removing it. Reads filter it out immediately, so the user sees the effect at once. A later purge job physically removes the rows after a grace period. This two-phase approach is nearly universal because it gives you three things: instant user-visible effect, a window to undo accidental or malicious deletions (account takeover, a mistaken click), and a place to sequence work across systems. The grace period is typically days to a few weeks, and it must be shorter than the legal deadline. Be careful: soft-deleted data is still personal data, so it must not leak into search, exports, or analytics while waiting.

5.2 The deletion pipeline

The robust pattern is an orchestrated, idempotent, observable pipeline:

  1. Accept and verify. Authenticate the requester (a deletion request is a high-risk operation, so require recent login or email confirmation), then write a durable deletion job row with a deadline.
  2. Mark and lock. Soft-delete the account, revoke sessions and tokens, stop outbound processing such as marketing emails.
  3. Fan out. Publish a UserDeleted(user_id, job_id) event, or call each owning service. The owner of each store, not a central team, implements the actual deletion because only it knows its schema.
  4. Acknowledge. Each owner reports success or failure per store. The deletion service tracks a checklist. Failed steps retry with backoff, then alert a human.
  5. Verify and certify. A sweeper scans for the user ID in each store (or a sample) and records proof, then marks the job complete. Keep a minimal ledger entry that "user X was deleted at time T" so that a restored backup can be re-cleaned (section 5.5). That record must itself hold no more than a hashed identifier.

Each handler must be idempotent (see Chapter 32): the event may be delivered twice, and deleting an already-deleted user must succeed quietly.

5.4 Event sourcing and immutable logs

Event sourcing stores state as an append-only sequence of events (Chapter 36), and a Kafka topic or audit log is by design never edited. This directly conflicts with erasure, because the user's name and email are embedded inside old events. There are four standard resolutions, and a good answer names the trade-offs.

  • Keep PII out of events. Events carry an opaque user_id and refer to PII held in a mutable vault (section 7). Deleting the vault record makes the events anonymous. This is the cleanest option and should be the default design.
  • Crypto-shredding. Encrypt each user's PII inside events with a per-user key. To erase, delete the key. The ciphertext remains but is unreadable (section 5.6).
  • Tombstones and compaction. On a compacted Kafka topic, publishing a record with the same key and a null value (a tombstone) causes compaction to eventually remove all earlier values for that key. This works for "latest state per key" topics but not for pure event histories, and it is eventual: it happens when the compaction thread runs, not when you publish.
  • Rewrite the log. Copy the stream, filtering out or redacting events, then swap. Costly and operationally risky; reserve it for rare or small streams.

5.5 Backups

Backups are the awkward corner. Rewriting every backup to remove one person is impractical, and the regulators' accepted position is roughly: backups are not queried in normal operation, are overwritten on a fixed schedule, and are put "beyond use". The engineering rules are therefore: (1) keep backup retention short and bounded (for example 35 days), so that the data ages out; (2) if you ever restore from backup, replay the deletion ledger immediately, before the restored database serves traffic, so deleted users do not come back; (3) use crypto-shredding for long-retention archives so you can render the data unreadable without touching the files. State your retention number out loud; "backups roll off after 35 days, so a delete is fully effective within 35 days of the request" is a strong, specific statement.

5.6 Crypto-shredding

Crypto-shredding turns the question "how do I find and erase every copy of this data" into "how do I destroy one key". You encrypt each user's (or each tenant's) sensitive data with a dedicated data encryption key (DEK). The DEK is stored encrypted by a master key encryption key (KEK) in a key management service (KMS) or hardware security module. This layered scheme is called envelope encryption. Data copies can then be anywhere: in backups, replicas, event logs, analytics. When the user is deleted, you delete that user's wrapped DEK. Every copy of the data becomes random bytes that nobody, including you, can decrypt.

Costs and caveats: you need one key per erasable unit, so the key table is itself critical and must be backed up carefully (losing it loses everyone's data, and a long-lived backup of it would resurrect deleted keys, so back it up with a short retention too). Reads need a key lookup, usually cached in memory with a short TTL, so erasure takes effect after the cache expires. You cannot search or index encrypted fields without extra machinery, so this fits best on payloads and not on query columns. And the argument that "ciphertext counts as deleted" depends on the algorithm staying unbroken; regulators have generally been tolerant but this is not settled law everywhere, so keep it as a layer, not the only defence.

6. Minimization, pseudonymization, anonymization, tokenization

The best deletion strategy is to not hold the data. Data minimization asks, for each field: do we need it, at this precision, for this long? Practical moves include storing the birth year instead of the birth date, a city or a coarse geohash instead of exact coordinates, truncating IP addresses (drop the last octet), keeping a hashed event instead of the raw payload, and dropping raw data once you have computed the aggregate you need.

Four related techniques are often confused. Interviewers like candidates who can separate them precisely.

Also remember that a stable pseudonym lets you build a behavioural profile, which can itself identify a person.

7. The PII isolation vault pattern

The most useful architectural pattern in this area is to separate identity from activity. Instead of every service holding names, emails, and addresses next to its own business data, you create one small, heavily protected service, the PII vault (or identity service), that owns all personal data. Every other service stores only an opaque user_id (a random UUID, never an email or a sequence that leaks signup order).

This single decision improves almost everything in the chapter. Deletion becomes mostly "delete the vault record", after which activity data elsewhere is unlinkable. Export starts at the vault. Breach impact shrinks, because a stolen orders database contains no names. Residency is easier, because only the vault must live in the user's region while the rest may be global (section 8). Auditing is concentrated on one choke point. Costs are the extra network hop (mitigated by caching profile snapshots in the calling service, with a short TTL) and the discipline needed to stop teams copying profile data back into their own tables "for convenience". Enforce this with schema tags and code review, and with a lint that fails builds when a non-vault schema declares a Personal field.

For payment cards the same idea is called tokenization, and it is usually bought rather than built: a payment provider holds card numbers and returns a token, so your systems never see a card, which keeps you out of most PCI DSS scope.

8. Data residency and sovereignty

Data residency means personal data must be stored in a particular country or region. Data sovereignty is the stronger idea that data is subject to the laws of the country where it is stored, so a foreign government's reach may matter. Reasons include GDPR transfer rules, national laws, many public-sector or banking regulations, and customer contracts demanding "EU-only" processing. Residency is often enforced as a hard requirement by large enterprise customers.

The design has these parts:

  • Home region per user (or per tenant). Chosen at signup from the country, the contract, or an explicit choice, and recorded in a small global directory. A routing layer looks up the home region and sends all requests there. This is the same cell idea as Chapter 42, where the cell boundary doubles as a legal boundary.
  • No cross-region replication of personal data. This conflicts with the active-active replication techniques of Chapter 26: you replicate within a region (across availability zones) for durability, and across regions only for non-personal data such as configuration, product catalogue, or anonymized metrics. Disaster recovery then means a second site inside the same legal area (for example Frankfurt and Ireland for the EU).
  • Regional keys. Keys for EU data are generated and held by a KMS in the EU, so that even operators elsewhere cannot decrypt. Some customers require keys they control (customer-managed keys, "bring your own key").
  • Cross-region users. A European user travelling in the US still talks to the EU region; the extra latency is the price. Multi-region features (a global social graph) need care: store edges as IDs, resolve profiles in the owning region.
  • Operations. Support engineers, logs, metrics, and backups are also data processing. Logs from the EU region must stay in the EU or be scrubbed. Debug access from another country is itself a transfer.

The trade-offs are higher cost (duplicate infrastructure per region, lower utilisation), operational complexity, and a harder product story for features that mix users across regions. Mention these when you propose regional silos.

9. Encryption and access auditing

Encryption is the main technical control that regulators and customers expect; the mechanics (TLS, key management, envelope encryption, secrets) belong to Chapter 55. For privacy specifically, remember the three layers. In transit: TLS everywhere, including internal service-to-service traffic. At rest: disk or database encryption protects against stolen media but not against a compromised application, which reads plaintext. Field-level (application-level) encryption: sensitive fields are encrypted before they reach the database with keys the database never sees, so a database administrator or a SQL injection cannot read them. Field-level encryption plus per-user keys is also what makes crypto-shredding possible.

Access auditing answers the question "who looked at this person's data, when, and why". Every read of a Personal or Sensitive field through the vault, every support-tool lookup, and every bulk export writes an append-only audit record: actor, target user, fields, purpose or ticket number, time, and source. The audit store must be tamper-evident (write-once storage, or hash chaining) and should not itself contain the data, only references. Uses: breach scoping (you can say exactly whose data an attacker's stolen credential touched, which determines the notification list), detecting insider abuse (an employee browsing celebrity accounts), and proving compliance. Add purpose-based access control: a support agent can read a profile only while holding an open ticket for that user, and access expires automatically.

11. Cost-aware design principles

Cost-aware design is not about being cheap; it is about spending deliberately. The following habits show up in strong designs.

  • Move less data. Bytes are billed when stored, when moved between zones or regions, and when sent to users. Compress, filter at the source, send deltas, and keep computation close to the data (run the query where the data is instead of copying the data to the query).
  • Keep traffic local. Prefer zone-aware routing so a request is served by instances in the caller's availability zone when possible. Co-locate chatty services. Keep the database primary and its heaviest readers in the same zone, and replicate across zones only what durability needs.
  • Match the resource to the access pattern. Hot, small, latency-critical data belongs on SSD or in memory; large, rarely read data belongs in object storage (Chapter 21); cold archives belong in archive tiers.
  • Cache the expensive thing. A cache is a cost lever, not only a latency lever (section 13).
  • Pay for peak only when at peak. Elastic capacity, spot instances, and scale-to-zero (section 15) turn fixed costs into variable ones.
  • Delete. Retention policies are the cheapest optimization there is, and the same policies satisfy the privacy rules of Part A. Storage that is deleted costs nothing and is not breached.
  • Tag and attribute. Every resource carries owner, team, and product tags so the bill can be sliced. What nobody owns nobody optimizes.

12. Storage tiering, compression and retention

Most data is read heavily when young and almost never when old. A photo is viewed many times in the first week and rarely after a year. A log line is searched during an incident in the first days and almost never later. Storage tiering exploits this by moving data through progressively cheaper (and slower or less convenient) storage classes as it ages.

12.1 Lifecycle rules in practice

Object stores let you declare lifecycle rules: "after 30 days move objects under logs/ to infrequent access, after 180 days to archive, after 7 years delete". Two details matter. First, warm and cold tiers charge a minimum storage duration (often 30 or 90 days) and a retrieval fee, so moving data that will be read again soon can cost more than leaving it. Second, there is also an automatic tiering class that watches access patterns and moves objects for a small monitoring fee per object, a good default when access is unpredictable. Very small objects can be a bad fit for tiers because of per-object overhead and minimum billable sizes, so batch tiny files into larger archives (a log pipeline writes hourly compressed files, not one file per line).

12.2 Compression, formats, and retention

Compression trades CPU for bytes (Chapter 7). Text such as JSON and logs often compresses 5 to 10 times with gzip or zstd; columnar formats such as Parquet add encoding and typically give another multiple while making analytic scans read only needed columns. Images and video are already compressed, so re-compressing them is futile; gains come from picking better codecs and resolutions (section 24). Compress before storing and before sending across zones or regions, because both are billed per byte.

Retention is the other lever. Set explicit retention per dataset: raw clickstream 30 days, aggregated daily tables 2 years, audit logs 7 years because a regulation says so, user uploads until the user deletes them. Combining compression (5x) with retention cut from 365 to 30 days (12x) reduces the log storage bill by about 60 times, with no impact on users.

13. Caching and offloading to reduce cost

A cache helps cost in three ways. It reduces compute, because a cache hit skips the work of rendering or querying. It reduces database cost, because a managed database is priced by the largest instance you need, and absorbing reads in a cache lets you run a smaller one and fewer replicas. It reduces egress when the cache is a CDN (Chapter 12): a CDN charges less per GB than origin egress, and a hit also avoids the origin's compute and its cross-zone traffic. For a read-heavy product with a 95 percent hit rate, the origin sees one twentieth of the load. Remember the other side of the ledger: a cache cluster is itself a cost (large memory is expensive per GB), and low hit rates make it pure overhead. The patterns and pitfalls are in Chapter 13 and Chapter 14.

Other offloading moves: serve static assets from object storage behind a CDN rather than from application servers; use pre-signed URLs so that large uploads and downloads go directly between the client and object storage instead of through your fleet; and answer repeated expensive queries with materialized results computed once per hour.

14. The cost of observability

Observability is the line item teams forget and then discover. Three signals have three cost shapes. Logs cost per byte ingested and per byte retained, so volume is driven by verbosity and request rate. Metrics cost per active time series, and the danger is cardinality: adding a label such as user_id to a metric multiplies series by the number of users and can turn one metric into millions of series. Traces cost per span; recording every span of every request is rarely affordable.

The standard remedies: log at INFO for state changes and WARN or ERROR for problems and never log inside tight loops; sample traces (for example keep 1 percent of normal requests plus all errors and slow outliers, which is called tail-based sampling); keep high-resolution metrics short (10-second resolution for 2 days, rolled up to 1-minute for 30 days and 1-hour for a year); set short retention on debug logs and longer on audit logs; and drop high-cardinality labels, using logs or traces for per-user detail. As a rule of thumb, observability costing 5 to 15 percent of the infrastructure bill is normal; 40 percent is a smell. Privacy ties in here too: less verbose logging means less PII retained.

To use spot safely, design workers to checkpoint progress, make tasks idempotent (Chapter 32), diversify across instance types and zones so one reclamation wave does not remove everything, and handle the termination notice by draining gracefully.

15.2 Right-sizing

Right-sizing means choosing the instance size that fits the measured workload. Typical clusters run at 10 to 25 percent average CPU because they were sized for a feared peak. Measure CPU, memory, and network over weeks, then reduce sizes or counts until the peak sits around 60 to 70 percent utilisation, leaving headroom for failures and spikes. Pick the right instance family: memory-optimized for caches, compute-optimized for CPU-bound services, storage-optimized for databases with heavy local IO. ARM-based instances often give 20 to 40 percent better price-performance for suitable workloads.

15.3 Autoscaling and scale-to-zero

Autoscaling (Chapter 43) adds and removes instances following a metric such as CPU, queue depth, or requests per second. The cost benefit depends on the ratio between peak and average load. If peak traffic is 4 times the daily average, a fleet sized for the peak is 75 percent idle on average; an autoscaler that tracks load can cut compute cost by roughly half or more. Scale-to-zero goes further for workloads that are idle most of the time, such as a dev environment, a nightly report service, or a rarely used internal tool: serverless functions and some container platforms run zero instances (and cost nothing) until a request arrives. The price is the cold start, a delay of hundreds of milliseconds to several seconds while a new instance starts, which is unacceptable for latency-sensitive user paths but fine for batch and background work. Serverless also has a break-even point: per-invocation pricing is excellent at low and bursty volume, but a function running constantly at high volume is usually more expensive than the same work on reserved containers.

16. Build versus buy, and unit economics

16.1 Build versus buy

The question is never "which has the lower list price". Compare total cost of ownership. Running your own message broker costs servers, but also on-call engineers, upgrades, capacity planning, security patching, and the opportunity cost of what those engineers could have built. A managed service charges a premium of perhaps 1.5 to 3 times the raw infrastructure, and that is usually cheaper than even one engineer's fraction of time for a small or medium team. Build (or self-host) when the capability is your core differentiator, when scale makes the premium larger than the engineering cost (a company spending 5 million dollars a year on a managed service may justify a team of five to run it), when you need control that the vendor cannot give, or when lock-in and data gravity are strategic risks. Buy when the capability is commodity (email delivery, payments, identity, hosted search at small scale), when time to market matters, and when the team is small. Also count exit cost: how hard is it to leave the vendor? Strong answers say "buy first, and design the seam so we can replace it".

16.2 Unit economics

Unit economics expresses cost per unit of value: cost per request, per active user per month, per GB stored, per video streamed, per message sent. It is the bridge between engineering and business. Total spend is a poor number because it grows with success; unit cost tells you whether growth is healthy. If a free user costs 4 cents per month to serve and a paying user pays 10 dollars, the maths works. If a free user costs 3 dollars (because of heavy media), it does not, and the design must change before scaling the marketing. Track unit cost over time as a service-level indicator: a rise with no change in behaviour indicates a regression (a new cross-AZ call, a cache that stopped hitting).

19. Offline-first design and sync

An offline-first app treats the local database as the primary data source for the user interface and the network as a background synchronization channel. The user reads and writes instantly against local data, whether or not the network exists, and the app reconciles with the server when it can. This makes the app feel fast (no spinner waiting on a round trip) and robust (a lift ride does not lose a note).

19.1 The building blocks

  • Local database. Usually SQLite (via platform libraries) or an object store built on it. Each synced row carries a stable client-generated ID (a UUID, so records can be created offline without a server-assigned ID), a version or timestamp, and a sync status (synced, pending, conflict).
  • Queue of mutations (outbox). Instead of "send the whole note", the client records an operation: {id, type: update_note, note_id, fields, base_version}. The queue is persisted, ordered, and survives app restarts. Each operation carries a unique ID so the server can deduplicate retries (idempotency, Chapter 32), because a response may be lost after the server has applied the write.
  • Sync token (cursor). The server returns an opaque token with each pull representing "everything up to here". The next pull sends the token and receives only what changed since. The token might encode a server-side sequence number or a change-log position. This is delta sync: transfer cost is proportional to the changes, not the dataset.
  • Tombstones. A deleted record cannot simply vanish from the server's change feed, or a device that was offline would never learn about the deletion and would keep (and maybe re-upload) its stale copy. The server keeps a tombstone ({id, deleted_at, version}) for a retention window, and the delta feed includes tombstones. If a device is offline longer than the tombstone retention, the server tells it to do a full resync instead of a delta. This is the same tombstone idea as in Part A, and it matters for privacy: tombstones must contain no personal data, only the ID.
  • Pagination of the initial sync. The first sync of a large account downloads in pages with a resumable cursor so that an interruption does not restart from zero.

19.2 Conflict resolution

A conflict happens when two devices (or a device and the server) change the same record while disconnected. Offline-first apps are eventually consistent systems (Chapter 28): they choose availability when partitioned, so conflicts must be resolved, not prevented. The usual options are:

StrategyHow it worksFits
Last write wins (LWW)The change with the later timestamp wins; the other is discarded.Simple settings and flags. Dangerous for text: one edit is silently lost. Device clocks are unreliable, so prefer server-assigned time or a version number.
Field-level mergeTrack changes per field, so editing the title on one device and the colour on another both survive.Structured records. Most practical default.
Optimistic concurrency with rejectionEach mutation carries the base version; the server rejects it if the record has moved on. The client fetches the new version and re-applies or asks the user.When correctness matters (inventory, money) and conflicts are rare.
Manual resolutionKeep both versions and show the user ("conflicted copy").Documents and files where automatic merging is risky.
CRDTs or operational transformData structures or transformations designed so concurrent edits merge deterministically and converge without coordination (Chapter 59).Collaborative text, counters, sets; used by real-time editors.
Watch out Never use the device clock as the only conflict arbiter. Phones have wrong time zones, manual clocks, and drift. Use server-assigned versions or hybrid logical clocks (Chapter 27), and make mutations idempotent so that retries after a lost response do not apply twice.

20. Retry and backoff on clients

Because mobile networks fail constantly, clients retry. Done badly, retries become a self-inflicted denial of service: after a 30-second server outage, ten million phones that all retry every second create a retry storm that prevents recovery. Rules for a good client:

  • Exponential backoff. Wait 1 s, then 2, 4, 8, up to a cap such as 60 seconds, doubling each time.
  • Jitter. Randomize each wait (for example choose a random value between half and full of the computed delay) so clients do not retry in synchronized waves. This matters more than the exponent.
  • Retry only what is safe and useful. Retry network errors, timeouts, and 5xx or 429 responses. Do not retry 4xx client errors such as 400 or 404; they will fail again. Honour a Retry-After header when the server sends one.
  • Idempotency keys. Non-idempotent operations (create an order) carry a client-generated key so a retry cannot duplicate them (Chapter 5).
  • Budgets and limits. Cap the number of attempts and total elapsed time, then surface the failure or park the work in the mutation queue. Use a client-side circuit breaker, and let the server shed load with 429 or 503 (Chapter 16, Chapter 40).
  • Network awareness. Wait for a connectivity signal rather than blindly retrying while the radio is off; use platform schedulers (WorkManager on Android, background tasks on iOS) that batch retries across apps for battery.

21. Push notifications

A phone cannot let every app hold its own always-on connection; it would destroy the battery. Instead each operating system runs one persistent, efficient connection of its own, and every app's notifications ride on it. On iOS that is the Apple Push Notification service (APNs); on Android it is Firebase Cloud Messaging (FCM) (web push uses a similar standard-based service). Your backend does not talk to the phone. It hands a message to APNs or FCM, which delivers it over the OS connection.

21.1 How it works

  1. Registration. On first launch (after the user grants permission) the app asks the OS for a device token, an opaque string identifying this app on this device. The app uploads it to your backend, which stores (user_id, device_token, platform, app_version, last_seen). A user may have several devices; a device may change users on login.
  2. Token lifecycle. Tokens change (app reinstall, OS restore, periodic rotation), so the app refreshes the stored token on every launch. APNs and FCM reply with errors for unregistered or invalid tokens; the backend must delete those, or the registry bloats with dead entries and every send wastes work.
  3. Sending. A notification service accepts a request such as "tell user 42 about this comment", applies preferences and dedup (the notification system case study), builds a platform payload (title, body, deep link, collapse key), and sends it to APNs or FCM over HTTP/2 with credentials (a signing key for APNs, a service account for FCM).
  4. Delivery. The platform holds the message and pushes it to the device when reachable. If the device is offline, it stores the message briefly and delivers on reconnect, and discards it after a time-to-live you set (or the platform's maximum).

21.2 Properties to state precisely

  • Delivery is not guaranteed. Push is best effort. Messages may be delayed, coalesced, throttled by the OS, or dropped when the device is off for long, the user disabled notifications, or battery saver is on. Never rely on push as the only way a user learns something critical, and never use it as the source of truth. Use it as a hint: "something changed, come and sync", then fetch authoritative state with an API call.
  • Payloads are small (a few kilobytes, typically 4 KB), and message content passes through the platform vendor, so do not put sensitive data in the payload. Send an ID and let the app fetch details after authentication, or use a notification extension that decrypts an end-to-end encrypted payload.
  • Collapse keys. For updates such as "3 new messages", a collapse key (a "collapse identifier" on Apple) tells the platform to replace an older undelivered message with the newer one, so a returning device does not receive 50 stale alerts.
  • Priority and TTL. High priority wakes the device immediately and uses more battery; normal priority may be batched. Set a time-to-live so a "your driver has arrived" alert is not delivered an hour late.
  • Silent (background) push. A message with no visible alert that wakes the app briefly to sync data. Useful to trigger a delta sync, but the OS rate-limits it heavily, delivery is even less reliable, and users who force-quit the app on iOS may not receive it. Treat it as an optimization, with periodic sync as a fallback.

21.3 Fan-out at scale

Sending a breaking news alert to 50 million devices is a write fan-out problem (Chapter 35). Do not loop in a request handler. Instead: put one job into a queue, have a splitter job page through the device registry in batches (for example 1,000 tokens per task) and enqueue per-batch send tasks, run many stateless workers that call APNs and FCM in parallel over pooled HTTP/2 connections, and respect each platform's rate guidance. At 50 million devices and, say, 20,000 sends per second sustained across the worker fleet, delivery of the last message takes 50,000,000 divided by 20,000 = 2,500 seconds, about 42 minutes. If the product needs it in 5 minutes, you need about 170,000 sends per second, so you shard the registry by user ID, scale workers horizontally, and may stagger sends across regions to avoid thundering herds against your own API when a million users tap the notification at once. Track metrics: accepted by the platform, rejected tokens, and (via the app) opened. Note that "accepted by APNs" means the platform has the message, not that the phone received it.

22. Long polling, WebSocket, or push

When the app is open and in the foreground, you can keep your own connection, as covered in Chapter 6. When it is in the background, you cannot, and push is the only reliable wake-up path. A sensible mobile design uses both:

MechanismWorks whenCost on mobileTypical use

WebSocket or SSEApp in foregroundPersistent connection costs battery and server memory; must handle reconnects (backoff, resume from last event ID)Chat, live scores, collaborative editing while the screen is on.

The common pattern: open a socket while the app is in the foreground for instant updates; on backgrounding close it; use push to wake the user or the app; on wake run a delta sync using the sync token. Each reconnect must be resumable: the client sends the last event ID or sync token it processed so that nothing is missed or duplicated.

23. API design for mobile

Everything in Chapter 5 and Chapter 7 applies, with mobile-specific emphasis because each round trip is slow and each byte may cost the user money.

  • Pagination. Use cursor-based pagination (an opaque cursor, not page numbers) for feeds and lists: stable under inserts, cheap for the database, and resumable after a dropped connection. Keep pages small (20 to 50 items) and let the app prefetch the next page as the user scrolls.
  • Batching and aggregation. A home screen that needs six resources should not make six sequential calls over a 300 ms network (1.8 seconds of latency). Offer a batch endpoint or a backend-for-frontend (BFF) that composes the data and returns one response shaped for that screen. GraphQL gives clients the same ability with field selection. The trade-off is a more specific API to maintain and the risk of over-fetching if the batch includes rarely needed parts, so allow clients to request sections.
  • Compression and lean payloads. Enable gzip or brotli on responses (often a 5x reduction for JSON). Consider Protobuf or another binary format for high-volume APIs. Omit unused fields, use sparse fieldsets, and send partial updates with PATCH. Use HTTP/2 or HTTP/3, which multiplex requests over one connection and recover better from loss, and which speed up connection setup (important on high-latency links).
  • Conditional requests. ETag or Last-Modified with If-None-Match lets the server answer 304 Not Modified with no body when nothing changed.
  • Resumable and chunked uploads. Upload large files in chunks with a resumable protocol or multipart upload to object storage (Chapter 21) via pre-signed URLs, so a dropped connection resumes at the last good chunk.
  • Versioning and forced upgrades. Because old app versions never disappear, APIs are versioned, additive, and tolerant. Clients must ignore unknown fields; servers must not remove or change meaning of existing fields while old versions are active. Send the app version in a header (for example X-App-Version or in User-Agent) so the server can adapt. For the rare incompatible change or security fix, implement a minimum supported version check: the server returns a specific error (for example HTTP 426 Upgrade Required or a structured code), and the client shows a blocking "please update" screen. Use it sparingly, announce deprecations ahead, and measure the version distribution so you know when an old API can be removed.

24. Images, video and the CDN

Media is usually the largest share of bytes, so it is the biggest mobile and cost lever. Serve images through a CDN (Chapter 12) from object storage. Generate multiple variants at upload time or on demand (thumbnail, small, medium, large, for 1x, 2x, and 3x screen densities) and let the client request the size it will actually display; sending a 4000-pixel photo to fill a 100-pixel avatar wastes 99 percent of the bytes. Use modern formats (WebP, AVIF for images; HEVC, VP9 or AV1 for video) with a fallback for devices that cannot decode them, and negotiate by the Accept header or client capability. Add blur-up placeholders (a tiny preview that loads instantly) and lazy loading so off-screen images are not fetched.

For video use adaptive bitrate streaming (HLS or DASH): the video is encoded into several quality levels, split into a few seconds long segments, and the player picks the level per segment according to measured bandwidth, dropping quality on a bad network instead of stalling. Cache segments at the CDN so popular videos are served from the edge. Upload paths should compress or transcode on the device where reasonable and resume after failures. Image transformations on the fly at the edge are convenient but cost compute; cache the results and bound the set of allowed sizes to avoid an unbounded number of variants.

25. Client caching, feature flags and kill switches

25.1 Client-side caching

The client is a cache tier like any other. HTTP caching with correct Cache-Control, ETag, and max-age headers lets the OS network stack avoid requests entirely. An in-memory cache serves the current session; a disk cache for images and API responses survives restarts, with a bounded size and an eviction policy (LRU) so low-end devices do not run out of space. Use stale-while-revalidate: show the cached content instantly and refresh in the background, updating the screen if it changed. The usual cache invalidation difficulty applies (Chapter 13): version cache keys, clear on logout (a privacy requirement: the next user of a shared device must not see the previous user's data), and purge on account deletion.

25.2 Feature flags and kill switches

Because you cannot instantly update installed apps, remote control is essential. A feature flag is a server-delivered setting that turns functionality on or off, or varies it by user segment, app version, country, or percentage (Chapter 43). The app fetches flags at launch (and periodically), caches them locally so they work offline, and falls back to safe defaults when the fetch fails. Flags allow gradual rollouts (1 percent, then 10, then 100), A/B tests, and per-version targeting. A kill switch is a flag specifically meant to disable a feature fast if it misbehaves: a crashing new screen, an expensive background sync, an integration with a failing third party. Because an app store release takes days, the kill switch may be your only way to stop an incident that is already on millions of phones. Design rules: code paths behind flags must be tested in both states; the flag service must be highly available and the client must tolerate its absence; keep the flag payload small and cacheable (CDN); remove old flags to avoid a combinatorial mess; and never use client flags as security, since a user can alter local state.

26. Analytics events and batching

Product analytics means the app emits many small events (screen viewed, button tapped). Sending each as its own HTTP request would be disastrous for battery, data, and server load. Instead the SDK batches: it appends events to a local persistent queue, then flushes when a threshold is reached (say 20 events, or 30 seconds, or when the app goes to the background) as one compressed request, ideally while on Wi-Fi or charging for non-urgent data. Each event has a client-generated ID and a client timestamp plus an upload timestamp, so the server can deduplicate retries and correct for device clock skew (server time minus upload time gives an offset). The server endpoint should be a thin, cheap ingestion service that validates and writes to a log such as Kafka (Chapter 34), returning quickly. Add sampling for noisy events, a cap on the local queue so a long offline period cannot fill the disk, and a consent check before collecting anything, as required in Part A. Do not put personal data in event properties; use the pseudonymous ID.

27. Worked example: a GDPR deletion pipeline

Requirement: a social app with 100 million users in EU and US regions. A user taps "Delete my account". We must erase their data in every system within 30 days, show the user an immediate effect, handle failures, and be able to prove it.

27.1 Design

  • Data map. Seven owners: the PII vault (profile, email, phone), the posts service, the media service (object storage plus CDN), the search index, the analytics warehouse, the notification registry (device tokens), and third-party processors (email vendor, support desk). The user is in exactly one region, found via the directory, so the job runs in that region; the global directory entry is removed last.
  • Phase 1, instant (seconds). Verify identity, soft-delete the account, revoke sessions and device tokens (so no push arrives), hide the profile and posts from reads and search results through a deletion filter, and queue the job. Offer a 14-day recovery window: during it the data is hidden but present. This protects against account takeover abuse.
  • Phase 2, purge (after the grace period). Each owner executes its own deletion, idempotently. The vault deletes the profile and the user's wrapped encryption key. Posts: delete the user's posts or anonymize them if they are part of other users' conversation threads (replace author with "deleted user"). Media: delete the per-user object prefix and all versions, then issue a CDN purge for the URLs. Search: delete documents by user ID. Warehouse: delete the pseudonym-to-user mapping so that historic events become unlinkable, and queue partition rewrites for tables that still hold direct identifiers. Notifications: remove tokens. Third parties: call deletion APIs and record request IDs.
  • Immutable streams. Events in Kafka hold only user_id; the sensitive payloads are encrypted with the per-user key. Deleting the key in Phase 2 renders them unreadable. Topics that hold latest state per key get a tombstone.
  • Backups. Retention 35 days, so the final residue disappears within 35 days of purge, which lands inside the 30-day expectation plus documented backup exception. The deletion ledger (hashed user ID, timestamp) is replayed after any restore.
  • Verification and audit. A sweeper queries each store for the user ID and records "zero rows" evidence. The audit log records who requested and which steps ran, without personal data. Dashboards track the age of the oldest open job and the failure count per owner; an alert fires when a job is older than 20 days.
  • Legal holds and exceptions. Invoices needed for tax law are kept with the minimum fields for the legal period, under a different purpose and a separate table, and the pipeline skips them explicitly and records why.

27.2 Numbers

Suppose 0.05 percent of 100M users delete each month: 50,000 jobs per month, under 2,000 per day, about one per minute. This is a trivially small event rate, so use a simple durable queue and workers. The expensive part is bulk work such as the warehouse rewrite: batch jobs once a week that group all pending deletions and rewrite each affected partition once, rather than one rewrite per user. If a single weekly rewrite scans 20 TB at a few cents per GB-scan-equivalent, that is on the order of hundreds of dollars a week, which is why you batch requests and use a mapping-table deletion for most analytics rather than rewriting. Also budget the compliance cost of the key store and audit log, which is small compared with a fine of up to 4 percent of global annual turnover under the GDPR.

28. Worked example: mobile offline notes sync

Requirement: a notes app with 20 million users, an average of 200 notes of 5 KB each, three devices per heavy user, offline editing, and sync within seconds when online.

28.1 Data and API

  • Server data model. A notes table keyed by (user_id, note_id) with title, body, version, updated_seq, and deleted flag. A per-user monotonically increasing change sequence number assigns each change its position in a per-user change log. The sync token is simply the last sequence seen.
  • Endpoints. POST /sync/push with an array of mutations (each with a unique mutation ID, note ID, base version, changed fields); GET /sync/pull?since=token&limit=200 returning changes, tombstones, and a new token, plus a more flag for paging.
  • Idempotency. The server stores recent mutation IDs per device (for example 7 days) and returns the original result for repeats.
  • Conflicts. Field-level merge for title, and for body a three-way text merge using the base version, and when that cannot merge cleanly, the server keeps both and marks a "conflicted copy" the user can reconcile.
  • Tombstones. Retained 90 days in the change log. A client with a token older than that gets a resync_required error and does a full download.

28.2 Client behaviour

  1. Editing writes to SQLite immediately and appends a mutation to the outbox.
  2. The sync engine pushes when connected, with exponential backoff and jitter on failure (1 s base, 5 min cap).
  3. After a push it pulls with the stored token, applies changes in a transaction, and saves the new token only after applying them, so a crash replays the pull instead of losing changes.
  4. When the app is foregrounded it opens a WebSocket for instant updates. When backgrounded, a high-priority push or silent push signals "changes available" and the app pulls; if that push is missed, the next launch pulls anyway. The push payload contains no note content.
  5. On logout or account deletion the local database and caches are wiped.

28.3 Numbers

Per user, 200 notes x 5 KB = 1 MB, so 20M users hold 20 TB (about 60 TB with three replicas, a modest relational or key-value cluster sharded by user_id, Chapter 25). A full initial sync downloads 1 MB, compressed to about 200 KB, a few seconds even on a poor connection, and is paged by 200 notes. Steady state: if an active user makes 20 edits a day, a user's day produces about 20 small pushes (batched by the engine into maybe 8 requests). With 5M daily active users that is 40M push requests per day, about 460 per second on average and perhaps 1,500 at peak, which a few dozen modest servers handle. Delta pulls are typically a few hundred bytes, whereas a naive "download everything each time" approach would transfer 200 KB times 8 times 5M = 8 TB per day of avoidable egress, roughly 700 dollars per day at 9 cents per GB. That one design choice, delta sync, pays for itself many times and also saves phone battery and user data plans.

29. Common mistakes

  • Copying profile data into every service "for convenience", then discovering during a deletion request that it lives in twelve places.
  • Putting emails, tokens, or full request bodies in logs and error trackers.
  • Treating backups and event logs as exempt from retention and erasure planning.
  • Using cross-region replication of everything, then learning that EU data left the EU.
  • Optimizing instance counts while egress, the database, and logging are ten times larger.
  • Assuming push is reliable, or sending sensitive content in the payload.
  • Retrying immediately and without jitter from millions of clients.
  • Trusting device clocks for conflict resolution, or hard-deleting server rows so offline clients never hear about it.
  • Shipping an API change that breaks app versions still in use, with no kill switch.

30. Interview questions and answers

How would you implement the right to erasure in a system with microservices, caches, search, and a data warehouse?

I would first build a data map and classify fields so that every store holding personal data is known. Then I would add a deletion orchestrator: it records a job, soft-deletes the account for immediate effect, and publishes a UserDeleted event. Each owning service deletes its own copy idempotently and acknowledges, and the orchestrator retries failures and alerts when a job gets old. For hard stores I use special techniques: crypto-shredding for payloads in logs and backups, mapping-table deletion in the warehouse, short backup retention, and a deletion ledger replayed after restores. Finally a sweeper verifies zero remaining references and I keep an audit record of the job.

Event sourcing keeps an immutable history. How can you comply with erasure?

The best approach is to keep personal data out of events: store an opaque user ID in the event and the PII in a mutable vault, so deleting the vault record anonymizes the history. If personal data must be in the events, encrypt it with a per-user key and delete the key (crypto-shredding), which makes all copies unreadable even in backups. On compacted topics a tombstone eventually removes older values for the key, but that is eventual and does not work for pure histories. The last resort is rewriting the stream, which is expensive and risky. I would also document the retention period, because auditors want a clear statement of when data is gone.

What is the difference between pseudonymization and anonymization, and why does it matter?

Pseudonymization replaces identifiers with a stable pseudonym while a separate table can map them back, so the data is still personal data in law and still needs protection, retention limits, and deletion. Anonymization irreversibly removes the ability to identify anyone, and properly anonymized data falls outside GDPR. It matters because teams often claim a dataset is anonymous when it is only pseudonymous, for example a hashed email, which can be reversed by hashing candidate emails. True anonymization is hard because combinations of quasi-identifiers can re-identify people, so aggregation, generalization, or differential privacy are needed.

How would you design for data residency in multiple regions?

I would make each region a self-contained silo with its own storage, keys, and PII vault, and assign each user a home region recorded in a small global directory that holds no personal data. A routing layer sends all requests for that user to the home region. Replication of personal data stays within the region (across zones) and disaster recovery uses a second location inside the same legal area; only non-personal or anonymized data is shared globally. I would also account for logs, backups, and support access as processing that must stay in region. The costs are duplicated infrastructure and harder cross-region features, which is an acceptable price for the constraint.

Your cloud bill doubled in a quarter. How do you investigate?

I start with cost attribution by tags and service to find which line grew, then separate volume growth from unit cost growth by dividing by requests or active users. Common culprits are egress (missing CDN or uncompressed responses), cross-AZ traffic from a new chatty call, storage that never expires, log volume, and over-provisioned instances. For each I pick a lever: caching or CDN, compression, lifecycle rules and retention, log sampling, right-sizing, or spot and reserved capacity. I would also set budget alerts and track cost per request so a regression is caught within days instead of at the end of the quarter.

When would you choose spot instances, reserved capacity, or on-demand?

Reserved capacity or savings plans cover the always-on baseline because the 30 to 60 percent discount only pays off if you actually use it all year. On-demand covers unpredictable or short-lived demand and the part of the peak that you cannot forecast. Spot gives 60 to 90 percent discounts for work that tolerates interruption, such as batch jobs, queue workers, CI, and stateless web capacity behind a load balancer, provided tasks are idempotent, checkpoint progress, and drain on the termination notice. A well-run fleet mixes all three and diversifies spot across instance types and zones so one reclamation wave does not take everything.

How does an offline-first mobile app sync with the server, and how are conflicts handled?

The UI reads and writes a local database. Each write is also saved as an idempotent mutation in a persistent outbox with a unique ID and base version. The sync engine pushes mutations when online, retrying with backoff and jitter, then pulls changes since its sync token and applies them locally, saving the new token only afterwards. Deletes travel as tombstones so offline devices learn about them. Conflicts are detected by comparing base versions; I resolve them with field-level merge where possible, optimistic rejection for critical data, and keeping both copies for text that cannot merge. For truly collaborative editing I would use CRDTs or operational transform.

Why is push notification delivery not guaranteed, and how do you design around it?

Push goes through APNs or FCM, which are best-effort: messages can be delayed, coalesced, dropped after a time-to-live, or throttled by the operating system, and users can disable notifications or put the app into restrictive battery modes. Silent pushes are even less reliable. So I treat a push as a hint that tells the app to sync, never as the source of truth, and keep the state in the server where the app pulls it on launch and on foreground. I set time-to-live and collapse keys to avoid stale floods, keep payloads small and free of sensitive data, prune invalid tokens, and measure open rates instead of assuming delivery.

How do you send a notification to 50 million devices?

I enqueue a single broadcast job and let a splitter page through the device registry in batches, creating per-batch send tasks that a large pool of stateless workers executes against APNs and FCM over pooled HTTP/2 connections. Throughput is sized from the deadline: 50 million in five minutes needs about 170,000 sends per second, so I shard by user ID and scale workers horizontally. I handle platform errors by deleting invalid tokens, retry transient failures with backoff, and stagger sends or rate-limit the follow-up traffic so a million users tapping at once do not overload my own API. Metrics track accepted, rejected, and opened counts.

How do you evolve an API when old app versions stay in use for years?

I make changes additive and backward compatible: new optional fields, new endpoints, never changing the meaning of existing fields, and clients that ignore unknown fields. The app sends its version so the server can adapt, and I monitor the version distribution to know when a deprecated path is safe to remove. For the unavoidable breaking change or a security fix, a minimum-supported-version check makes the server return an upgrade-required response, and the app shows a blocking update screen. Feature flags and kill switches let me turn off a faulty feature on installed apps without waiting for an app store release.

What does a back-of-envelope cost estimate look like in an interview?

I convert the load estimate to resources and then to dollars per line: compute from peak requests per second and per-core throughput, egress from requests times response size with a CDN offload ratio, storage from users times bytes with tier prices, the database as a managed instance, and logs from requests times line size. I add cross-zone traffic and observability because they are often forgotten. Then I divide by active users or requests for a unit cost and state which two lines dominate, usually egress and the database, and which lever reduces each. Order-of-magnitude accuracy is the goal, and I say which prices I am assuming.

31. Recap

  • Privacy: classify and tag fields, keep a data map, and turn GDPR and CCPA rights into platform capabilities: consent, export, deletion, retention, residency, breach response, and audit.
  • Deletion is hard because data is copied everywhere. Use soft delete then purge, an idempotent orchestrated pipeline with acknowledgements, tombstones, short backup retention with a deletion ledger, and crypto-shredding for immutable stores and logs.
  • Minimize first. Pseudonymization is reversible and still personal; anonymization is irreversible and hard; tokenization moves risk into a vault; plain hashes of low-entropy values are not safe.
  • Isolate PII in a vault with opaque IDs everywhere else, and give each legal region its own silo, keys, and vault, routed by a personal-data-free directory.
  • Cost: know the drivers (compute, storage, egress, requests, cross-AZ traffic, managed premiums, observability), rank them for your system, and pull the right levers: tiering with lifecycle rules, compression, retention, caching and CDN, reserved plus spot capacity, right-sizing, autoscaling, and scale-to-zero where cold starts are acceptable.
  • Think in unit economics: cost per request and per active user, estimate with simple arithmetic, and name the dominant lines. Build versus buy is a total-cost-of-ownership decision.
  • Mobile means battery, flaky networks, data costs, and old app versions. Go offline-first with a local database, an idempotent mutation queue, sync tokens, delta sync, tombstones, and a deliberate conflict policy.
  • Clients retry with exponential backoff and jitter, idempotency keys, and budgets. Push (APNs and FCM) is best effort and a hint to sync; use sockets only in the foreground; shape APIs with cursors, batching, compression, versioning, and a minimum-version gate; optimize media with variants and CDN; control behaviour remotely with feature flags and kill switches; batch analytics.
