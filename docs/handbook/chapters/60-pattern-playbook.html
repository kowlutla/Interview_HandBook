Chapter 60 · Module 10 · Pattern Playbook

Recurring Design Patterns

System design interviews look endlessly varied, but the same twenty or so problems keep returning in different clothes. This chapter is a catalogue: for each recurring problem it gives the technique, a diagram, the tradeoffs, realistic numbers, and a pointer to the chapter that owns the details, so you can recognise a pattern in seconds and then go deep where the interviewer probes.

1. How to use this playbook

Earlier chapters teach components one at a time: a cache, a queue, a shard map, a lock service. Real systems are built by combining those components to answer a recurring question. "How do I stop two buyers getting the same last seat?" is not a question about Redis or about SQL; it is a question about contention, and the answer is a small set of techniques that can be implemented in either. Experienced engineers think in these named patterns first and in products second. That is the habit this chapter builds.

Each pattern below follows the same five beats so you can scan quickly:

  • Problem: the symptom or requirement that triggers the pattern, in plain language.
  • Technique: the standard moves, explained one at a time, with just enough mechanism to be credible.
  • Tradeoffs: what you give up (consistency, freshness, complexity, money) in exchange.
  • Numbers: back-of-envelope figures that tell you when the pattern is needed. These are typical orders of magnitude, not guarantees; always re-derive them for the problem in front of you with the method in Chapter 2.
  • Owner: links to the chapters that teach the internals. This chapter deliberately does not re-teach them.
Interview tip When you hear a requirement, name the pattern out loud before naming a technology: "this is a hot-key problem, so I would split the key and coalesce misses." Naming the pattern shows you have seen the problem class before, and it lets the interviewer steer you to the depth they care about.

A note on the order. Sections 2 to 5 are about load shape (reads, writes, hot spots, contention). Sections 6 to 11 are about data and content shape (big files, counters, presence, money, search, geography). Sections 12 to 23 are about asynchrony and coordination (schedulers, notifications, queues, idempotency, sync, limits, tenancy, ML, media, leaderboards, workflows, degradation). Sections 24 to 26 are the practical payoff: a symptom-to-pattern table, a combined worked example, and Q&A.

2. Read-heavy versus write-heavy shaping

2.1 Problem

The first question to ask of any data path is the read-to-write ratio, because the two directions are scaled by opposite techniques. A profile page, a product catalogue or a URL redirect may be read a thousand times for every write. A telemetry pipeline, a chat message log or an ad-click stream is the reverse: writes dominate and reads are rare or aggregated. If you apply read-scaling tricks to a write-heavy system you add cost and staleness for no gain, and the converse fails just as badly.

2.2 Technique for read-heavy systems

  1. Cache the hot subset. Put an in-memory cache in front of the database using the cache-aside pattern from Chapter 13. Because access is skewed (a small fraction of keys gets most of the reads), a cache holding 5 to 20 percent of the data frequently serves 90 percent or more of requests.
  2. Add read replicas. Followers copy the leader's log and serve reads, as covered in Chapter 24. This scales read throughput roughly linearly but introduces replica lag, so a user may not see their own write; the usual fix is to route a user's reads to the leader for a short window after they write.
  3. Denormalise and precompute. Store the answer the page needs (a joined, aggregated, ready-to-render row) rather than computing it per request. This is the idea behind materialised views and behind CQRS (command query responsibility segregation): a normalised write model feeds one or more read models shaped for queries.
  4. Push static or semi-static content to the edge with a CDN, from Chapter 12.

2.3 Technique for write-heavy systems

  1. Choose a write-optimised storage engine. A log-structured merge tree (LSM) turns random writes into sequential appends to a memtable and a write-ahead log, then merges sorted files in the background. Chapter 19 explains why this sustains far higher write rates than an in-place B-tree, at the cost of read and space amplification.
  2. Batch. Group many small writes into one larger one: one Kafka produce request carries hundreds of records; one database round trip inserts a hundred rows. Batching trades a few milliseconds of latency for an order of magnitude more throughput, because per-request overhead (network, fsync, parsing) is amortised.
  3. Buffer through a log or queue. Absorb bursts into a durable log (Chapter 34) and let consumers write to the database at a rate it can sustain. The queue converts a spiky arrival rate into a smooth drain rate.
  4. Shard by a key that spreads writes evenly (Chapter 25), since a single leader is the write ceiling in leader-based replication.
  5. Aggregate before storing. If consumers only need per-minute totals, do not store every event in the hot store; roll up first (section 7).

Key idea Measure the ratio first. A read-to-write ratio above about 10 to 1 points to caching and replicas; a ratio near 1 to 1 or below points to batching, logs, LSM storage and sharding. Most real systems are read-heavy for the user-facing path and write-heavy for the ingestion path, and good designs shape the two paths separately.

3. Fan-out on write, fan-out on read, and the hybrid

3.1 Problem

A feed, timeline or inbox answers the question "show me what is new from everyone I follow." The data is written once (a post) but read by many followers. Fan-out is the act of spreading one event to many recipients, and the design question is when to do that work: at write time or at read time. The case study in Chapter C6 works the full design; here is the reusable shape.

3.2 Technique

  • Fan-out on write (push). When Alice posts, the system immediately appends the post id to the precomputed feed list of each of her followers (typically a capped list in a cache or wide-column store). Reading a feed is then one cheap lookup. The cost moves to the write: a user with 1,000 followers causes 1,000 list inserts.
  • Fan-out on read (pull). The post is stored once. When a follower opens the feed, the system fetches the recent posts of every account they follow and merges them by time. Writes are cheap; reads are expensive, because a user following 500 accounts triggers 500 lookups plus a merge.
  • Hybrid. Push for ordinary accounts, pull for very large ones. The "celebrity problem" is that an account with 50 million followers would make a single post cost 50 million writes. So the system marks such accounts as pull-based: their posts are not pushed. At read time, the feed service takes the follower's precomputed (pushed) list and merges in the recent posts of the few celebrities that user follows.

3.3 Tradeoffs and numbers

ApproachWrite costRead costBest when
PushO(followers) per postO(1)Reads vastly outnumber posts, follower counts are bounded
PullO(1)O(followees) plus mergeFew followees per reader, or users read rarely
HybridO(followers) for normal, O(1) for celebritiesO(1) plus a few celebrity fetchesHeavy-tailed follower distribution (almost every social network)

Quick arithmetic: 100 million daily active users, each posting twice a day, average 200 followers, gives 200 million posts times 200 = 40 billion list inserts a day, about 460,000 per second on average and perhaps 1.5 million at peak. That is feasible only with a sharded in-memory store and asynchronous workers, and it is the reason pure push needs the celebrity exception. Push is also wasteful for inactive users, so many systems fan out only to followers active in the last few weeks and rebuild the list on demand for others.

Watch out Push makes deletes and edits harder: removing a post means finding every list that contains its id. The usual answer is to store only ids in the feed list and filter against a "deleted" or "visibility" check at read time, so the post store stays the single source of truth.

4. Hot keys and hot partitions

4.1 Problem

Sharding spreads keys evenly, but it cannot spread traffic evenly when a single key is popular. One viral product, one celebrity profile, or one tenant that is 100 times larger than the rest sends a disproportionate share of requests to one cache node or one database partition, which saturates while the rest of the fleet idles. Adding nodes does not help, because the hot key still lives on exactly one of them. The signature is one shard at 100 percent CPU with the others near 10 percent, and p99 latency spikes. Chapter 14 covers hot keys and stampedes in caches, and Chapter 25 covers hot partitions in storage.

4.2 Technique

  1. Detect first. Sample request keys (for example one in a hundred) and keep a heavy-hitter sketch, a count-min sketch plus a small top-k list from Chapter 57, so you know which keys are hot without storing every key.
  2. Local (in-process) caching. Cache the hot key inside each application server for a short TTL, a second or a few seconds. With 200 app servers, the remote cache now sees at most 200 requests per TTL for that key instead of millions. This is the cheapest and most effective move.
  3. Key splitting (salting). Store the hot value under several keys, product:42#0 to product:42#9, and have each reader pick one at random. Reads spread over ten nodes. Writes must update all copies, so this suits read-hot, rarely-written keys. For write-hot keys, split the other way: write to a random sub-key and aggregate on read (this is the sharded counter of section 7).
  4. Request coalescing (single flight). When many requests miss on the same key simultaneously, let one request fetch from the origin and make the others wait for its result. This prevents a cache stampede from turning one popular miss into thousands of identical database queries.
  5. Replicate hot data. Give the hot partition more read replicas or move it to a dedicated node, or split the partition by a finer key (range split for sorted stores).
  6. Isolate noisy tenants. Put very large tenants on their own shard or cell (section 18).

4.3 Tradeoffs and numbers

A single Redis node commonly sustains on the order of 100,000 simple operations per second; a hot key that draws 500,000 reads per second cannot be served by any single node however large. A one-second local TTL cuts remote load for that key to (number of app servers) per second. The tradeoff is staleness of up to the local TTL, so do not use it for values that must be exact, such as an account balance. Salting multiplies memory by the split factor and complicates invalidation, so apply it only to keys that detection has proved hot.

5. Contention control: reservations, CAS, queues and flash sales

5.1 Problem

Contention is many actors competing for a scarce, shared thing: the last concert seat, the last unit of a discounted product, a driver who can accept only one ride, a username. Without control, two buyers both read "1 left", both decide to buy, and you oversell. A flash sale makes it worse by concentrating thousands of requests per second on a handful of rows. The full ticketing design is in Chapter C15, and the locking primitives are in Chapter 30.

5.2 Technique

  1. Reservation with a TTL (hold then confirm). Do not sell directly. First hold the item for the buyer for a short, fixed time (commonly 5 to 15 minutes) by writing a reservation record with an expiry. While the hold exists, no one else can take it. If the buyer pays in time, the hold becomes a sale; if not, it expires automatically and the item returns to the pool. The TTL is what stops abandoned carts from locking stock forever.
  2. Optimistic concurrency (compare-and-set). Read the row with a version number, compute the change, and write it back only if the version is unchanged: UPDATE stock SET qty = qty - 1, version = version + 1 WHERE id = 7 AND version = 12 AND qty > 0. If zero rows are updated, someone else won, so retry or tell the user. This needs no lock and works well when conflicts are uncommon, but under heavy contention most attempts fail and retry, wasting work.
  3. Pessimistic locking. Take a lock (SELECT ... FOR UPDATE, or a distributed lock with a fencing token from Chapter 30) so that only one actor at a time works on the item. Correct and simple, but holding locks across slow steps such as payment hurts throughput, so keep the locked section tiny.
  4. Queue the writes. Route all requests for one item through a single ordered queue or a single-threaded partition owner, so contention becomes a line. Throughput for that item is capped at the speed of one worker, but there are no conflicts and no retries. A common flash-sale shape is a fast atomic decrement in a cache for admission control, with accepted orders flowing into a queue for the slower database work.
  5. Shard the inventory. Split 1,000 units into ten buckets of 100 on different keys and have each request pick a bucket; contention drops tenfold. The cost is that the last few units are stranded across buckets, so you need a fallback that checks other buckets when one empties.
  6. Shed excess demand early. In a flash sale, 1 million users may chase 1,000 units. Admit only a bounded number (a waiting room or token bucket, section 17), and answer "sold out" from a cached flag as soon as stock hits zero.

5.3 Tradeoffs and numbers

A single row under pessimistic locking in a relational database typically sustains hundreds to a few thousand updates per second, depending on lock hold time: if each transaction holds the row for 5 ms, the ceiling is 200 per second. A cache-based atomic decrement can handle tens of thousands per second per node, which is why flash-sale designs put admission control there. Optimistic retries are cheap when the conflict rate is below roughly 10 percent and become a retry storm when it is above 50 percent, at which point switch to queueing or sharding.

Watch out Expiry must be enforced at confirm time, not only by a background sweeper. If the sweeper is late and the confirm step does not check the hold's expiry and owner, an expired hold that was already re-sold to someone else can be confirmed too, which causes a double sale. Always confirm with a conditional write that names the hold id.

6. Large files: chunking, resumable upload, presigned URLs, dedup

6.1 Problem

Uploading a 4 GB video through your application servers as one HTTP request fails in many ways: the connection drops at 90 percent and the user restarts from zero, a proxy times out, your app servers spend bandwidth and memory relaying bytes, and the same file uploaded by a thousand users is stored a thousand times. Object storage internals are in Chapter 21, and the sync-client perspective is in Chapter C9.

6.2 Technique

  1. Chunking (multipart). Split the file into parts, commonly 5 to 16 MB each, and upload them independently and in parallel. Parallelism uses more of the available bandwidth, and a failed part is retried alone.
  2. Resumable upload. The server (or object store) tracks which parts it has received. After a disconnect the client asks "which parts do you have?" and sends only the missing ones. The upload has an id, and parts are idempotent: re-sending part 7 overwrites part 7.
  3. Presigned URLs. The app server does not relay bytes. It authenticates the user, then hands the client a time-limited, scoped URL (signed with the server's credentials) that lets the client write directly to the object store. Bytes flow client to storage; your servers only handle small metadata calls. Expiry is typically minutes.
  4. Content-hash deduplication. Compute a cryptographic hash (such as SHA-256) of each chunk or file. Before uploading, the client asks "do you already have hash H?" If yes, no bytes are sent and the new file simply references the existing blob. Storage keyed by hash is content-addressable, and a reference count tracks when a blob can be garbage collected. Dedup can be per user (safe) or global (bigger saving, but leaks whether someone else already has a file, a known side channel).
  5. CDN delivery. Serve downloads from the edge (Chapter 12) using range requests, so a video seek or a resumed download fetches only the bytes needed.
  6. Integrity and finalisation. Each part carries a checksum; a final "complete" call assembles the parts, and the file becomes visible only after that, so readers never see a half-uploaded object. Abandoned multipart uploads should be cleaned up by a lifecycle rule.

6.3 Tradeoffs and numbers

A 4 GB file in 8 MB parts is 512 parts. With 8 parallel streams at 25 MB/s each, the upload takes about 20 seconds on a fast link instead of a single stream's several minutes, and a dropped connection costs at most a few parts of rework. Object stores typically cap the number of parts in a multipart upload (on the order of 10,000), which sets a floor on chunk size for very large files. Smaller chunks give finer dedup and cheaper retries but more requests and metadata; larger chunks do the opposite. Sync products often use content-defined chunking (boundaries chosen by a rolling hash) so that inserting a byte early in a file does not change every later chunk.

7. Counting at scale

7.1 Problem

Counting looks trivial until the count is hot. Likes on a viral post, views on a video, stock remaining, ad impressions: a single counter row updated by tens of thousands of clients per second becomes a hot key (section 4) and a lock convoy. There is also a second flavour: counting distinct things (unique visitors), which cannot be done with a simple increment at all, and storing every id to deduplicate is too expensive.

7.2 Technique

  • Sharded counters. Keep N sub-counters (say 16 or 64) for one logical counter. Each increment goes to a random sub-counter, so writes spread across N keys and N nodes. A read sums the N values (or reads a periodically refreshed total). Write throughput scales by N; read cost rises by N, which is fine because the total is usually cached.
  • Write-behind aggregation. Accumulate increments in memory in each app server (or in a cache) and flush a combined delta to the database every second or so. One hundred increments become one write of "+100". The risk is losing the unflushed delta in a crash, which is acceptable for views and likes and not acceptable for money.
  • Stream aggregation. Emit each event to a log and let a stream processor roll them up into per-minute or per-hour windows, as in Chapter C20. Exactly-once semantics, late events and windowing are the hard parts, covered in Chapter 37.
  • Approximate counting. When "about 1.2 million" is as good as "1,203,447", use a probabilistic structure. A HyperLogLog estimates the number of distinct elements in roughly 12 KB with about 1 to 2 percent error, however many items are added, and two HLLs can be merged by taking the register-wise maximum, so partial counts from many servers combine. A count-min sketch estimates per-item frequencies in small fixed memory with a bounded overestimate. Both are explained in Chapter 57.
  • Exact where it matters. Display counts can be approximate and eventually consistent, while billing and inventory counts are exact, transactional and usually reconciled against the event log.

7.3 Tradeoffs and numbers

TechniqueAccuracyFailure riskCost
Single row, lockedExactContention, ceiling of hundreds per secondLow, simple
Sharded counterExact (sum of parts), slightly stale totalLowN times the keys

For unique visitors, storing 100 million 8-byte ids exactly takes about 800 MB per counter and per time window; a HyperLogLog needs 12 KB, roughly 65,000 times smaller. If you need a distinct count per page per day across millions of pages, only a sketch is affordable.

8. Real-time presence and fan-out

8.1 Problem

Chat apps, collaborative editors and games must show who is online, who is typing, and deliver events to every interested connected client within a fraction of a second. The difficulty is that "online" is not an event that anyone reliably announces: phones lose signal, laptops sleep, processes crash. You must infer absence. Connection transport options (WebSocket, SSE, long polling) are in Chapter 6.

8.2 Technique

  1. Heartbeats. A connected client sends a tiny ping every N seconds (commonly 15 to 30). Each heartbeat refreshes the user's last-seen time.
  2. TTL keys. Store presence as a key with an expiry slightly longer than two heartbeat intervals, for example presence:user42 with a 45-second TTL. Every heartbeat resets the TTL. If heartbeats stop, the key expires on its own and the user is "offline" with no cleanup job. This is the single most useful presence trick.
  3. Pub/sub for fan-out. Each user or room maps to a topic. When someone posts a message or changes status, the event is published to the topic, and every gateway server that holds a subscriber's connection receives it and writes to the socket. A registry (user to gateway) tells the system which server holds a user's connection.
  4. Limit the fan-out of presence itself. Do not broadcast every status flip to all friends of a user with 5,000 friends. Send presence updates only to users who currently have that contact visible, and batch or debounce flapping (a flaky connection toggling every few seconds).
  5. Offline delivery. Pub/sub is fire-and-forget. For users who are offline, persist messages (an inbox or log) and deliver on reconnect using a last-seen cursor; the push channels are in section 13.

8.3 Tradeoffs and numbers

With 10 million connected users heartbeating every 20 seconds, the presence store absorbs 500,000 writes per second, so presence is itself a sharded, write-heavy workload (section 2). A shorter heartbeat detects loss faster but costs more writes and battery; a longer one is cheap but shows ghosts. Typical choices accept 30 to 60 seconds of detection delay. Redis pub/sub is fast but at-most-once; Kafka-style logs give replay at higher latency. Use the first for ephemeral signals (typing) and the second for messages that must not be lost (Chapter 35).

9. Ledgers and money

9.1 Problem

For money, "approximately right" is a bug. Balances must never be silently overwritten, every movement must be explainable months later to an auditor, retries must never charge a customer twice, and the system's idea of the truth must be checkable against the bank's. Most of the techniques in the other sections (eventual consistency, lossy aggregation, last-writer-wins) are forbidden here. The full design is in Chapter C14, with the transaction machinery in Chapter 31.

9.2 Technique

  1. Double-entry bookkeeping. Every transfer is recorded as at least two entries that sum to zero: a debit on one account and a credit on another. If the customer pays 100, the customer account is debited 100 and the merchant account credited 100. The invariant "all entries in a transaction sum to zero" makes loss or creation of money detectable by arithmetic.
  2. Immutable, append-only entries. Never update or delete a ledger row. A mistake is fixed by appending a reversing entry, so history is complete. A balance is a derived value: the sum of entries (kept as a cached running balance for speed, but always recomputable).
  3. Atomic posting. All entries of one transaction are written in one database transaction, so you can never persist the debit without the credit. Within a single database this is a normal ACID transaction; across services it is the saga or two-phase machinery of Chapter 48.
  4. Idempotency keys. The client sends a unique key with each payment request. The server stores the key together with the result, and a retry with the same key returns the stored result instead of charging again. See section 15 and Chapter 32.
  5. Reconciliation. Periodically compare your ledger with the external truth (the payment processor's settlement file, the bank statement). Mismatches (a charge the processor has but you do not, or vice versa) are listed as exceptions for automatic repair or human review. Reconciliation is how you find the bugs that every other control missed.
  6. Money as integers. Store amounts in the smallest currency unit (cents) as integers, with the currency code, never as floating point.

9.3 Tradeoffs and numbers

Append-only ledgers grow forever, so plan partitioning by time and archival, and keep running balances so reads do not scan history. A hot merchant account (a marketplace operator receiving every payment) becomes a hot row; the standard answer is to post to many sub-accounts and aggregate (the sharded-counter idea, with exactness preserved). Strong consistency costs throughput: a single relational primary can typically post thousands of balanced transactions per second, which covers most businesses, and beyond that you shard by account and avoid cross-shard transfers where you can.

Key idea Money systems combine three independent defences: structure (double-entry and immutability make errors visible), safe retries (idempotency keys make duplicates harmless) and external comparison (reconciliation catches what the first two missed). Mention all three in an interview.

10. Search and autocomplete

10.1 Problem

Relational indexes answer "find rows where column equals value" well, but not "find documents containing these words, ranked by relevance, tolerant of typos", and not "suggest completions as the user types" at 100 ms budgets. These are separate patterns that share an index-first mindset. Engine internals are in Chapter 22; the full suggestion design is in Chapter C11.

10.2 Technique

  • Inverted index for full text. For every term, store the list of documents containing it. A query intersects or unions those lists and scores results (TF-IDF or BM25). The search cluster is a secondary, derived store: the database remains the source of truth, and changes flow in through a pipeline (section 16), so an index can always be rebuilt.
  • Scatter-gather across shards. The index is split into shards. A query goes to every shard, each returns its local top K, and a coordinator merges them. Latency is set by the slowest shard, so tail latency and replica selection matter.
  • Autocomplete with a prefix structure. Use a trie, or an edge-n-gram index, or a sorted set keyed by prefix, storing the top few completions per prefix precomputed from query logs by popularity. Serving a keystroke becomes a single lookup; the expensive ranking happens offline.
  • Keep suggestions cheap and fresh enough. Rebuild suggestion data hourly or daily; layer in recent trending queries separately. Cache popular prefixes (the first one or two characters are the hottest keys; see section 4) and debounce on the client so a fast typist does not send a request per key.
  • Relevance is a product. Boost by recency, popularity and personal signals, and measure with click-through, not intuition.

10.3 Tradeoffs and numbers

Search indexing is typically near real time rather than immediate: a refresh interval of about one second is a common default in Lucene-based systems, so a freshly written document is not instantly searchable. Autocomplete targets a response in well under 100 ms end to end, because users perceive typing lag beyond that. A precomputed top-5 list per prefix over, say, 100 million distinct queries is dominated by short prefixes, so memory is manageable if you cap prefix length (often 10 to 20 characters) and prune rare queries.

11. Geo and proximity

11.1 Problem

"Find the restaurants within 2 km", "match the nearest drivers", "which delivery zone is this address in". Plain B-tree indexes on latitude and longitude do poorly, because a rectangle query on two independent dimensions cannot use one sorted order effectively. The index structures are in Chapter 58, and a full design is in Chapter C13.

11.2 Technique

  1. Map two dimensions onto one. Geohash, S2 and H3 divide the earth into cells with hierarchical ids, so nearby points share an id prefix (or a neighbouring cell id). A proximity query becomes "fetch the user's cell and its neighbours", which an ordinary index or key-value store serves well.
  2. Search neighbours, then refine. Cell lookup is a coarse filter. Compute exact distances for the returned candidates and sort, because a point near a cell edge may have its true nearest neighbour in the adjacent cell.
  3. Choose cell size to match density. A fixed grid is wasteful: Manhattan packs thousands of venues into a cell that holds none in a desert. Adaptive structures (quadtrees) split dense cells further.
  4. Separate static from moving data. Restaurants change rarely and can sit in a read-optimised, cached index. Drivers move constantly and are write-heavy (section 2); keep their latest positions in memory keyed by cell, expire stale entries by TTL (the presence trick of section 8), and update only when movement crosses a threshold.

11.3 Tradeoffs and numbers

A 6-character geohash covers a cell of roughly 1.2 km by 0.6 km, and 7 characters about 150 m by 150 m, which is a convenient range for "nearby" features. A million drivers reporting every 5 seconds generate 200,000 updates per second, so location ingestion is a sharded in-memory workload, not a relational one. Do not forget the edge cases: queries near the poles or the antimeridian (longitude wrap-around) and very dense or very sparse regions.

12. Scheduling and delayed work

12.1 Problem

Many features are "do this later": send a reminder in 24 hours, retry a failed payment in 10 minutes, expire a reservation, run a report every night, publish a post at 9:00. A naive implementation keeps a timer in memory on one server, which is lost when the server restarts and does not scale. Workflow and scheduler internals are in Chapter 38, and the full design is in Chapter C18.

12.2 Technique

  1. Persist the intent. Store each job with its due time in durable storage (a table with an index on run_at, or a sorted set keyed by due time). The schedule survives restarts.
  2. Poll for due work. A set of workers repeatedly selects jobs whose run_at is in the past and claims them atomically (a conditional update that sets claimed_by and a lease expiry, or SELECT ... FOR UPDATE SKIP LOCKED), so two workers never run the same job.
  3. Lease and heartbeat. A claimed job carries a lease. If the worker dies, the lease expires and another worker reclaims the job. This gives at-least-once execution, so jobs must be idempotent (section 15).
  4. Partition the timeline. To scale polling, shard jobs by id and give each worker a shard, or bucket by time slice (jobs due this minute) so workers scan only a small range.
  5. Delay queues and timing wheels. Some brokers support per-message delay, and in-memory timing wheels give cheap scheduling for very many short timers. Both still need a durable backing record for anything important.
  6. Retry with backoff and jitter. Failed jobs reschedule with exponentially growing delays plus random jitter, up to a cap, then land in a dead-letter queue for inspection.

12.3 Tradeoffs and numbers

A polling interval of one second gives about one second of schedule accuracy at a negligible query cost of one indexed range scan per second per shard. Sub-second precision requires in-memory timers fed from the durable store. For cron-style fleets, remember that "every night at midnight" from a million tenants creates a thundering herd; spread starts with a random offset. Time-zone and daylight-saving rules are a classic source of bugs, so store due times in UTC and keep the user's zone separately.

13. Notifications and fan-out to channels

13.1 Problem

One business event ("your order shipped") must reach a user through push, email, SMS or in-app, respecting preferences, quiet hours and rate limits, and without sending duplicates. External providers are slow, rate-limited and unreliable. The full design is in Chapter C8.

13.2 Technique

  • Decouple producers from delivery. Services publish a small event to a queue; a notification service owns templates, preferences and channel choice. Producers never call a provider directly.
  • One queue per channel. Separating push, email and SMS queues isolates failures and rates: a slow email provider cannot delay push. Each channel has workers sized to the provider's limits.
  • Preference and policy checks. Before sending, consult opt-outs, quiet hours, frequency caps and deduplication keys (a user should not get the same alert twice in a minute).
  • Retries, fallbacks and dead letters. Retry transient provider errors with backoff, fall back to a second provider where it makes sense, and park permanent failures (invalid token) for cleanup, such as deleting dead device tokens.
  • Idempotent sends. Give each notification an id so a retried worker does not double-send. Providers rarely guarantee exactly-once, so aim for at-least-once with deduplication.
  • Priority lanes. One-time passwords and security alerts must never sit behind a marketing blast of 5 million emails. Use separate queues or priority classes.

13.3 Tradeoffs and numbers

A campaign to 10 million users with a provider limit of 5,000 messages per second takes 2,000 seconds, about 33 minutes. That is acceptable for marketing and unacceptable for login codes, which is the reason for priority lanes. Bulk fan-out is also where batching (section 2) pays: many providers accept hundreds of recipients per API call.

14. Async processing pipelines and job queues

14.1 Problem

Some work is too slow, too bursty or too failure-prone to do inside a user request: image resizing, report generation, sending email, calling flaky partners. Doing it inline makes the user wait and ties your availability to the slowest dependency. The mechanics are in Chapter 34 and the delivery patterns are in Chapter 35.

14.2 Technique

  1. Accept, enqueue, acknowledge. The request validates input, writes a job (or event) durably, and returns immediately with a job id (HTTP 202). The user polls or is notified when it finishes.
  2. Competing consumers. A pool of workers pulls from the queue; add workers to increase throughput. With a partitioned log, parallelism is bounded by the partition count, and ordering is guaranteed only within a partition, so pick the partition key to keep related events together (for example the account id).
  3. At-least-once with idempotent handlers. A worker acknowledges only after finishing, so a crash means redelivery. Duplicates are therefore normal and handlers must tolerate them (section 15).
  4. Backpressure and bounded queues. Watch queue depth and age of the oldest message. If producers outpace consumers, scale consumers, shed low-priority work, or slow producers; an unbounded queue merely converts overload into latency and eventual memory exhaustion.
  5. Poison messages and dead-letter queues. A message that always fails must not block the line; after a few attempts move it aside with the error for investigation.
  6. The outbox pattern. To publish an event reliably when you also write to a database, write the event into an outbox table in the same transaction, and let a relay publish it. This avoids the dual-write failure where the row commits but the event is lost.

14.3 Tradeoffs and numbers

Queues add latency (milliseconds to seconds), operational surface and the need for idempotency, in exchange for decoupling and smoothing. A single Kafka partition typically handles on the order of tens of MB per second, so a 500 MB/s ingest needs dozens of partitions. Size consumers by Little's law: arrival rate times processing time gives concurrent workers needed, so 2,000 jobs per second at 0.5 s each needs about 1,000 concurrent workers.

15. Idempotent APIs

15.1 Problem

Networks fail ambiguously. A client sends "charge 50" and times out: did the server process it? The only safe client behaviour is to retry, and the only safe server behaviour is to make retries harmless. Queues redeliver, load balancers retry, mobile clients double-tap. Without idempotency every retry risks a duplicate charge, order or email. The id and key strategies are in Chapter 32.

15.2 Technique

  • Idempotency key. The client generates a unique key (a UUID) per logical operation and sends it in a header. The server stores the key, request fingerprint and eventual response in one table with a unique constraint on the key.
  • Insert first, then do the work. Atomically record "key K in progress". A concurrent duplicate hits the unique constraint and either waits or receives a conflict; a later duplicate receives the stored response. Saving the response in the same transaction as the side effect closes the crash window between doing the work and recording it.
  • Natural idempotency. Prefer operations that are idempotent by nature: "set status to shipped" instead of "advance status", PUT instead of POST, upserts keyed by a business id, conditional writes on version numbers.
  • Deduplication on consumers. Record processed message ids (with a retention window) or make the state transition itself conditional, so reprocessing changes nothing.
  • Expiry and scope. Keys are scoped per user or tenant and retained for a window (commonly 24 hours or longer) so unbounded storage is avoided. Reusing a key with a different payload should be rejected as an error.

15.3 Tradeoffs

The cost is one extra durable write and lookup per request, plus storage for keys. In return duplicate charges disappear as a class of bug. Remember that idempotency handles duplicates, not races between different operations on the same resource, which still need the contention tools of section 5.

16. Data sync between stores and change data capture

16.1 Problem

Real systems use several stores: a relational primary, a cache, a search index, an analytics warehouse. Keeping them in agreement by having the application write to all of them (dual writes) fails the moment one write succeeds and the other does not, leaving permanent silent drift. The eventing patterns are in Chapter 36.

16.2 Technique

  1. One source of truth, many derived views. Choose a single authoritative store; everything else is derived and rebuildable.
  2. Change data capture (CDC). Read the database's own transaction log (binlog or WAL) and publish each committed change as an event. Because the log is the database's ordered record of what happened, nothing committed is missed and nothing uncommitted is published.
  3. Stream to consumers. Consumers update the search index, invalidate or refresh caches, and load the warehouse. Each consumer tracks its own offset, so slow consumers do not block fast ones.
  4. Handle ordering and idempotency. Partition by primary key so changes to one row stay in order, and make consumers idempotent (upsert by key and version) because delivery is at-least-once.
  5. Backfill and reconcile. New consumers bootstrap from a snapshot and then switch to the log. Periodic reconciliation (compare counts or checksums between source and derived store) detects drift, in the same spirit as the ledger reconciliation of section 9.

16.3 Tradeoffs and numbers

CDC lag is typically sub-second to a few seconds, so derived stores are eventually consistent; the user may save a record and not find it in search for a moment. Mask this in the UI (show the just-created item from the write response) rather than by making reads wait. Schema changes in the source must be coordinated with consumers, so version events and add fields compatibly.

17. Rate limiting and quotas

17.1 Problem

Without limits, one buggy client, one abusive scraper or one big customer can consume capacity meant for everyone, and a retry storm can turn a brief slowdown into an outage. Limits protect the system, enforce fairness and implement commercial quotas. The algorithms and distributed designs are in Chapter 16 and Chapter C3.

17.2 Technique

  • Pick the algorithm for the behaviour you want. A token bucket allows short bursts up to the bucket size while enforcing an average rate. A sliding window gives a smoother, stricter count. A fixed window is simplest but permits double bursts at window boundaries.
  • Pick the key. Per user, per API key, per IP, per tenant, per endpoint; layered limits are common (a global cap, a per-tenant cap, a per-user cap).
  • Centralise counters, tolerate approximation. Keep counters in a fast shared store with atomic increments and expiry. To avoid a network hop per request, allow each node to hold a local allowance and sync periodically, accepting slight overshoot.
  • Respond helpfully. Return HTTP 429 with a Retry-After header so well-behaved clients back off; clients should use exponential backoff with jitter.
  • Distinguish limiting from admission control. Rate limits are per caller and policy-based; load shedding (section 23) is a global response to saturation.

17.3 Tradeoffs and numbers

A limiter in the request path must itself be very fast and fail open or closed deliberately: if the counter store is down, do you allow all traffic (risk overload) or block all (risk outage)? Most public APIs fail open for general traffic and fail closed for expensive or abuse-prone endpoints. A limit of 100 requests per minute per key with 1 million active keys needs about 1 million small counters, trivial for memory but hot for popular keys (section 4).

18. Multi-tenancy and cells

18.1 Problem

A B2B platform serves thousands of customers on shared infrastructure. Sharing is cheap, but one tenant's heavy query, bad deploy or data-volume spike must not hurt others, and a regional or software failure should not take every customer down. The models are in Chapter 42.

18.2 Technique

  1. Choose an isolation tier. Shared everything with a tenant id column is cheapest; schema or database per tenant isolates data better; dedicated stacks for the largest customers isolate fully at the highest cost. Many platforms use a mix, putting small tenants pooled and large ones siloed.
  2. Cells. Partition the whole stack (compute, storage, queues) into independent, identical cells, each serving a subset of tenants. A router maps tenant to cell. A failure or bad release is contained to one cell, limiting the blast radius to a fraction such as 1 in 20 of tenants. Cells also give a unit of capacity planning and a natural way to roll out changes gradually.
  3. Per-tenant limits and quotas. Apply the rate limits of section 17 and resource quotas per tenant, so a noisy neighbour is throttled instead of starving others.
  4. Tenant-aware sharding. Shard by tenant id so tenant data is co-located; handle the "whale" tenant by splitting it across shards or giving it its own cell (the hot-partition problem of section 4).
  5. Tenant context everywhere. Propagate the tenant id through every call, log line, queue message and cache key, and enforce it at the data layer. Cross-tenant leaks are the most damaging bug class in this domain.

18.3 Tradeoffs and numbers

Cells cost duplication (each cell has a minimum footprint) and complicate cross-tenant features and tenant migration between cells, but they convert "global outage" into "5 percent of customers affected", a trade that large platforms nearly always accept. A typical cell is sized so that losing one is survivable and the cell is still small enough to test fully.

19. ML serving and RAG awareness

19.1 Problem

Modern products include model-backed features: ranking, recommendations, fraud scoring, and LLM-based assistants. You rarely have to design the model in a system design interview, but you do have to show that you can serve it: feed it fresh features, meet a latency budget, control cost, version it safely and bound its failure modes. The storage choices (vector stores, feature stores, key-value stores) are touched on in Chapter 23.

19.2 Technique

  • Online versus batch inference. Batch inference computes predictions ahead of time (nightly recommendations) and stores them in a key-value store for instant lookup: cheap and simple, but stale and wasted for users who never visit. Online inference computes on request: fresh and personalised, but it needs low-latency serving, autoscaling and a fallback. Many systems precompute a candidate set in batch and rank it online.
  • Feature store. Features (user's purchases in the last 7 days) are computed once and served consistently. An offline store (warehouse) supports training, and an online store (low-latency key-value) supports serving. The key risk is training-serving skew, where features are computed differently in training and in production; using one definition for both prevents it.
  • Model versioning and safe rollout. Treat models like code: version them, deploy by shadow traffic, then canary, then ramp, and keep the previous version ready for instant rollback. Log inputs and predictions for monitoring drift.
  • Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG). To let an LLM answer from private data: split documents into chunks, compute an embedding (a numeric vector capturing meaning) for each, and store them in a vector index that supports approximate nearest-neighbour search. At query time embed the question, retrieve the top-k similar chunks, and place them in the prompt so the model answers grounded in that text. Chunk size, hybrid keyword-plus-vector retrieval and re-ranking drive quality.
  • Caching LLM responses. Exact-match caching of prompt to response is safe and cheap for repeated identical requests. Semantic caching (reuse an answer for a sufficiently similar question) saves more but risks returning a wrong answer, so use a strict similarity threshold and avoid it for personalised or sensitive content. Caching of the stable prompt prefix, where the provider supports it, also cuts cost and latency.
  • Latency, cost and guardrails. LLM calls take seconds, so stream tokens to the user and enforce timeouts. Cost scales with tokens, so cap context size, route easy requests to smaller models and set per-tenant budgets (section 17). Guardrails include input and output filtering, permission checks on retrieved documents (retrieve only what this user may see), prompt-injection defences, and a deterministic fallback when the model is slow or unavailable.

19.3 Tradeoffs and numbers

Typical budgets: a feature-store lookup in single-digit milliseconds, an embedding call tens of milliseconds, a vector search in tens of milliseconds, and an LLM generation taking one to many seconds, which dominates the end-to-end time. A 1,536-dimension float32 embedding is about 6 KB, so 10 million chunks need around 60 GB of raw vectors before index overhead, which is why quantisation and sharding the vector index matter. The principal risks are cost blow-ups, stale or leaking retrieval data, and confident wrong answers; mention evaluation sets and human-in-the-loop checks for high-stakes uses.

20. Video and media pipelines

20.1 Problem

An uploaded video is one huge, unplayable-on-most-devices file. Viewers have different screens and bandwidths, and playback must start fast and adapt as the network changes. The full design is in Chapter C10.

20.2 Technique

  1. Upload with the large-file pattern of section 6, directly to object storage.
  2. Transcode in a parallel pipeline. Split the video into short segments, and fan out transcoding jobs to workers producing several resolutions and bitrates (a "ladder"), then stitch the outputs. This is the async job pipeline of section 14, with a workflow orchestrator tracking which steps are complete and retrying failed segments.
  3. Package for adaptive streaming. Output short segments (a few seconds each) plus a manifest listing the renditions (HLS or DASH). The player measures its bandwidth and switches renditions between segments.
  4. Deliver via CDN. Segments are immutable, perfectly cacheable files; popular ones are served from the edge (Chapter 12) and long-tail ones from origin storage.
  5. Post-process. Thumbnails, moderation checks, captions and metadata extraction run as further pipeline stages, each independent and retryable.

20.3 Tradeoffs and numbers

Transcoding is CPU heavy and spiky, so run it on autoscaled or spot capacity with retries, and prioritise (a creator's new upload before a back-catalogue re-encode). One source video becomes perhaps 5 to 8 renditions, so storage is several times the original. Choosing segment length is a trade: shorter segments adapt faster and start sooner, longer ones reduce request overhead and improve compression.

21. Leaderboards

21.1 Problem

Games, contests and engagement features need "top 100 players", "my rank", and "the players just above and below me", updated continuously. Computing rank with ORDER BY score over millions of rows on every request does not scale. Sorted-set internals are in Chapter 59.

21.2 Technique

  • Sorted set. A structure (for example a Redis sorted set, built on a skip list) that keeps members ordered by score. Updating a score, fetching a rank and reading a range are each logarithmic in the set size. Top N and "neighbours of me" are range reads.
  • Shard for scale. One sorted set holds tens of millions of members on one node; beyond that, shard by score range (so global order is preserved and the top shard is small) or shard by member and merge top lists, accepting approximate ranks for far-down positions.
  • Time-bounded boards. Use one set per day, week or season, and expire old ones, which also bounds memory.
  • Persist and rebuild. Treat the in-memory set as a derived view of durable score events so it can be rebuilt after a crash.
  • Approximate ranks for the long tail. A player at rank 4,812,339 does not need exactness; bucketed score histograms give a close estimate cheaply.

21.3 Numbers

A sorted set operation costs roughly microseconds to tens of microseconds, so a single node can serve well over 50,000 rank queries per second. A 10-million-member board at about 100 bytes per entry occupies around 1 GB. Contention shows up only if one score is updated by thousands of writers at once, which is the counting problem of section 7 again.

22. Long-running workflows and sagas

22.1 Problem

Placing an order touches inventory, payment, shipping and notification services, each with its own database. A single ACID transaction cannot span them, and holding locks across network calls would be fragile anyway. If payment succeeds and shipping fails, you must undo the payment. Saga mechanics are in Chapter 48, the transaction background in Chapter 31, and workflow engines in Chapter 38.

22.2 Technique

  1. Saga. Break the process into local transactions, each with a compensating action that semantically undoes it (refund for charge, restock for reserve). If step 4 fails, run the compensations for steps 3, 2, 1 in reverse order.
  2. Orchestration versus choreography. An orchestrator holds the flow and tells services what to do (easy to see and debug, one component to build). In choreography each service reacts to events from others (loosely coupled, but the overall flow is implicit and harder to trace).
  3. Persist workflow state. Every step transition is recorded durably, so a crashed orchestrator resumes where it stopped. Durable-execution engines do exactly this.
  4. Every step idempotent and retryable (section 15), since the orchestrator will retry on timeouts.
  5. Timeouts and human steps. Long waits (manual approval, a payment that confirms in two days) are modelled as states with timers (section 12), not as blocked threads.
  6. Accept intermediate visibility. Between steps, other users can see partial states (money held, order pending). Design the user-facing states to be honest about this, for example "pending" instead of "confirmed".

22.3 Tradeoffs

Sagas give availability and autonomy at the price of eventual consistency and the effort of writing compensations, some of which cannot be perfect (you cannot unsend an email). The alternative, two-phase commit, gives atomicity but blocks on coordinator failure and is rarely offered across independent services.

23. Graceful degradation under load

23.1 Problem

Every system eventually receives more load than it can handle: a launch, a viral link, a dependency outage. Without a plan, latency climbs, queues grow, timeouts trigger retries which add more load, and the system collapses entirely (a metastable failure) rather than slowing down. Patterns for this are in Chapter 40.

23.2 Technique

  1. Timeouts everywhere, shorter than the caller's own timeout, so work that can no longer succeed is abandoned.
  2. Retries with budgets. Retry only idempotent calls, with backoff and jitter, and cap retries as a fraction of traffic so retries cannot multiply load.
  3. Circuit breakers. After repeated failures, stop calling a sick dependency for a cool-down period and fail fast, giving it room to recover.
  4. Bulkheads. Give each dependency or customer class its own thread pool or connection pool, so one slow dependency cannot exhaust shared resources.
  5. Load shedding. When saturated, reject excess requests early and cheaply (HTTP 503) rather than accepting everything and failing all of them slowly. Shed lowest-priority traffic first: bots, background syncs, then non-critical features.
  6. Degrade features, not the core. Serve cached or default content when recommendations are down, disable expensive features such as live counts, show "sold out" from a cached flag, and keep the critical path (login, checkout) alive.
  7. Queue-based leveling and admission control. A waiting room that admits users at the rate the backend can serve is better than letting everyone in to fail together.
Load levelResponse
NormalFull features, normal timeouts
Elevated (CPU above about 70 percent)Turn off expensive optional features, extend cache TTLs

Interview tip End your design with a short "what happens at 10x load or when the database is down" paragraph. Stating which features you would drop first, and what the user sees, signals production experience more than any component choice.

25. Worked example: combining patterns for a flash-sale checkout

Real designs rarely use one pattern. Consider a flash sale: 2,000 units of a console at a deep discount, go-live at noon, and 1.5 million users hitting the page within 30 seconds. We want no overselling, no duplicate charges, a responsive site, and a fair experience. Walk through the symptoms in order and attach a pattern to each.

25.1 Step 1: size the load

1.5 million users, each loading the product page and pressing Buy perhaps 3 times in the first minute, is about 4.5 million requests in 60 seconds, or 75,000 per second on average and perhaps 150,000 per second at the spike. Only 2,000 units exist, so at most 2,000 purchases can succeed: a 0.13 percent success rate. The system is overwhelmingly a read and reject workload with a tiny write core. That insight drives every choice.

25.2 Step 2: pattern by pattern

25.3 Step 3: check the failure paths

  • Cache node dies mid-sale: the stock counter is the scarce truth. Persist reservations durably (or replicate the counter) and rebuild buckets from them, because losing the counter risks overselling.
  • Payment provider is slow: the hold TTL keeps stock tied up, so size the TTL above the payment timeout, and make the charge idempotent so a late success is not charged twice.
  • Worker crashes after charging: the saga state and the idempotent charge let a replacement worker continue without a second charge.
  • Reconciliation: after the sale, compare units sold, reservations confirmed, ledger entries and the provider's settlement. Any difference is investigated before stock is restocked.
Key idea The skill being tested is layering: shed and absorb the load that does not need to touch the scarce resource (edge, admission, cache), then protect the scarce resource with one atomic, well-understood mechanism, then do the slow work asynchronously with idempotent, compensatable steps, and finally verify with reconciliation.

26. Interview Q&A

How do you decide between fan-out on write and fan-out on read for a feed?

I start from the ratio of reads to posts and from the distribution of follower counts. If users read their feed far more often than they post and most accounts have modest follower counts, fan-out on write is attractive, because a read becomes one lookup into a precomputed list and the write cost is bounded. The weakness is accounts with millions of followers, where one post would trigger millions of inserts, so I treat those accounts as pull-based and merge their recent posts into the feed at read time. That hybrid keeps both costs bounded. I would also skip fan-out to inactive users and store only post ids in the lists so edits and deletes remain cheap.

A single product page is overloading one cache node. What do you do?

First I confirm it is a hot key by sampling keys and checking per-node load. The cheapest fix is a short-TTL in-process cache on each app server, which cuts requests reaching the cache node from the whole request rate to roughly the number of app servers per TTL. Then I add request coalescing so simultaneous misses trigger one fetch. If it is still too hot, I split the key into several copies spread over nodes, accepting that writes must update all copies. I would mention the staleness tradeoff and say that values that must be exact, like balances, should not be served this way.

How do you prevent overselling the last item in a flash sale?

I make the decision at one atomic point rather than trying to coordinate many readers. A conditional decrement, either in a cache with atomic operations or in the database with a guard like qty > 0, ensures only as many reservations succeed as there are units. The success creates a time-limited hold, which is confirmed on payment and released if it expires, so abandoned carts do not strand stock. To handle the spike I put admission control and a cached sold-out flag in front, and I shard the counter if a single key becomes the bottleneck. Confirmation must re-check the hold with a conditional write to avoid confirming an expired one.

How would you design uploads of multi-gigabyte files?

I would never route the bytes through application servers. The client asks my API to start an upload, and receives an upload id and presigned URLs, then sends fixed-size chunks in parallel directly to object storage. The server records which chunks arrived, so after a failure the client asks what is missing and sends only those. A final complete call assembles the object and makes it visible. For efficiency I would hash chunks and skip any the store already has, and I would deliver downloads through a CDN using range requests. A lifecycle rule cleans up abandoned partial uploads.

How do you count likes on a viral post, and how do you count unique viewers?

For likes, a single row would be a hot spot, so I use sharded counters: increments go to a random sub-counter, a reader sums them, and the total is cached for a second or two. I add write-behind batching so each app server flushes combined deltas, which reduces writes dramatically at the price of losing a few seconds of increments on a crash, acceptable for likes. For unique viewers, exact deduplication needs memory proportional to the number of viewers, so I would use a HyperLogLog, which gives about one to two percent error in roughly twelve kilobytes and merges across servers. I would keep exact counts only where money or inventory is involved.

How does a presence system detect that a user has gone offline?

It cannot rely on the user announcing a disconnect, because crashes and lost signal send no message. Instead each client sends a heartbeat every 15 to 30 seconds, which refreshes a key with an expiry a bit longer than two intervals. If heartbeats stop, the key expires and the user is considered offline without any cleanup job. Status changes are published over pub/sub to the gateways that hold interested connections. To limit load I only send presence to contacts currently viewing that user, and I debounce flapping connections.

What makes a payment ledger trustworthy?

Three things work together. First, structure: double-entry records where each transaction's entries sum to zero and are immutable, with corrections made by appending reversing entries, so errors are visible and history is complete. Second, safe retries: idempotency keys stored with the result so a retry never charges twice, and atomic posting of all entries in one transaction. Third, external verification: reconciliation against the processor and bank records, which catches discrepancies the first two did not prevent. Amounts are integers in minor units, and balances are derived from entries rather than overwritten.

Why is dual writing to a database and a search index dangerous, and what replaces it?

Because the two writes are not atomic: one can succeed and the other fail, leaving the stores permanently inconsistent with no record of the divergence. Even without failures, concurrent writers can apply updates in different orders to the two stores. I would make the database the source of truth and use change data capture from its transaction log, or an outbox table written in the same transaction, to publish changes to the index. Consumers are idempotent and partitioned by key to preserve order, and a periodic reconciliation compares source and index to catch drift.

How do you keep a notification system from delaying critical messages during a big campaign?

I separate traffic by priority and by channel. Security and transactional messages such as one-time codes use their own queue and worker pool, so a ten-million-recipient marketing blast cannot sit in front of them. Each channel has its own queue, because providers have different rate limits and failure modes. Workers respect provider limits, retry with backoff, and send permanent failures to a dead-letter queue. Notifications carry ids so retries do not duplicate sends, and a preference and frequency-cap check runs before delivery.

What does graceful degradation look like when a database is overloaded?

First I want the system to slow down predictably instead of collapsing. Timeouts and circuit breakers stop requests from piling up behind the sick dependency, retry budgets prevent a retry storm, and load shedding rejects the lowest priority traffic early and cheaply. At the feature level I serve cached or default data, turn off expensive optional features, and protect the critical path such as login and checkout. For a surge I would use a waiting room to admit users at the rate the backend can handle. I would define in advance which features go first, so it is a runbook rather than an improvisation.

Where do ML and LLM features change a system design?

They add a slow, expensive, probabilistic dependency, so the usual patterns apply with extra care. I would precompute what I can in batch and rank online, serve features from a low-latency online store sharing definitions with training to avoid skew, and version models with shadow and canary rollout. For an LLM assistant over private data I would use retrieval: chunk and embed documents offline, search a vector index at request time filtered by the caller's permissions, and place the results in the prompt. I would cache responses carefully, stream output, cap tokens per tenant, and have a non-model fallback for timeouts and a guardrail layer on inputs and outputs.

27. Recap

  • Shape reads with caches, replicas and read models, and shape writes with batching, logs, LSM storage and sharding; measure the ratio first.
  • Fan-out on write makes reads cheap, fan-out on read makes writes cheap, and a hybrid handles the celebrity tail.
  • Hot keys are fixed by reducing requests (local cache, coalescing) and spreading what remains (key splitting).
  • Contention is resolved at one atomic point: reservation with TTL, compare-and-set, a queue, or sharded stock, with early shedding of surplus demand.
  • Large files use chunked, resumable uploads straight to object storage via presigned URLs, with content-hash dedup and CDN delivery.
  • Counters are sharded, batched or approximated (HyperLogLog), except where money demands exactness.
  • Presence is inferred from heartbeats and TTL keys, with pub/sub for fan-out.
  • Money needs double-entry immutable ledgers, idempotency keys and reconciliation.
  • Search, geo, scheduling, notifications and pipelines each reduce to a derived index or durable queue plus idempotent consumers.
  • CDC replaces dual writes; rate limits, quotas and cells isolate tenants; sagas coordinate multi-service work.
  • ML and RAG add latency, cost and guardrail concerns; cache, version, stream and provide fallbacks.
  • Degrade gracefully: timeouts, breakers, shedding, and dropping optional features first.
  • Start from the symptom, name the pattern, state the tradeoff, and link the mechanism to its owning chapter.
