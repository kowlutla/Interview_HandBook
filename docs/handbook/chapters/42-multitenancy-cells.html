Chapter 42 · Module 6 · Reliability and multi-tenancy

Multi-Tenancy and Cell-Based Architecture

Most B2B software serves many customers from the same running system. This chapter explains how to share infrastructure between customers without letting them harm or see each other, how to route, meter, migrate and delete them, and how cell-based architecture shrinks the damage any single failure can do.

1. What a tenant is, and why SaaS economics force sharing

A tenant is a customer of your service that owns an isolated slice of data and configuration, and whose users all belong to that slice. In a project-management product, one company is one tenant, and its hundreds of employees are users inside it. In a consumer product, the tenant may be a single account. The word matters because almost every design decision in this chapter follows from one rule: data and behaviour that belong to one tenant must never leak to, or be degraded by, another tenant.

Why not give every customer their own copy of the whole system? Because of economics. Imagine each customer needs a minimal deployment: two application servers, a database primary and replica, a load balancer, monitoring. Say that costs about $1,500 per month. If your average customer pays $200 per month, every customer loses you money, and you also have thousands of deployments to patch, monitor and upgrade. Sharing infrastructure lets you pack many small customers onto the same machines, where average utilization is far higher. Most tenants are idle most of the time, so the peak of the sum is much smaller than the sum of the peaks. This is called statistical multiplexing, and it is the reason SaaS margins work.

The price of sharing is risk. Shared machines mean one tenant's bug, traffic burst or attacker can touch another tenant's experience or data. The whole discipline of multi-tenancy is a menu of ways to buy back isolation, each costing some of the efficiency you gained.

Key idea Multi-tenancy is a dial, not a switch. At one end every tenant has dedicated everything (maximum isolation, maximum cost). At the other end every tenant shares everything (minimum cost, maximum blast radius). Good designs choose a different setting for each layer (compute, database, network, keys) and often a different setting for different classes of customer.

1.1 Dimensions of a tenant

Tenants differ along axes that drive design: size (a 5-person team versus a 200,000-seat enterprise), activity pattern (steady versus bursty batch imports at midnight), compliance needs (healthcare, government, data residency in one country), contract tier (free, standard, premium with an SLA), and customisation (custom fields, integrations). Real tenant populations are heavily skewed: typically a handful of very large customers produce a large share of the load while thousands of small ones produce little each. Section 10 treats this skew directly.

2. Isolation models: silo, pool, bridge

Three vocabulary words come from the cloud architecture literature and are worth using exactly in an interview.

  • Silo (dedicated): each tenant gets its own instance of a resource. Strong isolation, simple reasoning about security and performance, high per-tenant cost, and operational work that grows with the number of tenants.
  • Pool (shared): all tenants share the same resource, and tenant identity is just a value (such as tenant_id) carried in each request and row. Cheapest and easiest to operate as one fleet, but isolation is enforced by code and policy, so a bug can become a data leak, and one tenant can crowd out others.
  • Bridge (hybrid): some layers are pooled and others siloed, or most tenants are pooled while a few are siloed. For example, a shared application tier on top of a database per tenant, or a shared pool for small customers and dedicated stacks for enterprise customers who pay for them.

2.2 The three database patterns in detail

The database is where the hardest isolation decisions live, so it deserves its own comparison.

Shared schema with a tenant_id column

Every table has a tenant_id column, and it is part of the primary key and of every index that queries use (so that lookups for one tenant read a contiguous, small slice). This is how most large consumer-scale SaaS products run, because it scales to millions of tenants with a fixed number of tables. The downside is that isolation lives entirely in your queries: forget the WHERE tenant_id = ? once and you have a cross-tenant leak. Per-tenant backup and restore is hard (you must extract rows), and a noisy tenant's table scan competes for the same buffer cache as everyone else. Row-level security (section 4) is the main defence.

Schema per tenant

One physical database, but each tenant has its own namespace of tables. A request sets the search path to the tenant's schema, so queries need no tenant filter at all. Restoring one tenant is a schema-level dump. The limits are practical: databases keep metadata for every table, and with 20,000 tenants times 100 tables you have two million tables, which slows the catalog, backups, and above all schema migrations, which must be run once per schema (section 18). This is a good fit for hundreds to low thousands of tenants.

Database per tenant

Each tenant has a separate database, possibly on a separate server. Isolation is strong: a runaway query hurts only that database, you can place it in a chosen region, encrypt it with its own key, restore it from backup independently and even give the tenant a different version during a staged upgrade. The costs: connection pools multiply (each application instance needs a pool per database or a proxy), small tenants waste capacity, and the operations load rises with every tenant. It is the classic choice for the enterprise tier of a bridge model.

Interview tip Do not answer "which isolation model" with a single word. Say: "I would pool by default for cost, silo the database for tenants that need it for compliance or size, and keep the routing layer so a tenant can move from the pool to a silo without changing the application." That shows you treat isolation as a per-tenant property that can change over time.

3. Tenant context: identify once, carry everywhere

In a pooled system the single most important correctness property is that every operation knows which tenant it is acting for and that this value cannot be forged or lost. This is called tenant context.

3.1 Where the tenant id comes from

The tenant id must be derived from something the caller cannot choose freely. Good sources are a claim inside a signed token (a JWT or session issued after login, see Chapter 55), the verified subdomain of a request (acme.example.com) mapped through a directory, or an API key that is looked up to find its tenant. A bad source is a request body field or an unsigned header: an attacker simply writes another customer's id. Rule: the tenant id comes from authentication, never from user-supplied input.

3.2 Propagating it

The edge (API gateway) authenticates the request, extracts the tenant id, and attaches it to an internal context object. From then on it travels with the work:

  • In process: stored in a request-scoped object or a context variable that the framework passes to every handler. In languages with thread-local storage, be careful with async hand-offs: when work moves to another thread or a callback, the context can be lost or, worse, leak from a previous request that used the same thread.
  • Between services: sent as a header on every internal call (and re-validated, or sent inside a signed internal token, because internal services should not blindly trust a plain header from any caller).
  • Through queues: placed in the message envelope (a header or field), so the consumer restores the context before it processes the message. A consumer that handles a message with no tenant id should reject it, not guess.
  • Into logs and traces: added as a field on every log line and a tag on every span, which is what makes per-tenant debugging and metrics possible (section 19).

4. Enforcing isolation and preventing cross-tenant leaks

Carrying the tenant id is necessary but not sufficient. You must make it hard to write code that ignores it. Defences should be layered, because any single one will eventually be bypassed by a bug.

4.1 Layer 1: query scoping in the data access layer

Do not let feature developers write raw queries that must remember a filter. Put a repository or ORM layer in the middle that takes the tenant from the request context and adds tenant_id = :t to every query automatically (most ORMs support global filters or query interceptors). Inserts get the tenant id stamped from context, not from the payload. Lint or code-review rules forbid bypassing this layer, and any bypass (an admin or reporting job) is an explicit, audited, named exception.

4.2 Layer 2: row-level security in the database

Row-level security (RLS) moves the filter into the database engine itself. In PostgreSQL, for example, you define a policy on a table such as "a row is visible only if its tenant_id equals the session setting app.current_tenant". The application sets that session value when it borrows a connection, and from then on the database silently hides other tenants' rows from SELECT, UPDATE and DELETE, and rejects inserts whose tenant id does not match.

ALTER TABLE orders ENABLE ROW LEVEL SECURITY;
CREATE POLICY tenant_isolation ON orders
  USING (tenant_id = current_setting('app.current_tenant')::bigint);
-- per request, after taking a pooled connection:
SET app.current_tenant = '4217';

RLS is a safety net: even a query with a forgotten filter returns only the current tenant's rows. Pitfalls to mention: the application must connect as a role that is subject to the policy (table owners and superusers usually bypass it), the session setting must be reset when a pooled connection is returned (otherwise the next request inherits the previous tenant, a classic leak), and policies add small planning overhead. With a transaction-level connection pooler, set the value with a transaction-local setting so it cannot outlive the transaction.

4.3 Layer 3: tenant-aware caches, keys and queues

Shared infrastructure outside the database is a common leak path.

  • Caches: the cache key must include the tenant, for example t:4217:user:99. A key such as user:99 collides across tenants if ids are only unique per tenant. Generic caching, as in Chapter 13, becomes a leak source when the tenant is forgotten. Also remember invalidation: flushing "all of tenant X" must be possible, so a key prefix or a per-tenant version number (t:4217:v7:..., bump the version to invalidate) is useful.
  • Search indexes and blob storage: either index per tenant or add a mandatory tenant filter applied by the search layer; use per-tenant prefixes and scoped, short-lived credentials for object storage so a storage URL for one tenant cannot be altered to read another's.
  • Queues and streams: carry the tenant in the envelope, and consider per-tenant partitions or queues for fairness (section 5). Never process a message by trusting only the body.
  • Background jobs and scheduled tasks: these run with no user request, so no token supplies the tenant. Make the job explicitly iterate tenants and set the context for each one.
  • In-memory state: static or singleton objects holding per-request data are a well-known cause of cross-tenant leakage under concurrency.

4.4 Layer 4: cryptographic separation

Encrypt each tenant's data with a key unique to that tenant (envelope encryption: a per-tenant data key protected by a master key in a key management service, see Chapter 55). Even if a bug returns the wrong rows, the application would need the right tenant's key to decrypt them. It also gives a strong deletion tool (crypto-shredding, section 8.2) and lets high-assurance customers hold their own key.

4.5 Testing for leaks

Isolation bugs are silent: nothing crashes, someone just sees the wrong data. So you must test for them deliberately.

  • Two-tenant integration tests: every API test creates tenants A and B, writes data as A, then attempts to read, list, update and delete it as B, and asserts "not found" or "forbidden". Guess-the-id tests (B requests A's object id directly) catch insecure direct object references.
  • Schema-driven checks: a CI test scans the schema and fails if any table lacks a tenant_id column or an RLS policy, unless it is on an allow-list of global tables.
  • Canary tenants in production: synthetic tenants with distinctive data run continuously; an alert fires if their data ever shows up in another tenant's responses, logs or search results.
  • Fuzzing and concurrency tests: hammer the service with interleaved requests from many tenants to expose context that leaks across threads or pooled connections.
  • Penetration tests and code review checklists that specifically target tenant boundaries.
Watch out The most common real leak is not a clever attack; it is context that survives longer than the request: a pooled database connection still holding the last tenant's session setting, a thread-local never cleared, or a cache key without a tenant. Always clear context in a finally-style block and test that the next request starts clean.

5. The noisy neighbor problem and its controls

When tenants share a resource, one tenant can consume so much of it that others slow down or fail. This is the noisy neighbor problem. The offender is often not malicious: a customer starts a bulk import, a script loops on a retry bug, a marketing campaign sends ten times the usual traffic, or one tenant's report runs a table scan. The shared resource can be CPU, memory, database connections, disk IOPS, a cache, a queue, or an external API quota.

A concrete picture: a pooled API fleet handles 10,000 requests per second across 5,000 tenants with a database that tops out at 800 concurrent connections. Tenant X triggers an expensive export that holds 600 connections for a minute. Every other tenant now fights for 200 connections, queues build up, and latency rises for people who did nothing wrong. Fixing this requires controls at each layer.

5.1 Per-tenant rate limits

Limit how fast each tenant can call you, usually with a token bucket keyed by tenant id (and often a second key per API endpoint or per user). Chapter 16 covers the algorithms and the distributed implementation; here the multi-tenant points matter. Limits should reflect the contract tier (free 10 requests per second, standard 100, premium 1,000). Distinguish a burst allowance (a bucket larger than the steady rate so normal spikes pass) from the sustained rate. Return a clear 429 with a Retry-After header so well-behaved clients back off. Rate limiting protects the entry point but is blind to cost: one request that triggers a heavy report is not equal to one that reads a row, so also use weighted limits, where each operation consumes tokens proportional to its estimated cost.

5.2 Quotas

A rate limit says how fast; a quota says how much in total over a period: 1 million API calls per month, 500 GB storage, 50 concurrent exports, 200 seats. Quotas bound cost and also form the basis of billing tiers. Enforce them at the point where the resource is consumed, and handle the soft boundary gracefully (warn at 80 percent, block or charge overage at 100 percent).

5.3 Fair scheduling and weighted queues

Background work is where one tenant most easily starves others. If all tenants share one FIFO queue and tenant X enqueues 2 million jobs, the 50 jobs from tenant Y wait behind them for hours. Fixes:

  • Per-tenant queues with round-robin dispatch: workers take one job from each non-empty tenant queue in turn, so every tenant makes progress regardless of backlog size.
  • Weighted fair queueing: as above, but a premium tenant with weight 4 is served four jobs per turn against a standard tenant's one. Idle tenants do not accumulate credit.
  • Virtual queues over a shared stream: in a log such as Kafka, hash tenants onto partitions and run a fair dequeuer in the consumer, or give heavy tenants their own topic or partition set.
  • Concurrency caps per tenant: no tenant may occupy more than K workers at once, even if the system is otherwise idle, or a cap that relaxes when there is spare capacity.

5.4 Resource limits

Limits inside the runtime stop one tenant's request from using more than a defined slice. Examples: a maximum query execution time and result size, a per-tenant database connection cap (via a proxy), per-tenant memory limits for in-process caches, container CPU and memory limits when tenants run in separate pods, file upload size limits, and limits on the number of objects (a tenant cannot create ten million custom fields). The platform-level counterpart of this is the bulkhead pattern of Chapter 40: separate thread pools, connection pools, or instance groups per tenant class so exhaustion stays contained.

5.5 Partitioning heavy tenants

When controls are not enough, move the heavy tenant to its own resources: a dedicated worker pool, a dedicated database or shard, or a dedicated cell (section 12). This is the bridge model applied after the fact, and it works only if routing (section 6) lets you move a tenant without changing client behaviour.

Key idea The control you need depends on where the contention is. Rate limits protect the front door, quotas bound totals, fair queues protect background work, resource caps protect the inner pools, and placement removes the problem entirely. A mature system uses all five, because each fails in a different way.

6. Tenant routing: from tenant to shard or cell

Once data is spread over several databases, shards, or full-stack deployments, every request must be sent to the one that holds that tenant. The standard solution is a tenant directory (also called a routing table or catalog): a small, highly available lookup that maps tenant_id to a location such as cell-17 or shard-db-03.eu-west. Compare this with the hash-based sharding in Chapter 25: a hash gives no way to move one tenant to a different place without rehashing others, whereas a directory is an explicit assignment, so you can place whales alone, honour residency, and migrate one tenant at a time.

6.1 Design of the directory

  • Contents: tenant id, home cell or shard, status (active, migrating, suspended, deleting), residency region, tier, and an optional generation number that increases on every move.
  • Size and access pattern: even for millions of tenants the table is small, read far more than written, and cacheable. Routers keep an in-memory copy refreshed by short TTL or by change notifications.
  • Availability: the directory is on the critical path of every request, so it must be more available than anything it routes to. Replicate it widely, let routers serve from a stale cache if the directory is down, and keep it out of the cells it describes (a cell's failure must not take the directory with it). Directory availability designs follow Chapter 41.
  • Stale entries during moves: if a router has a stale mapping while a tenant migrates, the old cell must either proxy the request or answer with a redirect plus the new location. The generation number lets a cell reject requests that were routed under an old mapping.
  • Identifying the tenant before the lookup: from the hostname (acme.example.com or a custom domain), from a claim in the token, or from an API key prefix that embeds a routing hint. Doing this at the very edge keeps the rest of the path simple.

7. Onboarding, offboarding, configuration and feature flags

7.1 Onboarding (provisioning)

Creating a tenant is a small workflow that must be automated and idempotent, because manual onboarding is the first thing that stops a SaaS company from scaling. Typical steps: create the tenant record and id; choose a placement (cell, shard, region) using capacity and residency rules; allocate resources (a schema or database, a storage prefix, a key in the key management service); seed default configuration, roles and the first admin user; register routing in the directory; configure billing and metering; and send a verification event. Model it as a saga (a series of steps, each with a compensating undo) so a failure halfway leaves no half-created tenant, and make every step safe to retry. For pooled tenants this takes seconds; for siloed tenants that need a fresh database or stack it may take minutes, which affects the product design (show "setting up your workspace").

7.2 Offboarding

Offboarding is harder than onboarding because it is irreversible and has legal weight. A responsible sequence: suspend the tenant (block logins, keep data), allow a grace period for data export and reactivation, stop billing, then delete everything including backups on a defined schedule (section 8), and finally remove directory entries and release capacity. Record an audit trail of each step, because customers and regulators may ask for proof.

7.3 Per-tenant configuration and feature flags

Tenants differ in settings (branding, password policy, enabled integrations, data retention) and in which features they see. Store configuration as data, not as code or per-tenant deployments. Keep a layered resolution: platform defaults, then tier defaults, then tenant overrides, then optionally user overrides, where each lower layer overrides the one above. Load tenant config through a cache with change notification so edits take effect quickly without hitting the store on each request.

Feature flags are the same idea for behaviour: a flag service answers "is feature F on for tenant T?" evaluated from rules such as an allow-list, a percentage of tenants, or a tier. Uses include progressive rollout (turn a feature on for internal tenants, then 1 percent, then everyone), entitlement (premium-only features), and instant kill switches for a misbehaving feature. Watch for flag debt: flags that are never removed multiply the number of code paths you must test. Also, per-tenant customisation that changes schema (custom fields) needs careful design, usually generic key-value or JSON columns with per-tenant metadata, rather than altering tables per tenant.

8. Tenant data export, deletion and residency

Privacy law and enterprise contracts turn data lifecycle into a product requirement. The legal background is in Chapter 56; this section covers how multi-tenant structure helps or hinders it.

8.1 Export

Customers need to take their data with them (and law may require it). In a silo this is a database dump. In a shared schema it means extracting rows by tenant_id across many tables, which is a heavy, scan-like job. Run exports as asynchronous, throttled background jobs (so they do not become the noisy neighbor), write them to a tenant-scoped bucket, encrypt, and give a time-limited download link. Design a stable, documented export format.

8.2 Deletion

Deleting a tenant means removing it from primary stores, replicas, search indexes, caches, analytics warehouses, logs, and backups. Practical techniques:

  • Silo makes it trivial: drop the database or schema.
  • Pool needs a deletion job: delete by tenant_id in batches to avoid long locks, with partitioning by tenant (or by tenant hash) as an optimisation so entire partitions can be dropped.
  • Crypto-shredding: if all of a tenant's data is encrypted with a per-tenant key, destroying the key renders every copy unreadable, including copies in backups that you cannot practically edit. Teams usually still delete the data too, but the key destruction is the proof for backups.
  • Soft delete first: mark deleted, retain for a legally acceptable window (for example 30 days) to cover accidental or malicious deletion requests, then hard delete.
  • Verification: a post-deletion scan that searches every store for the tenant id and reports zero residue.

8.3 Residency

Data residency means a tenant's data must stay within a geography (for example the EU). Multi-tenant architecture supports it by letting the tenant directory include a region, and by placing the tenant in a cell that lives only in that region. All of the tenant's derived data (logs, backups, analytics, search indexes, support tooling, cache) must also stay in region, which is where teams often fail: the primary database is in Frankfurt, but a shared logging pipeline ships request bodies to a US region. Treat residency as a property of the cell and make cross-region flows an explicit exception. Cells make this cleaner than one global pool, because "the EU cells" is a well-defined set.

9. Metering, billing and cost attribution

If tenants share infrastructure you must still know what each one used, both to bill them and to know whether you make money on them.

9.1 Metering

Metering is recording billable usage events: API calls, stored gigabytes, seats, compute minutes, messages sent. Emit a usage event at the point of consumption with the tenant id, a metric name, a quantity, a timestamp, and a unique event id. Send events through a durable stream into an aggregator that sums them per tenant per period. Two properties matter. First, idempotency: events may be delivered more than once, so aggregation deduplicates by event id, otherwise customers are overcharged. Second, auditability: keep the raw events long enough to resolve disputes and rebuild invoices. Some meters are gauges rather than counters (storage used at a point in time), which you sample, for example hourly, and average.

9.2 Billing

The billing system consumes aggregated usage together with plan definitions (flat fee, per seat, tiered, usage-based with included allowance and overage) and produces invoices. Keep pricing logic out of the request path; metering must be low-overhead and must never block a request. Enforcement of quotas (section 5.2) uses a fast approximate counter, while billing uses the exact, replayable event log.

9.3 Cost attribution

Billing tells you revenue per tenant; cost attribution tells you cost per tenant. For siloed resources, cost is direct from cloud tags. For pooled resources you allocate shared cost using a driver: share of requests, CPU-seconds, storage bytes, or database time, measured per tenant through the same tenant-tagged metrics and logs. Compute margin per tenant (revenue minus attributed cost) and per tier. This often reveals that a few heavy tenants on a flat fee are unprofitable, which drives decisions to add usage-based pricing, raise quotas to an upsell tier, or move them to a dedicated stack with a matching price.

10. Large versus small tenants: the whale problem

Real tenant sizes follow a heavy-tailed distribution. A common shape: 90 percent of tenants are small and together use perhaps 20 percent of resources; the top 1 percent, the whales, can account for 30 to 50 percent. This skew breaks designs that assume uniform tenants.

  • Hot partitions: if tenants are placed by hash onto shards, one whale makes its shard hot (the same hot-key issue discussed in Chapter 25, except the "key" is a whole customer, and you cannot split it by hashing more finely if your data model keeps a tenant together).
  • Outsized limits: a whale may legitimately need a higher rate limit than the platform default, and exceed a single node's capacity.
  • Blast radius concentration: the whale's outage or migration affects a large revenue share.

Strategies, usually combined:

  1. Detect early. Track per-tenant share of load; flag tenants above a threshold (for example 5 percent of a shard) for placement review.
  2. Dedicated placement. Give whales their own shard, database, or cell (bridge model). Charge for it.
  3. Split the whale's data internally. For a tenant too large for one database, shard within the tenant, by sub-entity such as workspace, region, or business unit, so the directory maps a (tenant, sub-key) pair to a location.
  4. Pack small tenants densely. Many tiny tenants per shard, with caps, because their individual risk is low.
  5. Distinct limits by tier. Negotiated, contract-based limits rather than one global default.

11. Moving tenants between shards and cells

Because tenants grow, shift behaviour, and have changing compliance needs, you must be able to move a tenant from one location to another with little or no downtime. This is also how you rebalance capacity and evacuate a cell for maintenance. The directory (section 6) makes it possible; the procedure is a live migration.

The details in words: Phase 1 takes a consistent snapshot of the tenant's rows (or a backup restore) and loads it into the target; the tenant keeps working against the source. Phase 2 reads the source's change log (change data capture or replication stream) and replays everything written since the snapshot, until the lag is tiny. Phase 3 briefly blocks new writes for that tenant (or sets it read-only), lets the last changes drain, and compares checksums or row counts. Phase 4 updates the directory so that routers send traffic to the target, and increments the generation number. Phase 5 keeps the old copy read-only for a short safety period in case rollback is needed, then deletes it. Migrations of whales are scheduled in low-traffic windows and may take hours in the copy phase but still only a few seconds of freeze. Non-database state (caches, queues in flight, search indexes, files in object storage, scheduled jobs) must also move or be rebuilt; forgetting these is the common cause of a "successful" migration that loses jobs.

12. Cell-based architecture

Everything so far manages risk between tenants inside shared components. Cell-based architecture attacks the other half of the problem: the shared components themselves are a single point of failure. A bug in a deployment, a poisoned configuration, a runaway query, or an exhausted resource in a pooled system takes down every tenant at once. A cell is a complete, independent copy of the whole service stack (load balancers, application servers, caches, queues, databases) that serves a fixed subset of tenants. Cells share nothing with each other at runtime, and a tenant lives in exactly one cell. The idea comes from ship design (watertight compartments) and is used by large cloud providers: when one compartment floods, the ship stays afloat.

12.1 Cells versus other forms of sharding

This is closely related to sharding (Chapter 25), but there is a difference in what is partitioned. Sharding usually partitions a data store, while the stateless application tier stays shared and calls into all shards. A cell partitions the entire stack, including the application, caches, queues, and even deployment pipelines. That is why it limits blast radius for software faults and not only for data hot spots. It is also different from a region or availability zone: those are physical failure domains and a cell sits inside them (a cell often spans the three AZs of one region for high availability, see Chapter 41), whereas the cell is a logical failure domain that also contains software and configuration faults.

12.2 Properties of a good cell design

  • No shared runtime dependencies between cells. If cell 1 calls cell 2 or both depend on the same database, the independence is fake. The only shared pieces are the router and the control plane, kept deliberately tiny and simple.
  • A tenant is in exactly one cell (its home), so all of its requests hit the same stack and no cross-cell transaction is needed for ordinary work.
  • Fixed maximum size (section 14): cells are built to a tested capacity, and you scale by adding cells, not by growing them without limit.
  • Identical but independent: same code and infrastructure definition, independently deployed and scaled.
  • Cross-tenant features need a separate path. Anything that crosses tenants (global search across customers, marketplace, a global admin console) must be built on a side channel such as an asynchronous event feed into a separate data platform, not by reaching into cells synchronously.

13. Blast radius reduction math

Blast radius is the fraction of customers affected by a single failure. In a single shared pool it is 100 percent for any fault in a shared component. With N equal cells and a tenant in exactly one cell, a fault confined to one cell affects 1/N of tenants.

Now the availability effect. Suppose bad deployments or software faults cause one full-outage incident per year in a shared pool, lasting 60 minutes. Every tenant loses 60 minutes, so yearly availability from this cause is 1 - 60/525,600 = 99.989 percent. Move to 20 cells and deploy to one cell at a time, with the health check halting rollout after the first cell fails. Now the same bad deployment is caught after hitting one cell. The expected tenant-minutes lost are 60 minutes times 5 percent of tenants, so the average tenant loses 3 minutes per such incident instead of 60, and 95 percent of tenants lose nothing. Average availability from this cause becomes 1 - 3/525,600 = 99.9994 percent. The headline figure matters less than the shape: most customers never notice most incidents, and your support, communication and recovery efforts concentrate on a small set.

Independence also helps probabilistically. If a given cell has an independent probability p of being down at some moment, say 0.1 percent, the chance that k particular cells are simultaneously down is pk, so the chance that two specific cells are down together is one in a million. This only holds when failures are truly independent, which is why shared dependencies (section 12.2) and simultaneous deployments to all cells destroy the benefit.

Watch out Cells reduce the size of failures, not their frequency; in fact you now operate N copies, so some cell will have a problem more often. The win is that the average impact is small, and recovery can be parallel and rehearsed. Budget operations accordingly: automation of cell creation, deployment, and monitoring is mandatory, not optional.

14. The cell router, and choosing cell size

14.1 The cell router

The router is the one component every request touches, so its design must be minimal. It extracts the tenant (hostname, token claim, or API key), looks up the cell in the cached directory, and forwards the request. It does no business logic, holds no per-request state, and is itself horizontally scaled and spread across zones. Because it is shared, it is the biggest remaining risk to blast radius, so it is kept deliberately dull: few features, rare deployments, extensive load testing, and conservative timeouts. Some architectures push the lookup to the DNS or CDN layer: each cell has its own endpoint (cell7.api.example.com), a client or edge function resolves the tenant to a cell once, and later traffic goes straight to the cell, leaving only the lookup service as a shared dependency. A router must also support: a fallback when the directory is unreachable (serve from a stale cache), per-cell health-based draining, and the generation checks needed during migration (section 11).

14.2 Choosing cell size

Cell size is a trade-off with forces pulling both ways.

Smaller cellsLarger cells
Smaller blast radiusLarger blast radius
Less capacity headroom per cell for spikes and whales (a single big tenant may not fit)More statistical multiplexing, so better utilization and cheaper

Practical guidance for choosing: set the maximum size from what you can test and recover (the largest load you have actually load-tested a cell with, with a safety margin, and a recovery time you can accept); ensure the largest expected tenant fits comfortably (rule of thumb: no tenant above 10 to 20 percent of a cell, otherwise dedicate a cell); run each cell at a target utilization of about 50 to 70 percent so there is room for failover within the cell and for absorbing a neighbour tenant spike; and keep the fixed overhead per cell low enough that having dozens of cells is affordable. Start with a few cells and add as you grow, rather than starting with hundreds. Also keep spare empty cells, so new tenants and evacuations always have a place to go.

15. Deployment per cell: progressive rollout

The same cell boundary that contains runtime faults contains bad releases, if you deploy cell by cell. A progressive rollout uses waves: deploy to a canary cell holding internal and low-risk tenants, bake (wait and watch metrics) for some period, then a small wave of cells, then larger waves, with automatic halt and rollback when an alarm fires.

Key practices: treat configuration and feature flag changes as deployments with the same waves (many large outages come from config, not code); order waves from low to high risk (internal tenants and free tier first, whales and regulated tenants last); define the alarms that gate a wave (error rate, latency percentiles, saturation, tenant-specific canary checks) before the rollout, not during it; keep rollback fast and rehearsed; and avoid deploying to two regions at the same time. Having different cells running different versions at the same time requires backward-compatible interfaces between whatever is shared (router, control plane, any cross-cell event formats), a point connecting to the schema migration discussion in section 18.

16. Shuffle sharding

Cells isolate groups of tenants from each other. Shuffle sharding pushes isolation further, to the point where an individual troublemaker can only hurt a vanishingly small number of others. It is a way of assigning each tenant to a random subset of shared workers instead of to a fixed group.

16.1 The idea with numbers

Take 8 workers (servers or queue consumers). With plain sharding, split them into 4 shards of 2 workers; each tenant lives on one shard. If a tenant sends a poison request that crashes the workers it touches, it takes down its shard (2 workers). All tenants on that shard (one quarter of everybody, 25 percent) are fully down.

With shuffle sharding, each tenant is assigned a random pair of the 8 workers. The number of distinct pairs is C(8,2) = 28. The poison tenant, with pair {3,6}, kills workers 3 and 6. Another tenant is fully down only if its pair is exactly {3,6}: 1 of 28 pairs, about 3.6 percent of tenants. A tenant with a pair that shares just one worker with {3,6} (there are 2 x 6 = 12 such pairs, 43 percent of tenants) still has one healthy worker, so it keeps working at reduced capacity if its clients retry onto the healthy worker. Tenants with no overlap (15 of 28 pairs) are unaffected.

16.2 Why larger numbers make it powerful

The number of distinct subsets is the binomial coefficient C(n, k). For n = 100 workers and k = 5 per tenant it is 75,287,520. The chance that a specific other tenant has the same five workers as the poison tenant is about 1 in 75 million; with 10,000 tenants the expected number of tenants sharing all five is 10,000 / 75,287,520 which is roughly 0.00013. In practice you deliberately assign subsets so that no two tenants share more than one or two workers, which turns the expected damage from "a quarter of customers" to "essentially nobody completely".

16.3 Requirements and applications

  • The client (or router) must know the tenant's subset and retry across it, otherwise partial overlap is still an outage. Requests that fail should be retried on another worker of the subset, which means the failing request itself must not be what kills each worker in turn (a true poison request that crashes every worker it touches will spread through the subset, which is why subsets are small and why you also need request-level protections like timeouts and circuit breakers from Chapter 40).
  • Used by DNS services (assigning each customer a subset of name servers) and in load balancers, queues, and API fleets. It also pairs with cells: shuffle across workers inside a cell.
  • Cost: you need enough workers for the combinations to be meaningful, and you give up the simplicity of "tenant X lives on shard 3" for a per-tenant assignment table.

17. Bulkheading at platform level, and the AWS-style cell model

The bulkhead pattern (Chapter 40) partitions resources inside one service so that exhaustion of one partition does not sink the rest. Multi-tenant platforms apply it at several levels at once: separate thread and connection pools per downstream dependency or per tenant class inside a process; separate worker pools for interactive and batch traffic; separate queues for free and paid tiers; separate clusters for noisy workload types such as analytics; separate accounts and networks per environment; and separate cells per group of tenants. The higher the level of the bulkhead, the larger the class of failure it contains, and the more it costs, so you pick the level that matches the failure you most want to survive.

17.1 The AWS-style cell approach

Large cloud providers describe their services in terms of a control plane and a data plane, and increasingly cellularise the data plane. The data plane is the part that serves customer traffic (for example, reading and writing objects, running queries). It is simple, heavily tested, and built so each cell works without calling the control plane on the request path. The control plane (creating resources, changing configuration, placement decisions) is more complex and changes more, so it is kept off the hot path: if it is down, existing resources keep working. Concretely:

  • The service is deployed as many cells, each a separate stack with a hard limit on size and a defined set of customer resources.
  • A very thin routing layer maps customer (or resource) to cell. Placement is decided and recorded by the control plane.
  • Deployments go cell by cell, in waves, starting with small or internal cells, across regions and zones in sequence, with long bake times.
  • Cells are sized to tested limits; the answer to growth is "add a cell", never "make the cell bigger than we have tested".
  • Customer-visible impact of a bad cell is bounded to that cell's share, and recovery means fixing or evacuating one cell.

The lesson for an interview is to name the principle, not the vendor: keep the data plane small and independent, keep the control plane out of the request path, bound every failure to a cell, and deploy slowly.

18. Multi-tenant schema migration

Changing a database schema is risky even with one tenant. With many tenants it gains new dimensions: how many databases or schemas must change, what happens if the migration works on some and fails on others, and how old and new code coexist.

  • Shared schema: one migration for everyone. It is simplest, but it is all-or-nothing and the table is huge. Use online, non-blocking techniques: add nullable columns, backfill in batches, create indexes concurrently, and never run a long lock on a shared table.
  • Schema or database per tenant: the migration runs N times. Run it through a migration orchestrator that records the version per tenant, applies migrations in parallel with a concurrency cap (so it does not overload the servers), retries failures, and can pause on error. Expect the fleet to be at mixed versions for a while, and sometimes for good if a few tenants are blocked.
  • Cells: migrate cell by cell along the same waves as code, so a bad migration hits a small subset. Keep tenant data inside the cell, so no cross-cell consistency is needed.

The core technique is expand and contract (also called parallel change). Step one, expand: add the new column or table in a backward-compatible way, with old code still working. Step two: deploy code that writes to both old and new and reads the new where present. Step three: backfill existing data. Step four: switch reads fully to the new structure. Step five, contract: remove the old column once no running code depends on it. Each step is safe to roll back because old and new code are both compatible with the schema at every moment. This is also what makes it safe to run different cells at different versions. Never rename or drop a column in a single deploy.

19. Observability per tenant

Aggregated metrics hide tenant-level pain: an overall error rate of 0.1 percent might be 100 percent for one important customer, or one tenant's burst might be responsible for the latency that everyone sees. Tag everything with the tenant (and cell):

  • Logs and traces carry tenant_id and cell_id on every record, so you can answer "show me everything that happened for tenant X between 10:00 and 10:15". Make sure logs themselves respect residency and do not contain sensitive tenant content.
  • Metrics: request rate, error rate, latency percentiles, and resource use per tenant. A key caution is cardinality: a metric labelled by tenant with 10,000 values multiplies the number of time series by 10,000 and can overwhelm a metrics store. Typical solutions: emit full per-tenant detail only for the top tenants or in a log-based analytics store, and keep per-cell and per-tier aggregates in the real-time metrics system. Use a separate tenant-level pipeline (event stream into a columnar store) for ad hoc analysis.
  • Tenant health scores and SLOs: compute availability and latency SLO attainment per tenant (or per tier), not only globally. This surfaces the unhappy minority before they complain, and it supports SLA credit calculations.
  • Top-N views: "top ten tenants by CPU, by database time, by error count" is the first screen you want during a noisy neighbor incident.
  • Synthetic probes per cell that act as a real tenant (the canary tenants from section 4.5) detect cell failures before customers do, and a router-level probe detects directory problems.
  • Alerting on cells: alarms such as "error rate of cell 7 is above threshold" are what gate rollouts and trigger evacuations.

21. Worked example: sizing cells for 10,000 tenants

Suppose you are designing a SaaS product with 10,000 tenants today and room to grow 2x in two years. Step one is to measure the size distribution in a common load unit. Let one unit be the peak load of a small tenant (say 2 requests per second at peak and 1 GB of data). Based on measurement the tenant mix is:

ClassTenantsUnits eachTotal units
Small9,00019,000
Medium900109,000
Large901009,000
Whale101,00010,000
Total10,00037,000

Notice that 0.1 percent of tenants (the 10 whales) account for 27 percent of the load, and the top 1 percent (whales and large) account for 51 percent. That skew drives the design.

Step two: cell capacity. Load testing shows one full cell sustains 2,000 units. Run it at 60 percent target utilization to leave room for spikes and for a failure of one node within the cell, giving a planning capacity of 1,200 units per cell.

Step three: whales. A 1,000-unit whale takes 83 percent of a cell, which breaks the "no tenant above 10 to 20 percent of a cell" rule of thumb; mixing it with others is unwise. So each whale gets a dedicated cell. Each such cell has 200 spare units, which can host about 200 small tenants as a low-risk filler, or stay empty for headroom. We place about 2,000 units of small and medium tenants into the whale cells in total. That is 10 whale cells, hosting 10,000 + 2,000 = 12,000 units.

Step four: the rest. Remaining load is 37,000 - 12,000 = 25,000 units. At 1,200 units per cell: 25,000 / 1,200 = 20.8, so 21 shared cells. Large tenants at 100 units are 8 percent of a cell, within the guideline, but we place no more than two large tenants per cell and spread them (the 90 large tenants over 21 cells is about 4 per cell, so check the average: 4 x 100 = 400 units of 1,200, which is acceptable but we still cap at 5 per cell).

Step five: spares and growth. Add 2 empty spare cells for evacuation and new tenants, and then plan for the 2x growth by adding cells as the fleet crosses 70 percent of planned capacity. Total now: 10 whale + 21 shared + 2 spare = 33 cells.

Step six: blast radius. The whale cells also host about 2,000 small tenants as filler (2,000 units). That leaves 10,000 - 10 - 2,000 = 7,990 tenants spread over the 21 shared cells, about 380 per cell, so a shared-cell failure affects roughly 3.8 percent of tenants. A whale-cell failure affects one whale plus its filler tenants; since a whale is a large revenue share, whale cells get extra engineering such as multi-AZ and a warm standby. Compare with one pool, where the blast radius is 100 percent.

Step seven: per-cell cost shape. If fixed overhead per cell (load balancers, minimum replicas, monitoring) is the equivalent of 100 units, 33 cells carry 3,300 units of overhead, about 9 percent on top of 37,000. That is the price of isolation, and it is the number to compare against the cost of one all-customer outage. If overhead looked too high, you would increase cell planning capacity, but only to what you have tested.

Step eight: routing and operations. The tenant directory has 10,000 rows (trivial); routers cache it for 60 seconds. Deployments go in four waves: the first is a shared cell hosting internal tenants and a few friendly customers, then 3 cells, then 8 cells, then the remaining cells, with whale cells last. Rebalancing is a monthly job that moves tenants when a cell exceeds 70 percent, using the live migration from section 11.

22. Interview Q&A

What is multi-tenancy, and why would you choose pooled over siloed?

Multi-tenancy means a single running system serves many customers, each logically isolated from the others. Pooling shares compute, storage and operations across tenants, which raises utilization (most tenants are idle most of the time), keeps the cost per small tenant low, and means you operate and upgrade one fleet rather than thousands of deployments. Siloing gives stronger isolation and simpler compliance but costs more and multiplies operational work. I would pool by default and silo selectively for tenants that pay for it or need it for regulation, using a routing layer so a tenant can move between the two.

Compare shared schema, schema per tenant and database per tenant.

Shared schema with a tenant_id column scales to millions of tenants and has a single migration, but isolation depends on every query being scoped, so I would add row-level security and tenant-aware access layers, and per-tenant restore is awkward. Schema per tenant gives each tenant its own tables inside a shared database, which makes restore and customisation easier but strains the catalog beyond thousands of tenants and requires running migrations N times. Database per tenant gives the strongest isolation, per-tenant restore, residency and encryption, at the cost of connection management and operations that grow with tenant count. A typical answer is a bridge: shared schema for the long tail, dedicated database for large or regulated tenants.

How do you make sure one tenant can never read another's data?

I layer defences, because any single one can be bypassed by a bug. The tenant id is derived from the authenticated credential, never from request input; the data access layer injects it into every query; the database enforces it with row-level security keyed to a session setting that is reset when connections are returned to the pool; caches, queues and storage keys include the tenant; and data is encrypted with per-tenant keys. Then I test it: two-tenant integration tests that attempt cross-access, CI checks that every table has a tenant column and policy, and canary tenants in production that alert if their data appears elsewhere.

What is the noisy neighbor problem and how do you control it?

It is when one tenant consumes a disproportionate share of a shared resource and degrades others. I control it at several layers. At the front door, per-tenant rate limits (see the rate limiting chapter) and quotas bound the request rate and totals, with weights so expensive operations cost more. For background work, per-tenant queues with round-robin or weighted fair dispatch and per-tenant concurrency caps prevent starvation. Inside, connection and memory caps stop one tenant exhausting pools. If a tenant is persistently heavy, I move it to dedicated workers, a shard or a cell. I also need per-tenant observability so I can quickly identify who is responsible.

How do you route requests when tenants live on different shards or cells?

A tenant directory maps each tenant id to its home cell or shard, plus status and region. A thin router identifies the tenant from the hostname, token or API key, looks it up in a locally cached copy of the directory, and forwards the request. The directory is small and read-heavy, so caching makes the cost negligible, but it is on the critical path, so it must be replicated, highly available, and tolerate stale cached reads when it is down. Unlike hashing, an explicit directory lets me place whales, honour residency, and migrate one tenant at a time by flipping its entry with a generation number to guard against stale routing.

What is a cell, and how does it reduce blast radius?

A cell is a complete, independent copy of the service stack that serves a fixed subset of tenants, with no runtime sharing between cells apart from a thin router and a control plane. A fault in a cell, such as a bad deploy, a poisoned input or resource exhaustion, is confined to its tenants. With N equal cells the blast radius is 1/N: 20 cells means 5 percent of tenants for a cell failure, versus 100 percent in one shared pool. Combined with wave-based deployment that halts when an alarm fires, a bad release typically lands in one or two cells before it is stopped.

How do you choose the size of a cell?

I set a maximum from what I can load test and recover: the largest load I have actually driven through a full cell, run at roughly 50 to 70 percent utilization for headroom. Smaller cells give a smaller blast radius and faster recovery but higher fixed overhead and more operations; larger cells give better utilization but a larger blast radius and untested scaling limits. The largest tenant should fit as a modest fraction of a cell, and tenants that do not should be given a dedicated cell. We scale by adding cells, and I keep spare empty cells for new tenants and evacuations.

Explain shuffle sharding with an example.

Instead of assigning each tenant to one fixed shard, assign each a random small subset of the shared workers. With 8 workers and subsets of 2 there are 28 possible pairs, so if one tenant's poison requests kill its two workers, only tenants with that exact pair, about 3.6 percent, are fully down, compared with 25 percent under 4 fixed shards of 2. Tenants sharing only one worker still have a healthy one, provided clients retry across their subset. With 100 workers and subsets of 5, there are about 75 million combinations, so full overlap is almost impossible. It requires smart client retries and a per-tenant assignment record.

How do you migrate a tenant between cells with minimal downtime?

I copy a consistent snapshot to the target while the tenant keeps running on the source, then stream the changes made since the snapshot until the lag is tiny. I freeze writes for that tenant for a few seconds, wait for the last changes to drain, verify with checksums, and flip the directory entry to the new cell with a bumped generation number so stale routes are rejected or forwarded. The old copy stays read-only for a rollback window. I also have to move non-database state like queued jobs, files and search indexes, and I throttle the copy so it does not become a noisy neighbor for the source cell.

How would you run a schema migration across thousands of tenants?

I use expand and contract so every intermediate state works with both old and new code: add the new structure compatibly, write to both, backfill, switch reads, then remove the old structure later. For a shared schema it is one online migration with batched backfills and concurrent index creation. For per-tenant schemas or databases, a migration orchestrator tracks the version of each tenant, runs migrations in parallel under a concurrency cap, retries and pauses on failure, and the application tolerates mixed versions. In a cell architecture I follow the same waves as code, so a faulty migration hits few tenants.

How do you bill and attribute cost in a shared system?

Every consumption point emits a usage event with tenant id, metric, quantity, timestamp and a unique event id into a durable stream. An aggregator deduplicates by event id and sums per tenant per period; raw events are kept for audits, and billing applies plan rules to the aggregates. Metering stays out of the request path. For internal cost attribution, siloed resources use cloud tags, while pooled resources allocate shared cost by a usage driver such as CPU seconds or database time per tenant. Comparing revenue to attributed cost per tenant exposes unprofitable whales and informs pricing and quotas.

How do you handle a whale tenant?

First detect it by tracking each tenant's share of load and flagging those above a threshold. Then isolate it: give it a dedicated database, shard or cell, with its own limits negotiated in the contract, so it cannot starve the others and others cannot starve it. If one database is too small even for the whale, partition within the tenant by a sub-key such as workspace or business unit, and have the directory map the pair to a location. Whale cells get extra care, such as warm standbys, because their failure is high-impact in revenue even when it is low in tenant count.

23. Recap

  • A tenant is an isolated customer slice. Sharing infrastructure between tenants is what makes SaaS economics work, and isolation is what you buy back with some of the savings.
  • Silo, pool and bridge are choices made per layer (compute, storage, database, network, keys). The default is pool, with silo for those who need and pay for it.
  • Carry tenant context from the verified credential through every hop, and enforce it in layers: scoped queries, row-level security, tenant-keyed caches and queues, per-tenant keys, and tests that actively try to cross the boundary.
  • Noisy neighbors are controlled with rate limits (Chapter 16), quotas, fair weighted queues, resource caps and, finally, placement of heavy tenants on dedicated resources.
  • A tenant directory routes tenants to shards or cells and enables live migration: snapshot, catch up, short freeze, flip, verify.
  • Plan the tenant lifecycle: automated onboarding, careful offboarding, data export and deletion (crypto-shredding), residency, config layers and feature flags, and idempotent metering for billing and cost.
  • Cells are independent full-stack copies; blast radius is about 1/N, and deployments proceed wave by wave with automatic halts. Size cells to what you can test and recover.
  • Shuffle sharding assigns each tenant a random subset of workers so that a poison tenant fully overlaps almost nobody (C(100,5) is about 75 million).
  • Schema changes use expand and contract; observability is per tenant and per cell, with attention to metric cardinality.
