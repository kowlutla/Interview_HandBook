Chapter 4 · Module 1 · Networking and Communication

DNS in Depth

DNS is the internet's phone book, and also one of its most important traffic-steering and failure-prone systems. This chapter explains how a name becomes an address, where answers are cached, what each record type means, how DNS is used for load balancing, failover and service discovery, and why a "5 minute TTL" never means exactly five minutes.

1. What DNS is and why it exists

Computers on the internet find each other with numeric addresses called IP addresses, such as 93.184.216.34 (IPv4) or 2606:2800:220:1:248:1893:25c8:1946 (IPv6). Humans remember names such as www.example.com. The Domain Name System (DNS) is the distributed database that translates names into the data needed to reach them, most often an IP address. Before a browser can open a connection (see Chapter 3 for TCP and TLS), it almost always needs a DNS answer first, so DNS sits at the very front of every request.

In the early internet a single file called HOSTS.TXT, kept by one organization, listed every name and address, and everyone downloaded it. That stopped working as the network grew: the file was always out of date, one party controlled everything, and the download load grew with every new host. DNS, designed in the 1980s, replaced it with three ideas that still define it.

  • A hierarchy of names. Names are read right to left, and each part is managed by a different owner. Nobody holds the whole database.
  • Delegation. The owner of one level hands responsibility for the level below to someone else. The owner of com delegates example.com to the company that registered it, and that company decides everything beneath it.
  • Caching. Answers are reused for a time chosen by their owner, so the system answers billions of questions a day without the owners being asked each time.

For a system designer DNS matters in four ways. It adds latency to the first request to any new name. It is a dependency: if name lookup fails, your perfectly healthy servers are unreachable. It is a control point: changing what a name points to is the cheapest way to move traffic between servers, data centers or regions. And it is a cache hierarchy you do not control, which is exactly why changes spread slowly and unevenly.

Key idea DNS is a globally distributed, hierarchical, heavily cached key-value store. Every property you will be asked about, both the good (it scales enormously and is cheap) and the bad (changes are slow and cannot be pulled back), follows from those three words: hierarchical, distributed and cached.

2. The namespace hierarchy: root, TLD, authoritative

A full name such as api.eu.example.com. is read from right to left. The final dot (usually hidden) stands for the root. Then comes the top-level domain (TLD) com, then the second-level domain example, then subdomains eu and api. Each dot-separated piece is called a label. The tree below shows how responsibility is split.

2.1 The three kinds of servers

Root servers. They know only one thing: for each TLD, which servers run it. There are 13 root server names (a.root-servers.net through m.root-servers.net), but each is really hundreds of machines worldwide sharing one IP address through anycast (section 8). Resolvers ship with a built-in list of root addresses, called the root hints, as the starting point for every lookup.

TLD servers. A TLD is the last label: com, org, net, country codes like uk and de, and newer ones like dev. The organization that runs a TLD is called a registry. Its servers do not know your IP address. They know the NS records (name server records) for your domain, meaning the names of the servers you registered as authoritative. When you buy a domain from a registrar (the shop that sells domains), the registrar passes your chosen name servers to the registry, and that is what the TLD servers then hand out.

Authoritative servers. These hold the actual records for a domain, and their answer is the final truth for that domain. They are run by you, or more commonly by a managed DNS provider such as Amazon Route 53, Cloudflare, Google Cloud DNS or NS1. A single domain usually lists several authoritative servers (often four) in different networks, so that one failure does not take the domain offline.

2.2 Zones and delegation

A zone is a portion of the namespace managed as one unit, stored as a file of records. example.com is a zone. If its owner wants a team to manage eu.example.com separately, they add NS records inside the example.com zone pointing at the team's servers. That act is delegation, and eu.example.com becomes its own zone. Delegation is what lets the system scale: every owner manages only their own level, and the global tree needs no central operator beyond the root.

A subtle consequence: the NS records at the parent (the TLD) and the NS records in your own zone should agree. If they disagree ("lame delegation" or inconsistent NS sets), some resolvers follow one set and some the other, producing the maddening intermittent failures people blame on "DNS being flaky".

2.3 Who is who: stub, recursive, authoritative

Three roles recur throughout the rest of the chapter, so fix them now.

  • The stub resolver is the small piece of code in your operating system or browser. It does not walk the tree. It asks one configured server a question and expects a finished answer.
  • The recursive resolver (also called the caching resolver) is the server that does the walking on the client's behalf, and caches what it learns. Your ISP, your company, or a public service (Cloudflare 1.1.1.1, Google 8.8.8.8, Quad9) runs these.
  • The authoritative server answers only for the zones it owns and never walks the tree for anyone else. It is not a cache.

3. The full resolution path, with caches at every layer

Suppose a user types www.example.com and nothing is cached anywhere. The following happens, in order, before the first byte of HTTP can be sent.

  1. The browser checks its own small DNS cache. Miss.
  2. The browser asks the operating system's stub resolver, which checks the OS cache and the local hosts file. Miss.
  3. The stub sends one question, "what is the A record of www.example.com?", to the configured recursive resolver, normally learned from the network (DHCP) or set manually. The question is marked "recursion desired", meaning "please do all the work".
  4. The resolver checks its cache. Miss. It starts at the top and asks a root server. The root replies with a referral: "I do not know, but here are the NS records for com", plus the IP addresses of those servers (called glue, so the resolver does not need DNS to find DNS).
  5. The resolver asks a com TLD server. It replies with a referral: "here are the NS records for example.com", again with glue if needed.
  6. The resolver asks one of the authoritative servers for example.com. This one returns the real answer with its TTL, for example 93.184.216.34, marked authoritative.
  7. The resolver stores the answer (and the NS referrals) in its cache and returns the answer to the stub. The OS and browser cache it too, and the browser connects.

3.1 Recursive versus iterative queries

The two terms describe who does the work. In a recursive query, the client asks a server to return a final answer or an error and never a referral. The stub-to-resolver exchange is recursive. In an iterative query, the server returns the best it knows, possibly a referral, and the asker follows up itself. The resolver-to-root, TLD and authoritative exchanges are iterative. This split keeps root and TLD servers simple (they never chase anything) and puts the caching and the work at the resolver, where many users share the result.

3.2 Caching at each layer

Caching is why DNS works at planetary scale, and also why it is hard to reason about. A given name may sit in several caches at once, each with its own remaining lifetime.

  • Browser cache. Browsers keep their own short-lived cache, often capped at a minute or so regardless of TTL, and may also hold open connections to an address. Restarting the browser or clearing its host cache flushes it.
  • OS cache. Many systems cache lookups in a system service (for example the Windows DNS Client service or systemd-resolved on Linux); others cache little or nothing. Applications may also cache on their own, and this is where long-running programs bite you, as section 10 explains.
  • Recursive resolver cache. This one does the heavy lifting. If a million customers of an ISP resolve www.example.com within a TTL window, the authoritative server is asked once, not a million times. The resolver caches the final answer and also the intermediate referrals, so the next lookup for a different name under example.com skips the root and TLD steps and jumps straight to the authoritative servers. That is why roots and TLDs, despite serving the entire internet, receive a manageable load.
  • Authoritative side. No caching here, but managed DNS providers push records to many anycast points of presence, so lookups are served near the resolver.

Because of these layers, a typical lookup is fast. A cache hit in the OS costs microseconds, a resolver cache hit costs the round trip to the resolver (a few milliseconds), and a full cold walk may cost 50 to 200 ms or more, because it makes three or more dependent round trips. This is why the very first visit to a brand-new name is slower and why DNS lookup time shows up in page-load waterfalls. Browsers mitigate it with prefetching (dns-prefetch hints) and by reducing the number of distinct hostnames a page uses.

Interview tip When asked what happens when you type a URL, spend 20 seconds on DNS: stub resolver, recursive resolver, root, TLD, authoritative, then say that each layer caches by TTL. It shows you know that answers are cached in many places, which is the hook for every follow-up on failover and TTLs.

4. Record types

4.1 CNAME in detail, and its limits

A CNAME says "this name is just another name for that name". When a resolver finds a CNAME, it restarts the lookup on the target and returns the whole chain. CNAMEs are valuable because the owner of the target can change the final IP addresses without you touching your zone. This is exactly how CDNs and cloud load balancers work: you point www.example.com at d123.cloudfront.net, and the provider changes the addresses behind that name as it wishes.

The rules, which are common interview material:

  • A name with a CNAME may have no other records of any type. The CNAME stands for the entire name.
  • The zone apex (also called the naked or bare domain, example.com itself, with no www) must have SOA and NS records. Since a CNAME forbids any other record, a CNAME at the apex is illegal. It would also break your MX and TXT records, so mail would stop working. This is the "CNAME at the apex" problem.
  • Chains cost latency, because each extra hop is another lookup if not cached. Keep chains short, ideally one hop.
  • MX and NS records must not point at a name that is a CNAME.

4.2 ALIAS and ANAME

ALIAS (used by several managed DNS providers) and ANAME (a proposed standard that some providers implement) behave like a CNAME at the apex but are resolved by the authoritative server at query time. The server looks up the target's current addresses and returns them as ordinary A or AAAA records, so to the outside world the apex simply has addresses and all its other records remain valid. Cloud-specific forms exist too: Route 53's alias record points at an AWS resource such as a load balancer or CloudFront distribution and is free to query; Cloudflare calls the technique CNAME flattening. Because these are provider features, not part of the DNS standard, they are not portable between providers, and the server-side lookup is a source of extra latency or staleness if the provider caches the target's addresses for too long. A common caveat is that the answer is chosen from the provider's own vantage point, which can make location-aware steering for the target less accurate.

4.3 Other practical details

  • Multiple A records on one name are returned together, and resolvers typically rotate or shuffle their order. Clients usually try the first address, which gives crude load spreading (section 7).
  • Wildcard records such as *.example.com answer for any name that has no more specific record. Useful for multi-tenant SaaS, which gives each tenant a subdomain.
  • MX priority is "lower is preferred"; equal priorities share load. Mail failover is built into the protocol, which is one reason mail survives DNS and server outages better than most web setups.
  • TXT records are limited in size per string (255 bytes, with longer values split into multiple strings) and a response over about 512 bytes historically needed TCP, a point that comes back in section 6.

5. TTL: the tradeoff, and the propagation myths

Every record carries a TTL (time to live), in seconds. It is the owner's instruction to caches: "you may reuse this answer for this long without asking me again." After the TTL expires the cache must fetch a fresh answer. TTL is the only dial you have over how stale a cached answer can be.

5.1 The tradeoff

Long TTL (hours to a day)Short TTL (30 to 300 seconds)
Lookup latencyAlmost every user hits a cache, so lookups are fast.More cache misses, so more users pay the full walk.
Load and costFew queries reach authoritative servers. Cheap.Many more queries. Managed DNS bills per million queries.
Resilience to DNS outageHigh: cached answers keep working through an authoritative outage.Low: caches expire during the outage and users start failing.
Speed of change and failoverSlow: a bad or outdated record lingers for hours.Fast: most clients see a change within minutes.

The sentence to remember: short TTLs buy agility and cost latency, load and fragility; long TTLs buy speed and stability and cost agility. There is no universally right value. Stable records (MX, NS, the apex of a rarely moving site) can sit at hours. Records you may need to move in an emergency (the front door of a service with multi-region failover) are typically kept at 30 to 120 seconds.

5.2 The lowering-TTL-before-a-change routine

Planned moves, such as migrating to a new load balancer, follow a pattern. TTL changes only help once the old TTL has run out in every cache, so you must lower it in advance, at least one old-TTL ahead of the change.

  1. Say the record has a TTL of 86,400 seconds (24 hours). More than 24 hours before the migration, lower it to 60 seconds.
  2. Wait at least 24 hours, so every cache that fetched the old answer has expired it and now holds a copy with the new short TTL.
  3. Change the record to the new address. Most clients move within about a minute.
  4. Keep the old system running until traffic drains, then raise the TTL back to normal.

5.3 Propagation myths

People say "DNS takes up to 48 hours to propagate". The word propagate is misleading, because nothing is pushed. When you change a record at your authoritative server, that server has the new data immediately (with a managed provider, within seconds or a minute across its fleet). Nothing else is updated. Caches elsewhere simply hold the old copy until their own timer ends, and then they ask again and get the new value. So the real worst-case delay is about the old TTL, counted from the moment each cache last refreshed, not a mysterious global process.

  • Myth: the delay is always 24 to 48 hours. It is bounded by the previous TTL, plus some exceptions. The 48-hour folklore comes from NS changes at the registry, where the TLD's delegation records typically carry a TTL of up to 48 hours (for example, two days for com), so switching DNS providers really can take that long to be seen everywhere.
  • Myth: lowering the TTL after you make the change helps. No. Caches that already hold the old record keep it for the old TTL. Lower it before.
  • Myth: everyone obeys the TTL. Mostly, but not all. Some resolvers enforce a minimum TTL (for example ignoring anything below 30 or 60 seconds) or a maximum, and some caches, applications and runtimes ignore TTL entirely and resolve once at startup. Your lowest TTL is a request, not a guarantee.
  • Myth: a user sees the change at the same moment as another user. Not so. Each resolver refreshes at a different time, so for a window equal to one TTL, different users see different answers: some old, some new. Your system must work correctly during that mixed period.
  • Myth: you can "flush the internet". You cannot. You can flush your own resolver or a public one that offers a purge tool, but not millions of ISP resolvers and operating systems.
Watch out Every record in a CNAME chain has its own TTL, and each cache obeys each one. The effective freshness of your front door is bounded by the longest-lived link you do not control. If you point at a vendor name with a 1-hour TTL, your own 30-second TTL on the CNAME does not make you failover-ready within 30 seconds.

6. Transport and security: UDP, TCP, DNSSEC, DoT and DoH

6.1 UDP first, TCP when needed

DNS usually runs over UDP port 53. UDP has no connection setup, so a lookup is one small packet out and one back, with no handshake. That keeps lookups cheap, which matters because a single web page can trigger many of them. Classic DNS limited UDP responses to 512 bytes. If the answer does not fit, the server sets a truncated (TC) flag, and the client retries the same query over TCP port 53, where size is not constrained. Modern clients use the EDNS(0) extension to advertise a larger UDP buffer, commonly about 1,232 bytes to avoid IP fragmentation, so fewer queries need the TCP fallback. TCP is also used for zone transfers between a primary and secondary authoritative server, and for large DNSSEC answers.

The cost of UDP is that it has no built-in reliability or authentication. A lost packet is retried by the client after a timeout (often one to several seconds, which feels like a hang), and anyone able to guess a few fields can forge a reply. This is the root of the security issues below.

6.2 DNSSEC: signed answers

Plain DNS answers are not verified. DNSSEC (DNS Security Extensions) adds digital signatures so a resolver can check that an answer really came from the zone owner and was not altered. The zone publishes a public key (a DNSKEY record) and signs each RRset (RRSIG records). The parent zone vouches for the child's key with a DS record (a hash of the key), so trust forms a chain from the root down, with the root's key built into validating resolvers. A forged or modified answer fails signature validation, and the validating resolver returns an error instead of the bad data.

What DNSSEC provides is integrity and authenticity of the data. It does not encrypt anything (anyone on the path still sees which names you query), and it does not protect against a compromised authoritative server or against attacks beyond the DNS answer. It also adds operational risk: signatures expire, so if you forget to re-sign or botch a key rollover, validating resolvers will treat your whole domain as broken, causing a self-inflicted outage. Larger answers raise the chance of needing TCP, and DNSSEC deployment is still partial, so many teams consider it worth the care for high-value domains such as banks and government, while others run without it.

6.3 DoT and DoH: encrypted transport

Traditional lookups travel in clear text, so anyone on the path (a coffee-shop network, an ISP) can see and tamper with them. Two standards encrypt the hop between the client and the recursive resolver:

  • DNS over TLS (DoT) wraps DNS in a TLS connection on a dedicated port (853). Because it uses its own port, a network operator can easily recognize it and, if policy requires, block or allow it.
  • DNS over HTTPS (DoH) sends DNS queries as HTTPS requests on port 443, so they blend with normal web traffic. Browsers can enable it themselves and talk to a chosen resolver, which improves privacy against the local network but moves the visibility to the DoH provider and can bypass enterprise filtering and internal-name resolution.

Both protect the last hop only. The recursive resolver still walks the tree in the classic way (although encrypted transport between resolvers and authoritative servers is being rolled out), and the resolver itself still sees every name you look up. DNSSEC and DoT/DoH are complementary: one proves the data is genuine, the other hides it in transit.

7. DNS as a load balancing and failover tool

Because DNS can return different answers to different askers, it doubles as the coarsest and cheapest traffic director on the internet. Chapter 9 covers real load balancers (Chapter 9), which work per connection or per request. DNS works one level above: it decides which load balancer, data center or region a client even starts talking to.

7.1 Policies

  • Simple round robin. Publish several A records for one name (for example three IPs). The resolver returns all of them, and many rotate the order on each response; clients tend to use the first. Load spreads roughly evenly over many clients, at zero extra cost. It knows nothing about server health or capacity.
  • Weighted. Each record carries a weight, and the DNS server returns record i in proportion to its weight. A 90/10 split sends about 10% of lookups to a new stack: a cheap way to canary a new region or shift traffic gradually during a migration.
  • Latency-based. The provider keeps measurements of latency from network locations to each of your regions and answers with the region that is fastest for the asking resolver's network. It optimizes for speed rather than geography.
  • Geolocation (geo) routing. The answer depends on the asker's mapped location (country, continent, sometimes state). Used to keep data in a legal region, to serve a localized stack, or to send each continent to its closest deployment.
  • Failover (active-passive). A primary record is returned while healthy, and a secondary record only when the primary fails its health check.
  • Health-checked records. The provider runs probes from several vantage points against your endpoints (an HTTP request to /health every 10 or 30 seconds, say). A record whose endpoint fails a number of consecutive checks is withdrawn from answers. This is the feature that turns DNS from a static list into a failover system.

7.2 Why DNS load balancing is coarse

DNS balancing is valuable and everywhere, but it has hard limits that you should state in an interview.

  • Caching makes it slow to react. Removing a dead server from answers only affects lookups that happen after the change. Every cache that already holds the old answer keeps sending users to the dead IP until its TTL expires, and some ignore TTLs. Failover time is roughly detection time + TTL, never zero (worked in section 12).
  • Clients are uneven. A resolver is a stand-in for many users. If one large ISP resolver serves a million users and caches an answer pointing at server X, all million go to X for the TTL. Weights are applied per lookup, not per user, so a 50/50 weight can produce a 70/30 traffic split when a few huge resolvers (corporate networks, public resolvers, mobile carriers) happen to cache the same side.
  • Clients cache on their own. A long-running service that resolves once and keeps the address, or a connection pool that holds sockets for hours, never sees your update. Applications and runtimes vary widely in how well they honor TTL.
  • No load awareness. Round robin and weights do not know a server is overloaded, only that it is "healthy". Real load balancers use connection counts or response times.
  • Health checks are approximations. A probe from outside answers "does /health return 200?", not "can this server complete a payment?". A shallow check misses partial failures. A deep check that touches dependencies can take your whole fleet out of DNS at once when a shared database slows down, creating an outage bigger than the original problem. Providers typically allow requiring a minimum number of healthy targets or a "fail open" setting for this reason.
  • It balances sessions, not requests. One lookup leads to one connection that may carry thousands of requests, so a single heavy client sticks to one target for a long time.
Key idea Use DNS to choose between regions or large pools (a handful of stable targets), and use a real load balancer inside each pool to balance individual requests and react in seconds. DNS answers "which front door?" and the load balancer answers "which server behind the door?".

8. GeoDNS and anycast

Two different techniques both claim to "send users to the nearest place". They work at different layers and fail differently.

8.1 GeoDNS

A geo-aware authoritative server looks at the source address of the query and maps it, using an IP-to-location database, to a country or region, then returns the record configured for that region. Its strengths are policy and control: you can send EU users to EU infrastructure for data-residency reasons, route a country to a localized site, or steer by business rules. Its weaknesses all come from the fact that the server sees the resolver, not the user. A user in Berlin using a public resolver whose nearest egress is in Amsterdam is mapped to the Netherlands, which is usually fine; a user whose resolver sits in a distant data center may be sent far away. EDNS Client Subnet (ECS) lets the resolver include a truncated prefix of the client's address in the query so the authoritative can map the true location, at the cost of some privacy and of poorer caching (answers cached per prefix). Several public resolvers limit or omit ECS.

8.2 Anycast

With anycast one IP address is announced from many locations through BGP, the internet's routing protocol. Each router chooses the path it considers best, which usually (not always) means the nearest announcement. It needs no DNS cleverness and handles failure well: when a site fails or is taken out, it stops announcing the route and neighbors converge on the others typically within seconds to tens of seconds. It also dilutes attacks, since floods are absorbed by many sites at once. The tradeoffs: "nearest" is a routing outcome that can differ from lowest latency, you cannot deliberately steer a given client elsewhere, and long-lived TCP connections can break if routing shifts mid-connection, so anycast is a natural fit for short exchanges such as DNS itself (UDP query and answer) and for CDN edges that engineer around the problem.

8.3 How they combine

The two are used together. The root servers, TLD servers and big managed DNS providers (Route 53, Cloudflare, NS1) run their authoritative servers on anycast, so the DNS service itself is close to every resolver and survives site failures and floods. On top of that, the DNS answers they return may be geo-aware or latency-based, and may point to anycast addresses (as CDNs do, see Chapter 12) or to regional unicast addresses. A good mental model: anycast makes the DNS server reachable and resilient, while GeoDNS makes the answer location-specific.

9. Service discovery via DNS

Inside a company, services must find each other: the checkout service needs the addresses of the payment service, which change as instances are added, replaced and autoscaled. Service discovery is the general problem, covered in depth in Chapter 49. DNS is the oldest, simplest answer, and it is built into every language and operating system.

9.1 How it is done

  • Plain A records on an internal name. A private DNS zone maps payments.internal to the addresses of instances (or of an internal load balancer). Clients just call the name.
  • SRV records. Beyond an address, SRV gives port, priority and weight: _payments._tcp.internal SRV 10 60 8443 host-a.internal. This lets services run on dynamic ports and lets clients do weighted selection and priority-based failover. It requires clients that understand SRV; browsers and many HTTP libraries do not use it.
  • Platform-provided DNS. Kubernetes runs a cluster DNS (CoreDNS). A Service named payments in namespace shop gets payments.shop.svc.cluster.local, resolving to a stable virtual IP. A "headless" service (no virtual IP) returns the individual pod addresses instead. HashiCorp Consul and AWS Cloud Map also expose registered instances through DNS interfaces.

9.2 Caveats

  • Staleness. The same caching limits apply, but now with autoscaling, rolling deploys and instance churn measured in seconds, a 30-second TTL is slow. Clients hold addresses of instances that no longer exist.
  • Clients that never re-resolve. Some HTTP clients and database drivers resolve at startup or once per pool connection, and keep the first answer for the life of the process. The Java runtime's own address cache has historically differed by configuration (and in some setups was effectively unbounded), which has caused real incidents. Always check how your client library caches, and set connection lifetimes so pools periodically reconnect and re-resolve.
  • Only the first address gets used. Many clients try the first returned address, and with several records the order may be fixed by the server, not shuffled, so load is not spread.
  • No health awareness unless built in. Plain DNS will happily list an unhealthy instance. Service registries (Consul and similar) add health checks and remove failing instances from DNS answers, but still subject to caching.
  • Response size. Many records in one answer can exceed the UDP size and force a TCP retry; very large sets get truncated, which breaks assumptions that a client sees the full list.
  • Load on DNS. Chatty microservices with low TTLs can send enormous query volumes to the cluster DNS, a known cause of latency and failures in large Kubernetes clusters (compounded by search-domain expansion, where a short name triggers several failed lookups before the right one). Caching nodes locally helps.

For these reasons, larger systems often put a proxy or service mesh (Chapter 50) between services, which holds a live, push-updated view of instances so that DNS is used only to find the control plane or a stable virtual IP. DNS discovery remains perfectly good for coarse targets that change rarely, such as databases, queues or an internal load balancer's name.

DNS failures are dramatic because they affect everything at once and look like "the internet is down". The common ones follow, each with a mechanism and a mitigation.

10.1 Negative caching

When a name does not exist, the authoritative server answers NXDOMAIN ("non-existent domain"), and when a name exists but lacks the requested type it answers an empty "NODATA" result. Resolvers cache these negative answers too, for a time taken from the zone's SOA record (the smaller of the SOA's own TTL and its "minimum" field, as specified in RFC 2308). The trap: a team deploys a new service, someone tests newsvc.example.com before the record exists, gets NXDOMAIN, and the resolver now caches the "does not exist" answer. Even after the record is created, that resolver continues to say the name does not exist until the negative TTL elapses. The fix is to create records before they are looked up, and keep the SOA negative TTL modest (minutes, not hours).

10.2 Resolver and authoritative outages

Two different outages with different victims. If a resolver is down, only its users are affected, though they may be a large share of your traffic if it is a big ISP or public resolver, and you cannot fix it. If your authoritative servers are down, everyone eventually fails, but only as caches expire. That is the reason authoritative operators spread NS records over independent providers. In October 2016 a large attack on the managed DNS provider Dyn made many popular sites unreachable for hours for users whose resolvers had expired caches; sites that used a second DNS provider, or that had long TTLs, stayed up for many users. The lesson usually drawn: do not let one DNS provider be a single point of failure, and recognise that a very low TTL makes you more exposed to such an outage, not less.

10.3 DDoS on DNS

DNS is both a target and a weapon. As a target, a flood of queries (often for random nonexistent subdomains, designed to bypass caches and hit the authoritative server) can overwhelm a name server. As a weapon, attackers send queries with a spoofed source address to misconfigured open resolvers, which send larger replies to the victim: a reflection and amplification attack. Defenses: run authoritative servers behind anycast so the load is spread across many sites; use a provider with large capacity and scrubbing; apply response rate limiting (return the same answer less often to one source); never run open recursive resolvers to the public; and keep a second provider ready.

10.4 Cache poisoning

A resolver sends a query and accepts the first reply that matches the question and carries the right transaction ID. In a cache poisoning attack the attacker floods the resolver with forged replies hoping to match the query before the real answer arrives. If a forged reply is accepted, the resolver caches a wrong address (a server controlled by the attacker) for the TTL, and every user of that resolver is redirected invisibly. The famous 2008 Kaminsky attack showed how to do this quickly by forcing many queries for non-existent names. Mitigation layers: randomize both the query ID and the UDP source port so an off-path attacker must guess about 32 bits; 0x20 encoding (randomly varying the upper and lower case of the query name and checking the reply echoes the same); only accepting data in the "bailiwick" (data relevant to the zone being asked about); DNSSEC validation, the definitive fix because forged data cannot carry a valid signature; and, at the application layer, TLS with certificate checks, so even a redirected user cannot be served a convincing fake over HTTPS.

Watch out Many outages blamed on "DNS" are really caching behavior: a service moved but a client held the old IP, or a failing lookup was cached as negative. When debugging, ask three questions: what does the authoritative server say right now, what does the resolver say (and what is its remaining TTL), and what does the client application actually use?

11. DNS in multi-region failover design

When a service runs in two or more regions (Chapter 26 and Chapter 41 cover data and disaster recovery), the question is how users get steered away from a failing region. DNS is the standard first tool, and it is worth knowing both its design and its limits.

11.1 Design choices

  • Active-passive versus active-active. Active-passive sends everything to one region and fails over to the other; simple, but the standby is unproven until needed. Active-active serves users from several regions at once (latency-based or geo routing), so every region is exercised daily and failover is "remove one from the answers".
  • Pick a TTL for the target recovery time. The RTO (recovery time objective) you promise must exceed detection time plus TTL plus any client stickiness. A 5-minute RTO cannot be met with a 1-hour TTL.
  • Health checks should reflect user success. Check an endpoint that exercises the critical path, from several external locations, and require several consecutive failures before failing over to avoid flapping (rapidly switching back and forth). Do not let a failure of a downstream dependency shared by both regions trigger failover; it would only add traffic to the other region.
  • Fail over to a region that can take the load. If the secondary is sized at 30% of the primary, redirecting all users overwhelms it. Pre-scale, or rely on autoscaling with realistic ramp speeds, or shed load deliberately.
  • Data is the hard part. DNS moves the users; it does not move the data. Replication lag decides how much recent data is lost (RPO), and promoting a replica must be coordinated with the DNS switch. Writes arriving at two regions during a partial failure require conflict handling, covered in Chapter 24.
  • Manual versus automatic failover. Automatic failover cuts recovery time but can misfire on a false alarm and cause a self-inflicted outage; many teams alert on the health check and require a human to approve, or use automation with guardrails (minimum healthy region, rate limits on flips).
  • Fail back carefully. Returning to the primary should be deliberate, after data has been resynchronized, ideally gradually with weights (10%, 50%, 100%).

11.2 When DNS is not enough

If you need seconds-level failover regardless of client caching, put an anycast front door in front of both regions: a global load balancer or CDN with a single anycast IP that forwards to the healthy region. The client's cached DNS answer never changes, because the address is stable, and the change happens inside the provider's network. Many providers (global load balancers from the large cloud vendors, CDNs) work this way. A common layered design: a CDN or global anycast layer for fast failover, DNS routing as the policy layer and fallback, and a regional load balancer inside each region (Chapter 9).

12. Worked example: a regional failover with TTL math

Setup. An API at api.example.com serves 10,000 requests per second, mostly from a large pool of clients through resolvers. Region US is primary and region EU is the standby, using DNS failover with a health-checked failover record. Health checkers probe the US endpoint every 10 seconds, and the record fails over after 3 consecutive failed probes. The provider publishes a change to its name servers in about 5 seconds. The record TTL is 60 seconds. At time zero the US region fails completely (all requests fail).

12.1 Step by step

  1. Detection. Probes run every 10 seconds, so the first failed probe arrives between 0 and 10 seconds after the failure, and the third consecutive failure about 20 to 30 seconds after it. Take 30 s as the worst case.
  2. Publish. The provider flips the answer and pushes it to its name servers: about 5 s. The authoritative answer now says EU at t = 35 s.
  3. Cache expiry. Resolvers that fetched the US answer at an arbitrary earlier time hold it for up to 60 s from that moment. Each cache's remaining lifetime at t = 35 s is spread roughly evenly between 0 and 60 s. So the share of traffic still resolving to US falls linearly: 100% at t = 35 s, 50% at t = 65 s, 0% at t = 95 s.
  4. Worst case user. A user whose resolver cached the record just before the switch waits the full TTL: 35 + 60 = 95 s from failure. The average user recovers after about 35 + 30 = 65 s.

12.2 Counting the damage

  • Phase 1: 30 s at 100% failure = 30 × 10,000 = 300,000 failed requests.
  • Phase 2: a triangle, 60 s times 10,000 requests per second times 50% average share = 300,000 failed requests (during the extra 5 seconds of publish delay, add roughly 50,000 more; ignored here for clarity).
  • Total: about 600,000 failed requests, or about 60 seconds of full outage in effect, even though the recovery window is nearly 100 seconds.

12.3 What changing the knobs does

Cutting TTL from 300 s to 60 s removes about two thirds of the failures. Cutting from 60 s to 20 s removes only another third while tripling DNS load, and more clients ignore very small TTLs, so the real gain is smaller. Notice that detection (a flat 300,000 failures in every row) dominates once the TTL is small. To improve further, shorten probe interval and failure count (for example 5 s and 2 failures gives 10 s detection, but more false positives), or take DNS out of the critical path with an anycast front door (section 11.2).

12.4 Reality checks

  • Clients that cache beyond TTL (long-lived processes, some mobile apps, resolvers with a minimum TTL of 5 minutes) form a tail that keeps failing well after t = 95 s. Plan to watch for it and, for server-to-server traffic, set connection lifetimes.
  • The EU region suddenly receives the whole 10,000 requests per second, ramping over the 60-second decay. Its capacity and its cold caches (see Chapter 14 on stampedes) decide whether failover succeeds or merely moves the outage.
  • Retries by clients during phase 1 increase load on the remaining systems; retry budgets and backoff (Chapter 40) matter.

13. A practical checklist

  • Use two independent authoritative providers, or at least a provider with anycast and a track record, for any production domain, and list name servers from both.
  • Set TTLs deliberately: 60 to 300 seconds for failover-critical front doors, hours for stable records such as MX and NS. Lower TTLs one old-TTL before planned changes.
  • Keep CNAME chains short and avoid CNAME at the apex; use ALIAS/ANAME or a cloud alias record.
  • Health checks should be deep enough to detect real failure but not dependent on a component shared by all regions; require consecutive failures; add a minimum-healthy guard.
  • Put a real load balancer behind each DNS answer; use DNS to choose regions, not individual servers.
  • Make clients re-resolve: bounded connection lifetimes, no infinite DNS caching in runtimes.
  • Protect the registrar account with MFA and registry lock; enable auto-renewal; consider DNSSEC for high-value domains if you can run it carefully.
  • Create records before announcing them (negative caching), and keep zones in version control with review.
  • Rehearse failover. An untested DNS failover is a hypothesis.

14. Interview questions and answers

Walk me through what happens when I type www.example.com in my browser, focusing on DNS.

The browser checks its own cache, then asks the operating system's stub resolver, which checks its cache and the hosts file. If both miss, the stub sends a single recursive query to the configured recursive resolver, such as the ISP's or a public one. The resolver checks its cache, and if it misses it walks the hierarchy: it asks a root server, which refers it to the .com TLD servers, which refer it to example.com's authoritative servers, which return the A record with a TTL. The resolver caches the answer (and the referrals) and hands it back, and every layer on the way stores it for the TTL. Only after this does the browser open a TCP and TLS connection to the returned address.

Why can't you put a CNAME at the root of a domain, and what do you do instead?

A CNAME means that the name is an alias for another name, so no other record may exist at that name. The zone apex must hold SOA and NS records, and usually MX and TXT too, so a CNAME there would conflict with them and could break mail. The standard workarounds are provider-specific alias records (ALIAS, ANAME, Route 53 alias, Cloudflare CNAME flattening), where the authoritative server resolves the target itself and returns plain A and AAAA records, or redirecting the bare domain to the www name, which can carry a CNAME. The caveats are that these are not standardized or portable, and the provider may cache the target's addresses for a while.

You set the TTL to 60 seconds, then made a change. Why do some users still see the old value after 10 minutes?

The TTL is an instruction to caches, not a guarantee. Some resolvers enforce a minimum TTL, so 60 seconds may be stretched to several minutes. Some operating systems, browsers or application runtimes cache independently, and long-running services may resolve once at startup and never again. There may also be a longer TTL elsewhere in a CNAME chain, or the change may have been to NS records at the registry, where the parent zone's TTL is often a day or two. Also, if the TTL was lowered only shortly before the change, caches that fetched the record under the old TTL still hold it. The right practice is to lower TTL well in advance and to make sure clients honor it.

How would you use DNS to fail over between two regions, and how fast can it be?

Create a health-checked failover or weighted record where the primary region is answered while its probes pass, and the standby is returned when they fail. Recovery time is approximately the detection time (probe interval times the failure threshold) plus propagation to the name servers plus the TTL, with the average user recovering after about half the TTL and the worst-case user after the full TTL. With a 10-second probe, 3 failures and a 60-second TTL that is roughly 35 seconds to flip and up to 95 seconds for the last users, with a tail from clients that ignore TTLs. If I need faster than that, I would front both regions with an anycast global load balancer or CDN, so the client's cached address does not need to change.

What are the limits of DNS-based load balancing?

First, caching makes every change slow and uneven, so removing a failed server takes up to a TTL and sometimes more. Second, a resolver represents many users, so weights apply to lookups and not to users, and a few large resolvers can skew traffic badly. Third, DNS has no knowledge of server load, only of coarse health, and it balances connections or sessions, not individual requests. Fourth, some clients cache forever or use only the first address. For these reasons I use DNS to pick among regions or large pools, and a real load balancer inside each pool for fine-grained, fast-reacting balancing.

What is the difference between GeoDNS and anycast?

GeoDNS returns different IP addresses to different clients, based on the apparent location of the asker, so the decision is made when the name is resolved and is bound by DNS caching and by how well the resolver's location reflects the user's. Anycast announces one IP address from many sites via BGP and lets network routing deliver each packet to the nearest site, so the decision is made per packet, failover happens when a site withdraws its route, and no client-side change is needed. GeoDNS gives policy control (data residency, per-country content) and anycast gives fast failover and attack dilution. Large DNS and CDN providers combine them: anycast DNS servers that return geo-aware answers.

What is negative caching and how can it bite you?

Resolvers cache not only successful answers but also "this name does not exist" (NXDOMAIN) and "this type does not exist" answers, for a duration derived from the zone's SOA record. If someone looks up a service name before its record is created, the resolver remembers that it does not exist, and after you create the record some users still get failures until the negative TTL expires. It is a classic cause of "the new service works for me but not for them". Prevention is to create the record before any client or test uses it, and to keep the SOA negative-caching value short, a few minutes.

How does DNS cache poisoning work, and how is it prevented?

A recursive resolver sends a query over UDP and accepts a reply that matches the question and transaction ID. An attacker who cannot see the traffic floods the resolver with forged replies, racing the genuine answer, and if a forged one is accepted, the resolver caches the wrong address and redirects all of its users. Defenses include randomizing the query ID and source port, 0x20 case randomization, bailiwick checks that reject unrelated data, and above all DNSSEC, whose signatures a forged answer cannot carry. TLS with proper certificate validation is the additional safety net at the application layer, because a redirected user still cannot present a valid certificate for your domain.

Is DNS a good service discovery mechanism for microservices?

It is simple, universally supported and fine for coarse, slowly changing targets, such as a database primary or an internal load balancer. For dynamic instance sets it is weak, because of TTL caching, clients that never re-resolve, the lack of health awareness unless a registry adds it, and the load that short TTLs create on the DNS servers. SRV records add ports and weights but need client support. Systems with frequent churn typically use a registry with push updates, a sidecar or service mesh, or client-side load balancing, and use DNS only to bootstrap, as in Kubernetes where a stable service name resolves to a virtual IP and the platform handles the instance changes.

What are DNSSEC, DoT and DoH, and do you need all three?

DNSSEC signs DNS data so that a validating resolver can prove an answer is authentic and unmodified, with trust chained from the root. It gives integrity, not confidentiality. DNS over TLS and DNS over HTTPS encrypt the connection between a client and its recursive resolver, giving privacy and tamper resistance on that last hop, but they do not prove the data is correct, and the resolver still sees the queries. They address different threats, so they complement each other; whether you adopt them depends on the threat model. DNSSEC has an operational cost, since signing mistakes cause outages, while DoT and DoH are mostly a client or resolver choice.

How would you design DNS for a globally available service so that a DNS provider outage does not take you down?

I would delegate the zone to two independent managed DNS providers with their own anycast networks, publish both sets of NS records, and keep the zones synchronized through infrastructure as code. TTLs on stable records would be long enough that caches ride out a short provider incident, while failover-critical front-door records would use moderate TTLs. Health checks would feed both providers or I would use a provider-independent anycast entry point for the fastest path. I would monitor resolution from outside my own network, protect the registrar with MFA and lock, and rehearse the loss of one provider. The remaining cost is that provider-specific features such as ALIAS, geo and latency routing may not be identical across vendors, so I would limit myself to the common subset or accept weaker features on the secondary.

15. Related topics worth knowing

  • Split-horizon DNS. The same name returns different answers inside and outside the company network (private addresses internally, public ones externally), often done with separate zones.
  • Happy Eyeballs. Clients try IPv6 and IPv4 in quick succession and use whichever connects first, hiding broken IPv6 paths.
  • Dangling records. A CNAME pointing at a deleted cloud resource can let an attacker claim the resource and serve content under your domain (subdomain takeover). Clean up DNS when you delete infrastructure.
  • Low-TTL traffic management in CDNs. CDNs use TTLs of tens of seconds for their own steering; see Chapter 12.
  • Security overview. DNS-related controls (DNSSEC, CAA, registrar protection) are summarized in Chapter 55.

16. Recap

  • DNS is a hierarchical, delegated, heavily cached database. Root servers point to TLD servers, which point to a domain's authoritative servers, which hold the real records.
  • A stub resolver asks a recursive resolver, which walks root, TLD and authoritative servers iteratively. Browser, OS and resolver all cache answers; the resolver cache does the most work.
  • Know your records: A, AAAA, CNAME, NS, MX, TXT, SRV, SOA, plus ALIAS/ANAME. A CNAME must stand alone, so it cannot sit at the apex; providers synthesize an alias answer instead.
  • TTL trades agility for speed, load and resilience. "Propagation" is really cache expiry, bounded by the previous TTL; lower TTLs before a change, not after, and expect some clients to ignore TTLs.
  • DNS uses UDP with TCP fallback. DNSSEC authenticates data; DoT and DoH encrypt the client-to-resolver hop. They solve different problems.
  • DNS load balancing (round robin, weighted, latency, geo, health-checked) is coarse because of caching and uneven resolvers. Use it to pick regions, and a load balancer for requests.
  • GeoDNS tailors answers by location; anycast makes one address reachable from many sites. Providers combine both.
  • DNS service discovery (A, SRV, Kubernetes DNS) is simple but suffers staleness and clients that never re-resolve.
  • Failure modes: resolver and authoritative outages, TTLs wrong in either direction, negative caching, DDoS and amplification, cache poisoning. Mitigate with multiple providers, anycast, sensible TTLs, randomization and DNSSEC.
  • Failover time is roughly detection time plus TTL. With a 30 s detection and 60 s TTL, the worst-case user recovers after about 95 s and roughly 600,000 of 10,000 rps requests fail; faster recovery requires an anycast front door.
