Event-Driven Design

An event-driven system is built around facts that have already happened, announced to anyone who cares, instead of requests that tell one specific service what to do. This chapter explains how to design those events, the three very different things people mean by "event-driven", and the heavy-duty patterns that grow from it: CQRS, event sourcing, and change data capture.

1. Why event-driven design exists

Start with an ordinary online shop. When a customer places an order, the Order service must tell the Payment service to charge the card, tell the Inventory service to reserve stock, tell the Email service to send a receipt, tell the Analytics service to update revenue numbers, and tell the Recommendation service to learn from the purchase. The simplest way to build this is for the Order service to call each of them directly, one after another, over HTTP.

That design works, but it has a hidden cost: the Order service now knows about every other service. Adding a sixth consumer (say, a fraud scoring system) means changing and redeploying the Order service. If the Email service is slow, the customer's checkout is slow. If the Analytics service is down, the checkout either fails or needs special error handling. The Order service has become the conductor of a growing orchestra and is coupled, in code and in time, to everyone who wants to react to an order.

Event-driven design flips the direction of knowledge. Instead of the Order service saying "Payment, charge this card; Inventory, reserve this stock", it publishes one statement: "Order 1042 was placed". It writes that statement to a durable log or broker (the kind of system described in Chapter 34) and moves on. Any service that cares subscribes and reacts in its own time. The Order service does not know who the subscribers are, how many there are, or whether they are even running right now.

Key idea Event-driven design trades explicit control for loose coupling. The publisher stops knowing about consumers, and consumers stop being able to slow the publisher down. The price is that the flow of the business process is no longer written in one place, and results become eventually consistent. Every technique in this chapter is either a way to collect the benefit or a way to manage that price.

This chapter builds on the messaging mechanics from Chapter 34 (partitions, offsets, consumer groups) and the delivery patterns from Chapter 35 (fan-out, retries, dead-letter queues). Here we focus on the design questions: what should an event contain, who owns it, how do we derive read views from it, and what happens when the event itself becomes the database.

2. Events, commands and queries

Messages that flow between services come in three flavors, and confusing them is the root of most bad event-driven designs. They differ in intent, tense, and who is allowed to reject them.

2.1 Command: "please do this"

A command is a request addressed to one specific handler asking it to change something. It is written in the imperative: ReserveStock, ChargeCard, SendReceipt. A command can be refused (there is no stock left, the card is declined), it has exactly one intended receiver, and the sender usually cares about the outcome. A command is a form of coupling: the sender must know that a receiver exists and what it is called.

2.2 Event: "this happened"

An event is an immutable record that something has already happened, written in the past tense: OrderPlaced, StockReserved, PaymentDeclined. The publisher does not ask anyone to do anything. It cannot be rejected, because the past cannot be rejected; a consumer can ignore it or react to it but cannot make it un-happen. It has zero, one, or many receivers, and the publisher neither knows nor cares.

2.3 Query: "tell me the answer"

A query asks for information and changes nothing. It expects a reply, usually quickly: GetOrderStatus. Queries are the opposite of fire-and-forget: the caller waits.

3. Three things people mean by "event-driven"

The phrase "event-driven architecture" covers three quite different styles. They differ in how much data travels in the event and in whether the events are the source of truth. Mixing them up leads to arguments in which both sides are right about different things.

3.1 Event notification

The event is a thin signal: "Customer 77 changed their address." It carries the id of what changed and perhaps a timestamp, but not the new address. A consumer that wants the address calls back to the owning service (GET /customers/77) to fetch the current state. The publisher stays simple and the event is tiny, so it is cheap and rarely leaks sensitive data. The downsides: every notification can trigger a callback, so a busy topic with many consumers causes a read stampede on the owner; consumers are still coupled at runtime to the owner's API; and by the time the callback arrives, the state may already have changed again (usually fine, since you want the latest anyway).

3.2 Event-carried state transfer

The event carries enough data that consumers never need to call back: "Customer 77 changed address; new address is 12 Main St, Springfield, ZIP 01101, effective at 10:42." Each consumer keeps its own local copy of the fields it needs and updates it from the stream. This gives strong decoupling in time: the Shipping service can keep working even while the Customer service is down, because it has its own copy. The costs are larger events, duplicated data in many places, and a need to treat the copies as caches that may be stale. Data sync in microservices (Chapter 46) leans heavily on this style.

3.3 Event sourcing

Here the events are not a side channel at all: they are the primary storage. The current state of an object is never stored directly; it is derived by replaying every event that ever happened to it. "Account 9 opened, deposited 100, withdrew 30" is the account. Event sourcing is a way to store data, not merely a way to communicate between services (section 7). It may or may not publish events outward as well.

4. Choreography versus orchestration (a preview)

When a business process spans several services (place order, charge card, reserve stock, ship), someone has to decide who triggers what. There are two models.

In choreography there is no central brain. Each service reacts to events and emits its own: Order emits OrderPlaced; Payment hears it and emits PaymentCaptured; Inventory hears that and emits StockReserved; Shipping hears that and acts. The process exists only as the sum of these local reactions. This is the purest event-driven form, with minimal coupling, but the overall flow is hard to see, and a failure halfway needs every participant to know how to undo its own part.

In orchestration a central coordinator (an orchestrator or workflow engine) tells each participant what to do, often with commands, and tracks the state of the whole process. The flow is written in one place and is easy to read, monitor and change, but the orchestrator knows about everyone.

This is only a preview. Failure handling, compensation steps, timeouts and the outbox and inbox patterns that make either style safe are the subject of Chapter 48, and the transactional background is in Chapter 31. A practical rule: choreography works well for short, stable fan-out (3 to 4 reactions), and orchestration becomes the better choice as soon as a process has branching, deadlines, or compensation.

5. Designing a good event

An event is a contract that many teams will depend on for years. It is a public API, and it is harder to change than a REST endpoint, because old events sit in a log forever and consumers you have never heard of may be reading them. Designing the event well is where most of the leverage is.

5.1 Naming

Name events in the past tense, in the language of the business, after what happened: OrderPlaced, InvoicePaid, SeatReleased. Avoid names that describe a database operation (OrderRowUpdated, CustomerUpsert), because they expose your storage and say nothing about why the change occurred. The difference matters: CustomerUpdated tells a consumer little, whereas CustomerMovedAddress and CustomerChangedEmail tell it exactly what to do. A good name often has the aggregate (the thing that changed), a verb in the past tense, and lives in a namespaced topic such as shop.orders.v1.

5.2 Granularity

How big is one event? Too fine, and a single business action emits twenty events that consumers must reassemble in order (OrderLineAdded times ten, then OrderTotalComputed). Too coarse, and one OrderChanged blob forces every consumer to diff old and new to guess what happened. A reliable guide: one event per meaningful business fact, emitted at the boundary where a transaction commits. A customer pressing "Place order" is one event, OrderPlaced, containing the lines; it is not one event per line.

5.3 Schema and payload

A typical event has an envelope (metadata that is the same for all events) and a payload (business data specific to this event type).

{
  "eventId":       "01HZX9K3Q7M2N8V4B6T1R0YPSD",   // unique, lets consumers de-duplicate
  "eventType":     "OrderPlaced",
  "schemaVersion": 2,
  "occurredAt":    "2026-03-14T10:42:07.113Z",      // when it happened in the business
  "producer":      "order-service",
  "aggregateId":   "order-1042",                     // also the partition / ordering key
  "sequence":      7,                                // position within this aggregate
  "correlationId": "req-8f3a...",                    // the user request that started it
  "causationId":   "01HZX9K2...",                    // the event or command that caused this one
  "payload": {
    "customerId": "cust-77",
    "lines": [ { "sku": "A-100", "qty": 2, "unitPrice": { "amount": 1999, "currency": "USD" } } ],
    "total":  { "amount": 3998, "currency": "USD" }
  }
}

Use a schema language with enforced compatibility rules: Avro, Protobuf, or JSON Schema, kept in a schema registry that rejects incompatible changes at publish time. Money should be integer minor units plus currency, never a float. Timestamps should be in UTC. Choose explicit field names; avoid "data" blobs of free-form JSON that nobody can validate.

5.4 Event ids and idempotency

Brokers typically give at-least-once delivery, so a consumer may see the same event twice after a crash or rebalance. A unique eventId (a UUID or time-sortable ULID, see Chapter 32) lets the consumer record which ids it has already processed and skip repeats. Without an id, de-duplication must rely on guesswork about payload equality.

5.5 Ordering keys

A log such as Kafka guarantees order only within a partition, and the partition is chosen by hashing a key. So the key you choose defines the unit of ordering. Choose the aggregate id (order-1042): all events about one order land in one partition and are consumed in order, while different orders spread over partitions for parallelism. Keying by something coarse (like country) creates hot partitions; keying randomly loses ordering entirely, so OrderCancelled might be processed before OrderPlaced. Global ordering across all aggregates is almost never needed and is very expensive. Include a per-aggregate sequence number so consumers can detect gaps or out-of-order arrivals.

5.6 Metadata and the two clocks

Carry occurredAt (business time, when the thing happened) separately from the broker's ingest timestamp (when the system learned about it). They diverge during outages and backfills, and stream processors (Chapter 37) must choose which one to window by. The correlationId ties together everything triggered by one user action, and the causationId records the immediate parent. These two fields cost almost nothing and make tracing across services possible (section 14).

5.7 Versioning and evolution

Events outlive code. A consumer built today may read an event published three years ago. Plan for change from the start:

  • Additive changes are safe. Adding an optional field with a default is backward compatible: new consumers use it, old consumers ignore it. Most registries enforce this by default.
  • Never repurpose a field. Changing total from "with tax" to "without tax" while keeping the name silently corrupts consumers. Add a new field and deprecate the old one.
  • Breaking changes need a new version. Publish OrderPlaced.v2 to a new topic or with a new schemaVersion, run both for a migration window, and retire v1 once consumers have moved.
  • Tolerant reader. Consumers should read only the fields they need and ignore unknown ones. This single habit prevents most breakages.
  • Do not leak your internal model. Publish a deliberate, stable public event shape, not a serialized database row or JPA entity. Otherwise every refactor of your tables becomes a breaking change for the company.
Interview tip When asked to "design the events" for a system, say the five things out loud: the name in past tense, whether it is notification or state transfer, the schema and who owns it, the partition key and why, and how it will evolve. This shows you treat events as a long-lived API.

6. CQRS: separate models for writing and reading

CQRS stands for Command Query Responsibility Segregation. The idea is simple to state: use one model to handle commands (writes) and a different model to answer queries (reads). In a conventional application a single model serves both: the same orders table, the same entity classes, the same service layer. That is fine until the needs of the two sides pull apart.

6.1 Why the two sides diverge

Writes care about correctness: enforce business rules, validate invariants ("an order cannot ship before it is paid"), serialize concurrent changes to the same object. They want a normalized, transactional structure. Reads care about shape and speed: a screen wants "order number, customer name, three product titles, status badge, and total" in one lookup, perhaps joined from five tables, perhaps filtered by free text. Forcing both into one model means writes get slowed by indexes added for reads, reads need expensive joins, and the model becomes a compromise that serves neither well. Read traffic is also typically 10 to 100 times larger than write traffic and wants to scale differently.

6.2 How CQRS works

The write side accepts commands, loads the relevant object, checks the rules, and records the change in its own store. It then publishes an event describing what changed. The read side subscribes to those events and builds one or more projections (also called read models or views): denormalized structures designed purely to answer specific queries. A projection might be a row in a key-value store keyed by order id holding the whole screen, a document in a search index, or a pre-aggregated table of sales by day. Queries go only to the projections and never touch the write store.

6.3 Eventual consistency

Because projections are updated asynchronously, a read immediately after a write may return old data. This is eventual consistency: if no further writes happen, all projections converge to the correct state, typically within milliseconds to seconds, but with no hard bound. Section 12 covers how to make this tolerable for users. It is the defining cost of CQRS, and it is why you should not adopt it casually.

6.4 Levels of CQRS

CQRS is not all-or-nothing. A spectrum exists, and most teams should stop early:

  1. Separate code paths, same database. Command handlers and query handlers are different classes, and queries may use plain SQL or database views. No eventual consistency at all. This alone cleans up many codebases.
  2. Same database, separate read tables. A projector keeps denormalized tables in the same transaction or shortly after.
  3. Separate stores. Writes in a relational database, reads from Elasticsearch, Redis, or a column store, joined by an event stream. Full eventual consistency.
  4. CQRS plus event sourcing. The write side stores events instead of state (section 7). Powerful but the heaviest.

6.5 When CQRS is worth it, and when it is not

Reach for it when read and write workloads differ sharply in volume or shape, when you need several very different views of the same data (a search view, a reporting view, a mobile summary), or when the domain is complex enough that the write model benefits from being free of reporting concerns. Avoid it for plain create-read-update-delete applications, for small teams, and where users demand read-your-own-write behavior everywhere. In an interview, the honest answer is: "I would start with one model and introduce a read model for the one query that hurts, rather than split everything."

Watch out CQRS does not require event sourcing, and event sourcing does not require CQRS in a strict sense (though a pure event-sourced store makes queries so awkward that they nearly always go together). Many production systems use CQRS with an ordinary relational write store plus a change-data-capture feed (section 9) to build the read side.

7. Event sourcing in depth

Ordinary databases store current state. When a customer changes their address, the old address is overwritten and gone, and unless someone built an audit table you cannot tell what it used to be, or why it changed. Event sourcing reverses this: the database stores the sequence of facts, and the current state is computed from them. This is exactly how a bank ledger works: your balance is not the primary data, it is the sum of all transactions. A balance that disagreed with the ledger would be a bug in the balance.

7.1 The event store

The event store is an append-only database. Events are only ever added at the end; they are never updated or deleted. Each event belongs to a stream, usually one stream per aggregate instance (account-9), and carries a version number that increases by one with each event in that stream. The store offers three core operations: append events to a stream, read a stream in order, and read all events across streams in global order (for projections). Purpose-built stores (EventStoreDB) exist, but many teams build one on a relational table with columns (stream_id, version, event_type, payload, metadata, created_at), or use Kafka with infinite or long retention.

7.2 Aggregates and optimistic concurrency

An aggregate is a cluster of objects treated as one consistency unit (an account, an order). All rules that must hold atomically are enforced inside one aggregate. To handle a command, the application: (1) loads the aggregate's events and replays them to rebuild its current state, (2) runs the business rules against that state, (3) produces one or more new events, and (4) appends them to the stream with an expected version. If another writer appended an event in the meantime, the expected version no longer matches and the append fails; the command is retried against fresh state. This is optimistic concurrency and it gives each aggregate serial ordering without locks.

append(stream = "account-9", expectedVersion = 3, events = [MoneyWithdrawn(30)])
  -> succeeds only if the stream currently ends at version 3
  -> otherwise "version conflict": reload, re-check rules (is there still enough balance?), retry

7.3 Replay, snapshots and projections

Replaying thousands of events on every command would be slow. A snapshot is a saved copy of the aggregate's state at a given version, say after event 1,000. To load the aggregate, read the latest snapshot and replay only the events after it. Snapshots are a pure optimization: they can be deleted and regenerated at any time because the events remain the truth. Take them periodically (every N events or every few minutes). Many aggregates never need them: a typical order has perhaps 10 to 30 events over its life, so replay is trivial; a long-lived account with millions of events does need them, and a better remedy is often to split the aggregate (one stream per month or per statement period).

Reading uses projections, exactly as in CQRS. A projector reads the global event feed and updates a read model. A key power of event sourcing is that projections are disposable and rebuildable: need a new report, a new search index, or a fix to a bug in an old projection? Create the new projector, point it at the start of the event history, and let it replay everything. Because it consumes the same feed at its own pace, the live system is unaffected. This is why a log with long retention (Chapter 34) pairs naturally with event sourcing, and why a stream processor (Chapter 37) can serve as the projector.

7.4 What you gain

  • A complete audit trail for free. You know not just the state, but every change, in order, with who and why. Regulated industries value this enormously.
  • Temporal queries. "What was this account's balance on 31 March?" is just a replay up to that moment. "How many carts were abandoned after adding a third item?" is answerable from history you would otherwise have thrown away.
  • Debugging by replay. Reproduce a production bug by feeding the exact event sequence into a local copy of the code.
  • New read models later. Decisions you did not foresee (a new report) can be answered from the full past, not only from the date you started collecting.
  • Natural fit for event-driven integration. The events you store are the events you publish.

7.5 Schema evolution and upcasting

The events are immutable and live forever, but your understanding of the domain changes. In 2024 you wrote AddressChanged{address: string}; in 2026 you need structured fields. You cannot go back and rewrite the history. Instead you use upcasting: on read, a small function transforms an old event version into the current shape before the application sees it (v1 -> v2, splitting the string into street, city, postcode with best-effort defaults). The stored bytes never change; the code always works with the newest shape. Chains of upcasters (v1 to v2 to v3) can accumulate, which is a real maintenance burden. Alternatives are to publish a new event type and handle both in the aggregate, or, as a last resort, to run a one-off migration that copies the stream into a new one, which breaks the "never rewrite" ideal and should be rare and audited.

7.6 The GDPR deletion problem

Privacy law (such as GDPR's right to erasure) says that on request you must delete a person's personal data. An append-only, immutable log directly contradicts this. Practical approaches, from most to least favored:

  • Keep personal data out of events. Store a reference such as customerId in the event and keep the name, address and email in a separate, mutable store that can be erased. Events then contain nothing that identifies a person on their own. This is the cleanest option and should be the default design.
  • Crypto-shredding. Encrypt each person's personal fields in events with a per-person key held in a key store. To "delete" the person, destroy the key; the ciphertext remains in the log but is permanently unreadable. Projections must also be rebuilt or purged.
  • Rewrite the stream. Copy the stream with personal fields redacted and swap it in. It works but defeats immutability and is operationally risky; reserve it for emergencies.

Whichever you pick, remember that copies exist: projections, search indexes, data warehouse tables, backups and downstream consumers all hold the data. Erasure must propagate to all of them, which is easier if the erasure itself is published as an event (CustomerErased). See Chapter 56 for the wider privacy picture.

7.7 Pitfalls

  • It is a large commitment. Event sourcing changes how you model, test, query and operate. Adopting it for a simple CRUD domain is a classic over-engineering mistake.
  • Querying is hard. You cannot run "find all accounts with balance over 1,000" against a stream store; you need projections for every question, and each is more code.
  • Eventual consistency everywhere, including where users expect immediate feedback.
  • Bad events are forever. A poorly named or too-low-level event (FieldXSet) is a permanent tax, because you cannot fix history, only upcast it.
  • Replay side effects. A projector rebuilding history must never resend emails or recharge cards. Separate pure projections from side-effecting reactions, and make the latter ignore replayed events (for example by checking occurredAt or using a different consumer group that starts at "latest").
  • Snapshot and upcaster drift, and a growing event store, need housekeeping and monitoring.
  • Cross-aggregate invariants cannot be enforced atomically. They need a saga or process manager (Chapter 48).

Let us follow one account, account-9, through its life, using event sourcing with a read-side projection and a snapshot. Rule: the balance may not go below zero (no overdraft).

Notice what a command does here: it is judged against the state rebuilt from events, and only the accepted outcome becomes a fact. The balance of 100 is nowhere stored as a primary value; replaying 0 + 100 - 30 + 50 - 20 produces it.

8.1 Concurrency in action

Suppose two withdrawals of 80 arrive at the same moment when the balance is 100 (stream at version 2). Both handlers load the stream at version 2, both see 100, both decide 80 is allowed, and both try to append with expectedVersion = 2. The store accepts the first (now version 3, balance 20) and rejects the second with a version conflict. The second handler reloads, now sees a balance of 20, and rightly rejects the withdrawal. Without the version check, the account would have gone to minus 60. This is the same guarantee a row lock gives, but achieved with a compare-and-append.

8.2 Projections

Three projectors consume the global feed. The balance view keeps one row per account, updated on every deposit or withdrawal, and serves "what is my balance" in one key lookup. The statement view stores the list of transactions per month for the app screen. The fraud view counts withdrawals per hour per account. Each is a separate consumer group with its own offset, so a slow or buggy fraud projector never delays the balance view.

8.3 Rebuild and snapshot

Suppose after two years there are 2,000,000 accounts averaging 400 events each, 800 million events in all. The risk team asks for a new view: "largest single withdrawal per account, per quarter". We write the projector, start a new consumer group at offset zero, and let it read the entire history. At a throughput of 50,000 events per second, a rebuild takes about 800,000,000 / 50,000 = 16,000 seconds, a little over 4.4 hours, in the background while production continues. When it catches up we switch the risk dashboard to the new view. For loading account-9 itself, 400 events replay in well under a millisecond, but for a corporate account with 5 million events we snapshot every 1,000 events, so a load reads one snapshot plus at most 999 events.

9. Change data capture

Not every system can or should be event sourced. Most are built on an ordinary database with current-state tables, and still need to tell other systems about changes: refresh a search index, invalidate a cache, feed a warehouse, trigger a workflow. Change data capture (CDC) is the technique of turning a database's own changes into a stream of events, without asking the application to publish them.

9.1 Three ways to detect changes

MethodHow it worksStrengthsWeaknesses
PollingA job periodically runs SELECT ... WHERE updated_at > :lastSimple, no special permissionsDelay equals the polling interval; misses deletes; misses rows updated in the same timestamp tick or by transactions that commit out of order; adds query load
TriggersDatabase triggers write each change to a shadow "changes" tableCaptures all changes including deletes, inside the same transactionAdds write latency and load to every transaction; trigger logic is hidden and awkward to maintain; schema changes break it
Log-based CDCRead the database's own transaction log (MySQL binlog, PostgreSQL write-ahead log, Oracle redo log)Low impact on the source; captures every committed change including deletes in commit order; near real time; no application changeNeeds log access and configuration; operational complexity; events mirror table structure

9.2 How log-based CDC works

Every serious database already writes each change to a durable log before applying it, in order to survive crashes and to feed its own replicas. MySQL calls this the binlog (set to row-based format so each row change is recorded), and PostgreSQL calls it the write-ahead log (WAL), exposed through logical decoding and a replication slot. A CDC tool such as Debezium connects as if it were a replica, reads this log, decodes each committed row change into a structured event with the row's before and after values and an operation type (create, update, delete), and writes it to a topic, typically one topic per table, in Kafka via Kafka Connect.

9.3 Initial snapshot

The transaction log does not go back to the beginning of time; old segments are discarded. So when a CDC connector first starts, it cannot learn the existing rows from the log. It performs an initial snapshot: it records the current log position, reads every existing row of the captured tables (emitting them as "read" events), and then switches to streaming the log from the recorded position. The recorded position guarantees that changes made during the snapshot are not lost. For large tables, snapshots can take hours and add read load, so teams use incremental snapshots (chunked, interleaved with the live stream), run them against a replica, and avoid re-snapshotting by protecting the connector's stored offset and the replication slot. A warning: an abandoned PostgreSQL replication slot prevents WAL cleanup and can fill the source database's disk.

9.4 Ordering

Log-based CDC preserves commit order within a database. Debezium keys each message by the table's primary key, so all changes to one row go to the same Kafka partition in order, which is the ordering that matters for building a correct copy of that row. Order across tables or partitions is not guaranteed on the consumer side, so a consumer joining orders and order_lines may see a line before its parent order and must tolerate that (buffer, retry, or design projections that accept either order). Transaction boundary markers can be emitted if grouping is needed.

9.5 Schema changes

When someone runs ALTER TABLE, the shape of subsequent events changes. Connectors track schema history and publish the new shape, often with a schema registry, but a renamed or dropped column is a breaking change for downstream readers. Treat table schemas as a published API: add columns rather than rename, keep a deprecation window, and monitor compatibility. This is also the main reason raw CDC streams of internal tables should not be exposed broadly across a company (section 16).

9.6 The outbox pattern via CDC

There is a trap when a service wants to both update its database and publish an event: these are two systems, and without a distributed transaction one can succeed while the other fails. Update the order row then crash before publishing, and the world never learns the order was placed. Publish first then fail the commit, and the world hears about an order that does not exist. The fix, detailed in Chapter 31 and Chapter 48, is the transactional outbox: in the same local database transaction that changes the business tables, insert a row into an outbox table describing the event. A relay then publishes outbox rows to the broker. Instead of a polling relay, CDC is an elegant relay: Debezium tails the log, sees the new outbox row, and publishes it (it even has an outbox event router that maps columns to topic, key and payload). The business change and the intention to publish commit atomically, and the delivery is at least once, so consumers must be idempotent.

10. Materialized views: keeping search, cache and warehouse in sync

A materialized view is a precomputed, stored answer to a query, kept up to date as the source data changes. Databases offer them natively, but in a distributed system the term is used more broadly: any secondary copy of data, shaped for a specific access pattern, maintained from the primary source. A search index, a Redis cache of product pages, a data warehouse table, a recommendation feature store and a CQRS read model are all materialized views.

The common mistake is dual writes: the application writes to the database and then also writes to Elasticsearch and Redis in the same request. When one write fails, or two requests interleave differently on each system, the copies silently diverge, and nothing ever repairs them. A single flawed ordering is enough: request A sets the price to 10, request B sets it to 12, the database sees A then B, but the search index sees B then A and keeps 10 forever.

The robust alternative is a single source of truth with a change feed: write only to the primary database, capture its changes by CDC or an outbox, and have each derived view subscribe to the feed and update itself. Order is preserved per key, failures are retried from the last offset, and a broken view can be rebuilt by replaying the feed or re-snapshotting.

10.1 Details that decide correctness

  • Idempotent upserts. A view must apply an event keyed by the entity id with an upsert, so replays and duplicates produce the same result. Writing quantity = 7 is idempotent; writing quantity = quantity + 1 is not.
  • Version guard. Store the source version (or log position) on each view row and ignore any event older than what is already applied. This neutralizes out-of-order or replayed updates. Elasticsearch supports external versioning for exactly this purpose.
  • Deletes are events too. CDC emits a delete and often a tombstone; the view must remove the entry. Polling-based syncs usually miss deletes.
  • Cache invalidation. For caches (Chapter 13), the change stream gives a clean way to delete or refresh keys when the source changes, instead of relying only on TTL. Prefer invalidating (deleting) the key over writing the new value, to avoid racing with a concurrent read-through fill.
  • Warehouse loading. Stream to a landing area as an append-only change log, then merge into current-state tables on a schedule. Keeping the raw change log allows historical (point-in-time) analysis, in the spirit of Chapter 37.
  • Backfill and repair. Every view needs a documented way to rebuild: re-snapshot the source, or replay the stream from the beginning, into a new copy, then switch aliases. Without this, any bug becomes permanent drift.
  • Lag monitoring. The key health metric is consumer lag, and also end-to-end freshness: now minus the source timestamp of the latest applied event.

11. Event streams as an integration backbone

As the number of systems grows, point-to-point integration scales badly: N systems with direct links approach N times (N minus 1) connections, each with its own protocol, schedule and failure behavior. A central, durable, replayable event stream (a Kafka-like log; see Chapter 34) used as the shared backbone turns this into N connections: each system publishes its facts once and subscribes to what it needs.

What the backbone gives you:

  • Decoupling in time and space. Producers and consumers need not be running together, and neither knows the other's address.
  • Replay. A new service can start from the beginning of the stream and bootstrap its own state, instead of asking for a data export.
  • Independent scaling and fan-out. Adding a tenth consumer costs the producer nothing.
  • A shared record. Analytics, search, audit and machine learning all read the same facts that operations produced.
  • A migration tool. During a monolith-to-services move (Chapter 44) a CDC feed from the old database lets new services build their data without touching the monolith.

What it demands: governance. Topics need owners, documented schemas with compatibility checks, naming conventions, retention and access policies, and a deprecation process. Without these the backbone becomes a swamp (section 16). Also, give each domain its own events: a company-wide CustomerEvent that every team edits is a coupling hub, not a backbone. See also Chapter 46 for how services keep their own data and use events instead of shared tables.

12. Temporal coupling and eventual-consistency UX

Temporal coupling means two components must be available at the same moment to get work done. A synchronous call has it: if the Inventory service is down, an Order that needs it fails right now. An event-driven flow removes it: the Order service writes its event and finishes; Inventory processes the event whenever it is next up, even an hour later. This improves availability and absorbs load spikes (the log is a buffer), but it moves the problem to the user experience, because the user is no longer told the final outcome at the moment of the click.

12.1 Designing for the gap

  • Acknowledge, then complete. Respond immediately with "Order received" (HTTP 202 Accepted) and a handle for status, rather than pretending it is finished. Show states honestly: Pending, Confirmed, Failed.
  • Read your own writes. After a user's own write, show them the result locally (optimistic UI), or route their next read to the write model, or pass a version token ("I need data at least as fresh as version 57") that the read side waits for briefly. This solves the most jarring case, a user saving something and not seeing it.
  • Push the outcome. Use WebSocket, server-sent events, or a push notification to tell the user when the asynchronous work finishes, rather than making them refresh.
  • Make failure visible and recoverable. If payment later fails, the user needs an email or in-app message and a clear way to retry. A background failure with no signal is a lost customer.
  • Choose the boundaries wisely. Keep things strongly consistent where the user cannot tolerate staleness (does this seat still exist, is the balance enough) and accept lag where they can (analytics counters, recommendations, search results).
  • Idempotent actions. Impatient users click twice. A client-generated request id lets the server drop the duplicate command (Chapter 32).

13. Testing and debugging event flows

An event-driven system has no single call stack to step through. Behavior emerges from many independent reactions, and bugs hide in timing, ordering and duplicates. A practical test strategy has several layers.

13.1 Test levels

  • Unit tests on pure logic. Aggregates and projectors are ideal for the "given events, when command, then events" style: given [AccountOpened, MoneyDeposited(100)], when Withdraw(500), then expect rejection. With event sourcing these tests read like business specifications and need no database.
  • Consumer tests. Feed a consumer a recorded event and assert its effect. Run each test with the same event twice to prove idempotency, and with events in a swapped order to prove tolerance.
  • Contract tests. The producer's schema and the consumers' expectations are verified in CI (schema registry compatibility checks, consumer-driven contracts; see Chapter 47). This catches the most common production break, a producer renaming a field.
  • Integration tests with real infrastructure. Start a broker and database in containers (for example with Testcontainers), run the real publish-and-consume path, and assert eventual outcomes by polling with a timeout, never by sleeping a fixed time.
  • End-to-end and chaos tests. Kill a consumer mid-batch, delay messages, duplicate them, and restart the broker, then check the system converges to the right state.
  • Replay tests. Replay a captured slice of production events into a new version of a projector and compare its output with the old one. This is a strong safety net for upgrades.

13.2 Debugging approaches

  • Follow the correlation id. Search logs and traces for the id of the originating request to see every event it caused across services.
  • Inspect the log itself. Because events are durable, you can read the actual messages on the topic at a given offset, which beats guessing. Keep a tool to browse topics by key.
  • Check consumer lag and the dead-letter queue first. A stuck consumer or poison message (Chapter 35) explains most "nothing happened" reports.
  • Reproduce by replay. Copy the relevant events into a test environment and replay them against the suspect code, with breakpoints.
  • Think in orderings. When a bug is intermittent, ask which interleaving of events would produce it, and write that interleaving as a test.

14. Observability for event flows

You cannot operate what you cannot see. Event flows need specific signals; the general toolkit of correlation ids, distributed tracing and log aggregation is covered in Chapter 52 and SLO practice in Chapter 54. The event-specific points:

  • Propagate trace context through messages. Put the trace id and span id in event headers, so a trace continues across the asynchronous hop. A consumer span is usually linked to the producer span, not a child that blocks it, because the work happens later.
  • Consumer lag. Per consumer group and partition, the number of events (and, more usefully, seconds) behind the head of the log. Alert on sustained growth, not on brief spikes.
  • End-to-end freshness. The time from occurredAt to the moment the projection reflects it. This is the number that matches user experience.
  • Throughput and error rates per topic and consumer: processed per second, failures, retries, and dead-letter volume.
  • Business-level checks. Reconciliation jobs that compare source and view counts or checksums catch silent drift that technical metrics miss.
  • Poison and duplicate indicators. A repeated same-offset failure, or a rising duplicate-drop count, signals trouble early.

16. Anti-patterns

16.1 Event soup

Hundreds of fine-grained, loosely named events, with chains where A triggers B triggers C triggers A, and nobody can say what a business process does. Symptoms: a bug hunt requires reading six repositories; a change to one event breaks unknown listeners; infinite loops appear. Cures: model events around business facts and bounded contexts (Chapter 39), document each flow, use orchestration for complex processes, and keep a catalog of events with owners and consumers.

16.2 Events as remote procedure calls

Publishing CalculateTaxRequested and waiting on TaxCalculated using a temporary reply topic is a synchronous call wearing an event costume. It adds latency and failure modes (lost replies, correlation bookkeeping) and none of the decoupling. If the caller truly needs the answer now, use a real request-response call; if it does not, make the interaction a fact plus a later reaction.

16.3 Huge events

Events carrying entire documents, images or hundreds of kilobytes per message hurt broker throughput, memory, and replay time, and often leak data to consumers who should not see it. Brokers typically have a message size limit near one megabyte and perform best with small messages. Use the claim check pattern: store the large blob in object storage and put a reference plus metadata in the event. Also resist the temptation to publish "the whole aggregate on every change" when two changed fields would do.

16.4 Missing ownership

If no team owns a topic, nobody maintains its schema, nobody answers when it breaks, and many teams publish different shapes into it. Every event type needs one owning producer, a documented schema, a compatibility policy and a named contact. Related error: consumers reaching into raw CDC topics of another team's tables, which makes private table design an accidental public API. Publish deliberate domain events (through an outbox) for cross-team use and keep raw CDC for internal pipelines.

16.5 Other traps

  • Assuming exactly-once. Design every consumer for duplicates and out-of-order arrival; see Chapter 32.
  • Dual writes to a database and a broker without an outbox (section 9.6).
  • Event sourcing everywhere. Applying it to every entity because it is fashionable, including those with no need for history.
  • Ignoring schema evolution until the first breaking change takes down three teams.
  • Leaking internal state by serializing database entities directly as events.
  • No replay or backfill story, so a fixed bug cannot repair the already-corrupted views.
  • Hidden synchronous coupling: a consumer that synchronously calls five other services in its handler makes the "decoupled" system fragile again.

17. Interview Q&A

What is the difference between an event and a command, and why does it matter?

A command is a request to a specific handler to do something, written in the imperative, and the handler may refuse it. An event is an immutable statement that something already happened, written in the past tense, with any number of listeners and no ability for them to veto it. It matters because it decides the direction of coupling: commands make the sender depend on the receiver, while events make receivers depend on the publisher's facts. If you name an event like a command (SendEmail) and expect one service to act, you have built an RPC with extra steps and lost the benefits of decoupling.

Explain event notification versus event-carried state transfer. When would you choose each?

Notification sends only a signal and an id, so consumers call the owner to fetch current data; the event is small and the owner stays the single source of truth, but consumers remain coupled to the owner's availability and can cause a read stampede. Event-carried state transfer includes the changed data so consumers keep local copies and never call back, which gives resilience and fast reads at the price of bigger events and stale or duplicated data. I would choose notification for large, sensitive or fast-changing data, and state transfer when consumers must keep working while the owner is down or need low-latency local lookups.

What is CQRS and when is it worth the complexity?

CQRS uses separate models for commands and queries: the write side enforces rules against a normalized store, and the read side maintains denormalized projections updated from events, so each can be shaped and scaled independently. It is worth it when reads and writes have very different volumes or shapes, when several different views of the same data are needed, or when the write domain is complex. The cost is eventual consistency and extra moving parts, so for ordinary CRUD I would not use it, or I would start with separate code paths over one database and only split storage for the one query that hurts.

How does event sourcing work, and how do you handle concurrent updates to the same aggregate?

Instead of storing current state, you append immutable events to a per-aggregate stream and compute state by replaying them. To handle a command you load and replay the stream, check the business rules, and append the resulting events with an expected version number. If another writer appended first, the version no longer matches and the append is rejected, so you reload, re-evaluate the rules against fresh state, and retry. This optimistic concurrency gives serial ordering per aggregate without locks, and for example prevents two simultaneous withdrawals from overdrawing an account.

What are snapshots and upcasting in event sourcing, and why are they needed?

A snapshot is a stored copy of an aggregate's state at some version, so loading means reading the latest snapshot and replaying only later events; it is a disposable optimization for streams with many events. Upcasting addresses schema evolution: because stored events are immutable, when their shape changes you transform old versions into the current shape on read, instead of rewriting history. Both exist because the log is permanent: snapshots keep reads fast as it grows, and upcasters let new code understand events written years ago.

How do you reconcile event sourcing with the GDPR right to erasure?

The best approach is to keep personal data out of events, storing only an identifier in the event and holding the name, address and email in a separate store that can be deleted. If personal data must live in events, use crypto-shredding: encrypt each person's fields with a per-person key and destroy the key on an erasure request, which makes the ciphertext unreadable while leaving the log intact. Rewriting streams is a last resort. In all cases you must also purge or rebuild projections, search indexes, warehouse copies and backups, which is easier if the erasure is itself published as an event.

Why is log-based CDC usually better than polling or triggers, and what are its caveats?

Log-based CDC reads the database's own transaction log, so it captures every committed change, including deletes, in commit order, with near real-time latency and little extra load, and without changing the application. Polling has delay, misses deletes and can miss concurrent updates, while triggers add overhead to every write transaction and are hard to maintain. The caveats are operational: you need log access and correct configuration, an initial snapshot for existing data, care with abandoned replication slots that retain log files, and awareness that the events mirror table structure, so schema changes become downstream breaking changes.

What is the transactional outbox and how does CDC relate to it?

The outbox solves the dual-write problem: updating a database and publishing a message cannot be atomic across two systems, so a crash between them loses or invents events. With the outbox, the service writes the business change and an event row to an outbox table in the same local transaction, so they commit or fail together. A relay then forwards the outbox rows to the broker, and CDC is an excellent relay because it tails the log, sees the new outbox row and publishes it in order. Delivery is at least once, so consumers must de-duplicate using the event id.

How do you keep a search index and a cache consistent with a primary database?

Avoid dual writes, because partial failures and interleaved requests make the copies silently diverge. Write only to the primary database, capture its changes with CDC or an outbox, and let separate consumers update the index and invalidate the cache from the ordered change stream. Use idempotent upserts and a version check so replays and out-of-order events do not corrupt the view, handle deletes explicitly, and keep a rebuild path by replaying the stream or re-snapshotting. Monitor consumer lag and end-to-end freshness, and reconcile counts periodically.

A user places an order and the confirmation page shows nothing because processing is asynchronous. How would you fix the experience?

First, be honest in the interface: return 202 Accepted immediately with an order id and show a PENDING state rather than pretending to be done. Then give read-your-own-writes by showing the user's own submission optimistically, or routing their next read to the write model, or passing a version token the read side waits on briefly. Finally, push the result through WebSocket, server-sent events or a notification when it completes, and make failures visible with a retry path. For things like seat availability that cannot tolerate staleness, keep that check synchronous and consistent.

Choreography or orchestration for an order process?

For a short, stable chain with a few independent reactions, choreography is simple and keeps coupling minimal, since each service reacts to the previous service's event. As soon as the process has branches, deadlines, compensation for failures or a need to answer "where is order X right now", orchestration is better because the flow is written down in one place and its state is observable. The orchestrator becomes a coupling point, so keep it free of business logic belonging to the participants; the details are in the sagas chapter.

What are common anti-patterns in event-driven systems?

Event soup is hundreds of poorly named events chained together so no one can follow a process. Events as RPC means publishing a request event and waiting for a reply event, which adds latency and complexity without decoupling. Huge events overload the broker and leak data, and are better handled with a claim check pointing to object storage. Missing ownership leaves schemas unmaintained and lets other teams depend on raw table-shaped CDC topics. I would also add assuming exactly-once delivery and skipping a replay path.

18. Recap

  • An event is a past-tense fact with many possible listeners; a command asks one handler to act and can be refused; a query returns data without changing anything.
  • "Event-driven" means three different things: notification (thin signal, callback), event-carried state transfer (fat event, local copies), and event sourcing (the log is the database).
  • Design events as long-lived public contracts: past-tense business names, one event per business fact, an envelope with unique id, version, aggregate id and correlation ids, an ordering key equal to the aggregate id, and additive-only evolution.
  • CQRS splits writing and reading models, joined by events and projections; it buys independent scaling and shape at the price of eventual consistency. Start light.
  • Event sourcing stores append-only events per aggregate, rebuilds state by replay, uses expected-version concurrency, snapshots for speed, upcasting for evolution, and needs a deliberate GDPR strategy such as keeping personal data out or crypto-shredding.
  • Log-based CDC (Debezium on the binlog or WAL) turns database changes into events with no app change; mind the initial snapshot, per-key ordering, schema changes, and replication slot hygiene. The outbox pattern with CDC solves the dual-write problem.
  • Keep search, cache and warehouse in sync through one source of truth and a change feed, with idempotent upserts, version guards, delete handling and a rebuild path.
  • Embrace eventual consistency in the UX with pending states, read-your-writes and push updates; test with replay, duplicates and reordering; observe lag and end-to-end freshness.
  • Avoid event soup, events as RPC, huge events and ownerless topics; pick the lightest pattern that fixes the actual problem.
