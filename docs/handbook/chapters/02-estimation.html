Estimation and Metrics

Before you draw a single box you need to know how big the problem is. This chapter teaches you to turn a vague product description into numbers (requests per second, bytes stored, bandwidth, servers), to read the metrics engineers use (percentiles, availability nines, durability), and to use Little's Law to reason about concurrency. It ends with full worked estimations you can imitate in an interview.

1. Why we estimate at all

A system design interview question usually sounds like "design a photo sharing app" or "design a chat service". Nothing in that sentence tells you whether the system must serve a thousand users or a billion. Yet the right design is completely different at those two sizes. At a thousand users, one small database server and one application server hosted anywhere will do, and anything more elaborate is wasted effort and money. At a billion users, a single database cannot even hold the data, a single server cannot even hold the open network connections, and every design choice (how you shard, how you cache, how you replicate) becomes essential. The size of the problem decides the shape of the solution, and estimation is how you find out the size.

In real engineering work, estimation also answers practical questions long before code exists. How many servers should we order? Will the network link between two data centers be saturated? Is it cheaper to keep this data in memory, on SSD, or in cheap archival storage? Back-of-the-envelope calculations, named after the idea of doing the arithmetic on the back of an envelope, are fast ways to answer these questions to within a factor of two or three. That precision is all you need. If the answer is "about 50,000 requests per second", it does not matter whether the truth is 40,000 or 60,000, because the design (a cluster of stateless servers behind a load balancer plus a sharded database) is the same either way. What matters is that you did not mistake 50,000 for 50 or for 5,000,000.

1.1 What estimation is good for

  • Choosing an architecture. If your peak write rate is 200 per second, a single relational database primary can take it. If it is 200,000 per second, you must partition the data (see Chapter 25) or use a store designed for high write throughput.
  • Finding the bottleneck. Computing storage, bandwidth, memory, and request rate side by side shows which resource will run out first. A photo service is usually limited by storage and bandwidth, while a chat service is usually limited by the number of open connections and by message fan-out.
  • Sizing and costing. Servers, disks, and network capacity cost money. Estimation tells you whether a design costs thousands of dollars a month or tens of millions.
  • Sanity checking. If a design needs a petabyte of RAM, the estimate has just told you that the design does not work, and you can find out in one minute instead of after months of building.
  • Showing judgment in an interview. Interviewers do not expect exact numbers. They watch whether you state assumptions out loud, use round numbers, keep units straight, and draw a conclusion from the result ("so we need to shard the metadata store but a single region is fine").
Key idea Estimation is not about being right to three digits. It is about getting the order of magnitude (the power of ten) right, writing down your assumptions so they can be challenged, and using the result to make a design decision. A number that does not change a decision was not worth computing.

1.2 The overall method

Almost every estimate follows the same shape. You start with a business-level number you can guess or ask about (daily active users), multiply by behavior per user (how many actions per day), convert the daily total into a per-second rate, adjust for the fact that traffic is not uniform (peak factor), and multiply by the size of each item to get storage and bandwidth. Then you divide by what one machine can do to learn how many machines you need. The diagram shows this pipeline. Every later section of this chapter is one of these steps explained in detail.

2. Powers of two, powers of ten, and unit conversions

Computers count in powers of two, because everything inside them is built from bits that are either 0 or 1. People count in powers of ten. Estimation mixes the two constantly, and the good news is that they are close enough that you can mostly ignore the difference. This section gives you the small table you should know by heart and the handful of conversions that appear in nearly every calculation.

2.1 The size ladder

A byte is 8 bits and holds one character of plain English text. Each step up the ladder multiplies by 1,024 (which is 210), but for estimation we round to 1,000, an error of only 2.4% at the first step and about 10% at the fifth step (petabytes). You can accept that error because your input assumptions are far less accurate than 10%.

The practical use is a quick mental trick: 1 million items of 1 KB each is 1 GB; 1 billion items of 1 KB is 1 TB; 1 billion items of 1 MB is 1 PB. Memorize those three and you can convert most storage questions in your head. The pattern is that the count and the size each contribute a power of ten, and you add the exponents: a billion (109) times a kilobyte (103) is 1012, which is a terabyte.

2.2 Time conversions

Rates are quoted per second but user behavior is described per day, so the conversion between them is the single most repeated step in estimation.

  • One day has 24 x 60 x 60 = 86,400 seconds. For quick work round it to 100,000 (105) seconds; the error is about 14%, and it makes the math trivial.
  • One year has about 31.5 million seconds, so round to 3 x 107 (or exactly 365 x 86,400 = 31,536,000).
  • One month is about 2.6 million seconds (30 days) and a week is 604,800 seconds.

Using these, one million requests per day is 1,000,000 / 86,400 = about 12 requests per second, and one billion per day is about 11,600 per second. A very handy rule of thumb follows: "X million per day" is roughly "12 times X per second", and "X billion per day" is about 11.6 thousand times X per second. If your boss says 200 million requests per day, you immediately say "about 2,300 per second".

2.3 Bits versus bytes, and decimal network units

Storage is measured in bytes (capital B), network links in bits per second (lowercase b). A byte is 8 bits, so a 1 Gbps (gigabit per second) link carries at most 125 MB/s of data in theory, and in practice a bit less because of protocol overhead. A 10 Gbps network card carries about 1.25 GB/s. When you compute bandwidth in bytes per second (which is natural, because payload sizes are in bytes) you must multiply by 8 to compare with link capacity in bits per second. Forgetting this factor of eight is the most common arithmetic slip in bandwidth estimates.

Interview tip Write the units next to every number as you calculate, and carry them through the multiplication ("100 million users x 2 KB = 200 GB"). Interviewers notice when a candidate quietly confuses MB with Mb or per day with per second, and writing units is the cheapest way to avoid the mistake.

3. Latency numbers every engineer should know

Latency is the time one operation takes from start to finish. The numbers below, popularized by a well-known list of "latency numbers every programmer should know", are not exact for any particular machine, and hardware keeps improving, but their ratios are stable and are what you need to internalize. Memory is roughly a thousand times faster than an SSD, which is roughly a hundred times faster than a spinning disk seek, which is roughly ten times faster than a trip across an ocean. When you decide whether to cache something in memory, store it on SSD, or fetch it from another region, those ratios drive the decision.

OperationTypical timeWhat to take from it
CPU cache (L1) referenceabout 1 nsEffectively free compared to everything below
Branch mispredict, L2 cache reference3 to 5 nsStill nanoseconds
Mutex lock or unlock (uncontended)about 20 nsLocks are cheap until they are contended
Main memory (RAM) referenceabout 100 nsThe speed of an in-memory cache or hash map lookup
Compress 1 KB with a fast algorithmabout 2 to 3 microsecondsCompression is cheap relative to the network
Send 1 KB over a 1 Gbps networkabout 10 microsecondsThe wire time for a small payload
Read 4 KB randomly from an SSDabout 20 to 150 microsecondsNVMe at the fast end, older SATA at the slow end

Round trip between regions on the same continentabout 30 to 70 msBounded by the speed of light in fiber
Round trip across oceans (for example US to Europe or Asia)about 100 to 250 msThe reason CDNs and regional deployments exist

3.1 What these numbers teach

  • Memory beats SSD beats disk, by orders of magnitude. That is why caches exist (Chapter 13) and why databases try to keep their working set in RAM. A cache hit measured in 100 ns to 1 ms (depending on whether the cache is in-process or across the network) versus a database read of several milliseconds is a large difference when multiplied by hundreds of thousands of requests per second.
  • Network round trips dominate. Within a data center a round trip is about half a millisecond, which is already 5,000 times a memory reference. A request that makes 20 sequential calls to other services pays at least 10 ms in network time alone, before any of those services does real work. This is why designers avoid long chains of synchronous calls and favor batching and parallelism.
  • Geography is not negotiable. The speed of light in fiber (about 200,000 km per second) puts a floor under cross-region latency. A user in Singapore talking to Virginia pays well over 150 ms per round trip, no matter how clever the code. The remedy is to put data nearby (see Chapter 12 and the multi-region discussion elsewhere in this handbook).
  • Sequential access is much cheaper than random access on spinning disks. Reading 1 MB sequentially costs about the same as one or two seeks. This is why log-structured and append-only designs (write to the end of a file, never update in place) are so common for high write rates.
  • Compression is almost always worth it for network transfer. Compressing 1 KB costs a few microseconds, while sending it costs 10 microseconds even on a fast local link, and far more across regions.
Watch out These are rules of thumb, not guarantees. Cloud networks have variable latency, SSDs slow down under heavy write load, and a "same data center" call through a busy load balancer and a service mesh can take several milliseconds, not 0.5. Use the numbers to compare options and to spot absurd designs, not to promise a specific response time.

4. From daily active users to requests per second

QPS stands for queries per second, and in system design it means requests per second hitting some component: the web tier, a database, a cache. (Some people say RPS, requests per second, or TPS, transactions per second; the idea is identical.) It is the central number because it tells you how much work arrives, and everything else (servers, database capacity, cache size) is sized from it.

4.1 Start from daily active users

The starting number is usually DAU, daily active users: how many distinct people use the product on a given day. It is a better base than total registered users, because most registered accounts are inactive, and it is better than monthly active users (MAU) because we want daily behavior. A rough relation is that DAU is somewhere between 10% and 60% of MAU, depending on how habit-forming the product is. A messaging app might have 50% or more of its monthly users active daily; a tax filing service has far less. If the interviewer gives you total users, ask or assume what fraction is active on a day, and say so.

Next you need actions per active user per day, for each kind of action. Think of the product as a list of operations. For a photo app these might be "open the feed" (read), "view a photo" (read), "upload a photo" (write), and "like" (write). Estimate each separately because reads and writes have different costs, different sizes, and different scaling strategies. The reason for separating them is explored in section 8.

4.2 Average QPS

Multiply DAU by actions per user per day to get total requests per day, then divide by 86,400 seconds. For example, 50 million DAU, each performing 20 requests per day, gives 1 billion requests per day, which is about 11,600 per second on average.

total per day = DAU x actions per user per day
              = 50,000,000 x 20 = 1,000,000,000

average QPS   = total per day / 86,400
              = 1,000,000,000 / 86,400  ~ 11,600

4.3 Peak versus average

The average is nearly useless for sizing, because users do not arrive evenly. A system that is idle at 4 a.m. local time may be flooded at 8 p.m. Servers must be sized for the busy periods, or the system fails exactly when the most people are using it. The peak factor (also called peak-to-average ratio) is how many times the busiest moment exceeds the daily average.

  • For a consumer product concentrated in a few time zones, peak is typically 2 to 3 times the daily average. Usage rises through the evening and falls overnight. A product spread across the whole world has a flatter curve (peak maybe 1.5 to 2 times), because someone is always awake.
  • For products with sharp events, such as ticket sales, flash sales, live sports scores, breaking news, or a game launch, the short-lived peak can be 10 times to 100 times the average. These are the cases where you design explicitly for bursts (queues, rate limiting, pre-scaling).
  • Business software has a "9 to 5" shape: heavy during working hours on weekdays, nearly nothing at night and weekends. The peak-to-average ratio over a whole week can be 3 to 5 times even though the peak-to-business-hours-average ratio is low.

In an interview a good default is peak = 2 to 3 times average, stated out loud, with a note on what changes if the traffic is bursty. Continuing the example: 11,600 average QPS times a peak factor of 3 is about 35,000 peak QPS.

Watch out Do not size a system for the average and then add "some buffer". Size for the peak, then add headroom on top of the peak (section 9). The two adjustments (peak factor and headroom) are separate: one accounts for the shape of demand, the other for failures, deployments, and forecasting error.

5. Storage estimation

Storage estimation answers "how much disk will we need, and how fast does it grow?" It matters because storage has to be bought and provisioned in advance, because the amount of data determines whether you need to shard, and because the cost of storing data forever is easy to underestimate.

5.1 The recipe

  1. Count the objects created per day. Use the write side of your request estimate: new photos, new messages, new rows. Reads create no new storage.
  2. Estimate the size of one object. Include the payload and the metadata around it: IDs, timestamps, user references, indexes. A chat message is 100 bytes of text but its stored row with sender, conversation, timestamp, and message id may be 200 to 300 bytes.
  3. Multiply to get bytes per day, then by 365 for a year, and by the number of years you retain data (for example 5 years).
  4. Multiply by the replication factor. Data is stored in several copies for durability and availability. Three copies is common, so raw storage is three times the logical data. Erasure coding (storing coded fragments instead of full copies) can bring the overhead down to about 1.3 to 1.5 times, which is how large object stores keep cost down.
  5. Add overhead for indexes and growth. Database indexes, free-space reserve (disks should not run above roughly 70 to 80% full), and backups can add another 30 to 100%. Say it and use a round factor.

5.2 An example

Suppose 10 million new photos are uploaded per day, and each photo is stored in a few resolutions totaling 2.5 MB. Per day: 10,000,000 x 2.5 MB = 25 TB. Per year: 25 TB x 365 = about 9.1 PB of logical data. With three-way replication, that is about 27 PB of raw disk each year. That one result already tells you that this is a multi-year data-center scale problem, that the images must go to a distributed object store rather than a database, and that cost optimization (erasure coding, moving old photos to cheaper storage) is part of the design.

Always separate blobs (large unstructured objects like photos and videos) from metadata (small structured records about them). Blobs dominate bytes; metadata dominates query traffic. In the example, metadata at 500 bytes per photo is only 10,000,000 x 500 B = 5 GB per day, or about 1.8 TB per year, which fits comfortably in a sharded database. Blobs and metadata live in different systems with different scaling needs.

5.3 Hot, warm and cold data

Not all data is accessed equally. Recent data is read often, and old data is rarely touched. A tiered design keeps hot data (the last days) on fast SSD, warm data on cheaper disks, and cold data in archival storage that costs a fraction as much but is slow or costly to retrieve. In estimation, say how much of the total is hot. If only 5% of photos are viewed after the first month, then the fast tier needs to hold perhaps one month of uploads, not the whole archive.

6. Bandwidth estimation

Bandwidth is how many bytes per second a link or a service moves. There are two directions to estimate, because they differ: ingress (data coming into your system, mostly writes and uploads) and egress (data going out, mostly reads and downloads). Egress is almost always larger because content is read many more times than it is written, and egress is also what cloud providers usually charge for.

bandwidth (bytes/s) = QPS x average payload size (bytes)
bandwidth (bits/s)  = bytes/s x 8

Use peak QPS to size the links and the load balancers, since the network must carry the busiest moment. Use average QPS to estimate monthly transfer volume and cost.

Example: 46,000 average photo views per second at 200 KB each is 46,000 x 200 KB = 9.2 GB/s, which is about 74 Gbps on average, and 222 Gbps at a peak factor of 3. A single server with a 10 Gbps card cannot come close, so this traffic must be spread across many servers, and most of it must come from a CDN (Chapter 12) rather than from your origin. If the CDN serves 90% of the views, the origin only needs to sustain about 22 Gbps at peak, a manageable number.

Interview tip State both a bandwidth result and the decision it supports: "That is 222 Gbps at peak, far above one data center edge link, so images must be offloaded to a CDN with a high hit ratio." The sentence after the number is where the marks are.

6.1 Do not forget the small stuff

Payload size is not the whole story. Each HTTP request carries headers (commonly 500 bytes to 2 KB with cookies), TLS and TCP add overhead, and responses are often compressed (text compresses to a quarter of its size or better, while JPEG and video are already compressed and do not shrink further). For text-heavy APIs with small payloads, header overhead can be a significant share. For large media, it is negligible. Mention it when payloads are small.

7. Memory and cache sizing

Memory is fast and expensive, so you spend it where it saves the most. The usual question is "how big does the cache have to be?" The method is based on the observation (the 80/20 rule, a rough description of skewed access patterns, formally related to Zipf distributions) that a small fraction of items receives most of the traffic. If 20% of items get about 80% of the reads, a cache that holds only that 20% can absorb most requests.

7.1 The recipe

  1. Estimate the number of distinct items read in a day (for example, the number of distinct photos or profiles requested).
  2. Estimate the size of the cached form of each item (the serialized object, which may be smaller than the stored form, since you cache only what you serve).
  3. Decide what fraction to cache. Starting with 20% of the daily distinct items is the classic assumption.
  4. Multiply: cache size = distinct items per day x item size x fraction cached. Add 20 to 30% overhead for hash table structure, keys, and fragmentation.

Example: 500 million distinct user profiles are read per day, each 1 KB when cached. Caching 20% means 100 million profiles x 1 KB = 100 GB. A single large in-memory cache server may hold 64 to 256 GB, but you would typically shard the cache across several nodes both for capacity and for throughput, and run replicas for availability. So perhaps 3 to 6 cache nodes cover it. Cache nodes are cheap compared to the database servers they protect: one in-memory node can serve on the order of 100,000 or more simple lookups per second, versus a few thousand to tens of thousands for a database node.

7.2 Hit ratio matters more than size

The benefit of a cache is determined by its hit ratio: the fraction of requests served from it. If the hit ratio is 90%, the database sees only 10% of the traffic. Going from 90% to 99% cuts the database load by another factor of ten (from 10% to 1% of requests), which is why small improvements in hit ratio at the high end are valuable. If your 35,000 peak QPS read workload has a 90% cache hit ratio, the database sees only 3,500 QPS, which a single well-provisioned primary with replicas can handle. Cache design, including eviction policies and invalidation, is the subject of Chapter 13.

Memory is also used for things other than caches: per-connection state in a chat gateway (each open WebSocket might take 10 KB or more of memory for buffers and session data), in-process data structures, and the operating system's file cache. When estimating connection-heavy systems, multiply concurrent connections by memory per connection.

8. The read to write ratio

The read:write ratio compares how many reads the system serves for every write. It is one of the most design-shaping numbers, because reads and writes scale in completely different ways.

  • Reads scale by copying. You can answer reads from caches, read replicas, and CDNs, which all hold copies of data. Adding more copies adds more read capacity cheaply, with the cost being slightly stale data.
  • Writes scale by splitting. Every write must reach the authoritative copy of the data, so adding copies does not help (it makes it worse, since each copy must be updated). To scale writes you must partition (shard) the data so different writers handle different pieces, or use a store built for high write throughput.
SystemTypical read:write ratioConsequence
Social feed, photo viewing100:1 to 1000:1Cache aggressively, use CDN and read replicas; precompute feeds
URL shortenerabout 100:1Cache the hot mappings; the write path is tiny
Chat messagingclose to 1:1 (each message is written once and read once or a few times)Write path matters as much as read; storage layer must handle heavy writes
Metrics and loggingwrite-heavy, 1:0.01 or lessUse append-only, batched ingestion; reads are rare and can be slower
E-commerce catalogabout 1000:1 for browsing, but checkout is write-criticalDifferent paths need different treatment

In an interview, state the ratio as an assumption ("this is read-heavy, around 100 to 1"), compute read QPS and write QPS separately, and let the ratio decide where to invest: caches and replicas for read-heavy systems, partitioning and batching for write-heavy ones. Note that the ratio sometimes hides a fan-out: one write of a post by a celebrity can become millions of timeline reads or, in a push model, millions of timeline writes. Estimate after taking the fan-out into account.

9. Servers needed and capacity headroom

With peak QPS in hand, the number of servers is peak QPS divided by the QPS one server can sustain, rounded up, with a safety margin. The hard part is knowing what one server can do. That depends entirely on what the request does.

9.1 How much can one server handle?

Typical orders of magnitude, from which you should pick a defensible number and state it:

  • A stateless application server running business logic that calls a database and a cache: roughly 500 to 5,000 requests per second per server, depending on how much CPU each request needs and how long each waits for downstream calls. If each request needs 5 ms of CPU on one core, a 16-core machine can do about 16 / 0.005 = 3,200 per second at 100% CPU.
  • A server returning small cached or static responses with an efficient event-driven design: 10,000 to 100,000+ per second.
  • A relational database node: roughly a few thousand to ten-thousand-plus simple queries per second, much less for complex joins; write rates are lower than read rates.
  • An in-memory cache node: 100,000 or more simple operations per second.
  • A WebSocket gateway: limited not by request rate but by concurrent connections, typically 50,000 to a few hundred thousand per server when tuned, bounded by memory and file descriptors.

When the interviewer does not provide a figure, say "I will assume one app server handles about 1,000 requests per second, we can refine this with a load test." Showing that you know it must be measured is as valuable as the number.

9.2 Headroom

Headroom (also called capacity buffer or safety margin) is spare capacity above the expected peak. There are several reasons you need it, and each contributes:

  • You never run a server at 100% utilization. As utilization approaches 100%, queues build and latency climbs steeply (section 14). A common target is to keep average CPU at 50 to 70% at peak.
  • Failures. If you have three availability zones and one fails, the other two must absorb its traffic. To survive the loss of one of three zones, each must normally run at most about 66% of its capacity at peak, since the surviving two carry 1.5 times the per-zone load.
  • Deployments. Rolling out a new version takes some servers out of rotation at a time.
  • Forecast error and growth. Your DAU number is a guess; traffic grows between purchase decisions; a marketing campaign may double traffic.

A simple rule is to provision 1.5 to 2 times the peak requirement, then adjust for specifics such as how many zones you span.

9.3 Worked sizing

Peak QPS at the application tier is 139,000 (the photo example below). Assume one server sustains 2,000 requests per second at full CPU and that we target 60% utilization at peak.

usable per server   = 2,000 x 0.6        = 1,200 req/s
servers for peak    = 139,000 / 1,200    ~ 116
survive 1 of 3 zones: needs 3/2 as many   ~ 174
round and add deploy slack                ~ 180 servers, 60 per zone

The same arithmetic applies to any tier. The result (180 stateless servers) is a very comfortable size for a load balancer and an autoscaling group, which tells you that the application tier is not the hard part of this problem. The hard parts are the storage and the bandwidth.

Key idea Servers needed = (peak QPS / per-server capacity) / target utilization, then inflated for failure domains (multiply by N/(N-1) when you have N zones and must survive losing one). Peak factor, utilization target, and failure headroom are three separate multipliers; name each one when you use it.

10. Percentiles and tail latency: why averages mislead

When someone says "our API responds in 120 ms", which 120 ms do they mean? Latency is not one number; every request has its own latency, and the collection of them forms a distribution. Summarizing a distribution with a single average hides the part that hurts users most.

10.1 What a percentile is

The p50 (50th percentile, the median) is the latency that half of requests beat. The p95 is the latency that 95% of requests beat, meaning 1 in 20 requests is slower. The p99 is beaten by 99% of requests, so 1 in 100 is slower, and p99.9 means 1 in 1,000 is slower. To compute a percentile, sort all measured latencies from fastest to slowest and take the value at that position: with 1,000 samples, the p99 is the 990th value.

10.2 Why the average misleads

Imagine 99 requests that take 100 ms and one request that takes 10 seconds (a timeout after retries, a garbage collection pause, a lock held too long). The average is (99 x 100 ms + 10,000 ms) / 100 = 199 ms, which describes nobody's actual experience: 99 users saw 100 ms and one saw ten seconds. The median says 100 ms and hides the problem entirely, while the p99 exposes it. Averages are also easily moved by a handful of outliers and tell you nothing about how bad the worst cases are. A service can have a fine average and a miserable p99.

Use percentiles for two different purposes. The median (p50) tells you the typical experience. The high percentiles (p95, p99, p99.9) tell you how bad it gets and are what you write into SLOs (service level objectives, internal targets such as "99% of requests complete in under 300 ms") and into SLAs (contractual promises, with penalties).

10.3 Why the tail matters more than it seems

You might think "only 1% of requests are slow, who cares". Two effects make the tail important.

  1. Users make many requests. If loading one page makes 50 requests and each has a 1% chance of being slow, the chance that at least one is slow is 1 - 0.9950, about 39.5%. Four in ten page loads feel slow, even though only 1% of individual requests are slow. Heavy users, who make the most requests, hit the tail most often and are often your most valuable users.
  2. Fan-out amplifies the tail. Suppose a request calls 100 backend servers in parallel and must wait for all of them (a search that queries 100 shards). If each backend is slow 1% of the time, the chance that at least one is slow is 1 - 0.99100, about 63%. The slowest of the 100 sets the overall latency, so the overall p50 is roughly the single-server p99. This effect is called tail latency amplification, and it is why large systems obsess about p99 and p99.9 of each component, not the median.

10.4 Taming the tail

  • Timeouts and retries with budgets. Cap how long you wait, and retry on another replica, but limit retries so they do not create a storm.
  • Hedged requests. Send a duplicate request to a second replica if the first has not answered after, say, the p95 latency, and use whichever answers first. This trades a small amount of extra load (about 5%) for a large cut in tail latency.
  • Remove sources of pauses. Garbage collection tuning, avoiding noisy neighbors, keeping a cache warm after deployments, and smoothing background jobs.
  • Keep utilization moderate. Queueing delay dominates the tail at high utilization (section 14).
Watch out You cannot average percentiles. If one server reports a p99 of 200 ms and another a p99 of 400 ms, the fleet-wide p99 is not 300 ms. Percentiles must be computed from merged raw samples or from mergeable histograms. Monitoring systems that store only per-minute averages of p99 give misleading dashboards.

11. Availability and the nines

Availability is the fraction of time a system is able to do its job. It is quoted as a percentage and conversationally as "nines": 99.9% is "three nines", 99.99% is "four nines". Each extra nine means ten times less allowed downtime, and, importantly, roughly ten times more engineering effort and cost.

Look at what the table says about effort. At 99.9% you have almost 9 hours a year of slack: a single bad deployment or a slow manual recovery fits within it. At 99.99% you have under an hour per year, which is less than the time it takes a human to be paged, log in, diagnose, and fix. So four nines requires automated failure detection and recovery (health checks, automatic failover, automated rollbacks), redundancy in every layer, and careful release practices. Five nines (five minutes a year) is achieved only by systems where every failure is handled automatically with no human in the loop, and it is very expensive. Many consumer products target 99.9% to 99.99%; core infrastructure such as DNS, payment authorization, or cloud storage targets higher.

11.1 Combining components: serial and parallel

A request usually passes through several components, and the availability of the whole depends on how they are connected.

  • In series (all must work): availabilities multiply. Three components of 99.9% each give 0.999 x 0.999 x 0.999 = 99.7%, which is about 26 hours of downtime a year. Every dependency you add in the critical path lowers the total, which is why "just call one more service" has a cost, and why you should make non-essential dependencies optional (degrade gracefully when they fail).
  • In parallel (redundant, any one suffices): unavailabilities multiply. Two independent 99.9% servers, either of which can serve the request, fail together with probability 0.001 x 0.001 = 0.000001, so the pair is 99.9999% available. This is the mathematical argument for redundancy and replication.
Watch out The parallel formula assumes failures are independent. Two replicas in the same rack, on the same power feed, running the same buggy release, fail together, so the real availability is far lower than 99.9999%. This is why we spread replicas across availability zones and regions, and why shared dependencies (the load balancer, the DNS provider, a configuration service) cap the whole system's availability no matter how many replicas sit behind them.

11.2 Availability measured as requests, and SLOs with error budgets

For services it is more useful to measure availability by requests than by clock time: the fraction of requests that succeed (for example, return a non-5xx response within the timeout). A service that fails 0.1% of all requests spread evenly across the day has 99.9% request availability even though it was never completely down. An error budget is the allowed unreliability implied by the SLO: at 99.9%, you may fail 1 request in 1,000, or be down for 43 minutes a month. Teams spend the budget on risky launches; when it is exhausted, they slow down and invest in reliability. This turns "how reliable should we be" from an argument into a measured trade-off.

12. Durability

Durability is the probability that data, once successfully stored, is not lost or corrupted. It is a different promise from availability. Availability asks "can I read my data right now?" Durability asks "will my data still exist next year?" A system can be temporarily unavailable (an outage) yet fully durable (the data is safe on disks and will be back when the outage ends). The opposite, available but not durable, is worse: a cache that always answers but loses everything when restarted.

Durability is also quoted in nines, but of a different quantity: the yearly probability of losing a given object. Large object stores advertise eleven nines (99.999999999%) of annual durability. That means that if you store 10 million objects, you can expect to lose, on average, one object every 10,000 years: 10,000,000 x 10-11 = 0.0001 objects per year. The figure is a design target, backed by modeling, not a guarantee that no loss can ever occur, and it does not protect against your own mistakes such as deleting objects or overwriting them with corrupted data.

12.1 How durability is achieved

  • Replication. Keep several full copies on different disks, machines, racks, and availability zones. A disk fails; the data survives on the others, and the system re-creates the missing copy. Durability depends on how fast the repair happens: data is lost only if all copies fail before repair completes.
  • Erasure coding. Split an object into, for example, 10 data fragments and compute 4 parity fragments, so any 10 of the 14 reconstruct the object. It tolerates the loss of any 4 fragments with only 1.4 times storage overhead, versus 3 times for triple replication. The costs are more CPU and more network traffic on reads and repairs, so it is used for large, cold objects.
  • Write acknowledgment rules. A write is "durable" only after it reaches stable storage on enough replicas (for example, a quorum), not when it reaches memory on one machine. A database that acknowledges writes before flushing them to disk (relaxed fsync) can lose recent writes in a crash. Replication strategies are covered in Chapter 24.
  • Checksums and scrubbing. Disks silently corrupt data (bit rot). Storing checksums and periodically reading and verifying all data detects corruption, and a good copy is used to repair it.
  • Backups and versioning. Replication copies mistakes immediately: if a bug deletes a row, all replicas delete it. Only point-in-time backups, snapshots, versioned objects, and delayed replicas protect against logical errors and malicious deletion. Backups must be tested by actually restoring.
  • Geographic redundancy. Copies in another region protect against the loss of an entire site.

The right durability level depends on the data. Payment records and user-uploaded photos need the highest durability. A cache, a derived search index, or a thumbnail can be rebuilt from the source of truth, so losing it costs only time. Say explicitly which data is the source of truth (must be durable) and which is derived (can be recomputed), because it determines where you spend your replication budget.

13. Little's Law: how many requests are in flight?

Little's Law is a simple and surprisingly powerful relationship from queueing theory. For any stable system, in the long run:

L = lambda x W

L      = average number of items in the system (concurrency, "in flight")
lambda = average arrival rate (items per second, e.g. requests per second)
W      = average time each item spends in the system (seconds)

The law needs no assumptions about the shape of arrivals or service times; it holds as long as the system is stable (what goes in eventually comes out, so the queue does not grow forever), and it applies to a whole system or to any part of it. Think of a coffee shop: if 2 customers arrive per minute and each stays 10 minutes, then on average 2 x 10 = 20 customers are sitting inside. If you know any two of the three quantities you can compute the third.

13.1 Worked examples

Example 1: threads per server

A service receives 5,000 requests per second and each request takes 200 ms (mostly waiting for a database). Concurrency L = 5,000 x 0.2 = 1,000 requests in flight. If each in-flight request occupies a thread in a thread-per-request server, and one server is configured with 200 threads, you need at least 1,000 / 200 = 5 servers just to hold the concurrency, regardless of CPU. Now a downstream slowdown raises W to 1 second. Without any change in arrival rate, L = 5,000 x 1 = 5,000 requests in flight, five times more. Thread pools fill up, new requests queue, and W grows even more. This is the mechanism of a cascading failure: latency growth increases concurrency, which exhausts resources, which increases latency further. It is why timeouts and load shedding matter.

Example 2: database connection pool

The application issues 2,000 database queries per second, each taking 5 ms. L = 2,000 x 0.005 = 10 connections busy on average. A pool of 10 is therefore exactly the average, and bursts will queue; a pool of 20 to 30 gives comfortable headroom. A pool of 1,000 would be wasteful and harmful, since databases perform worse when too many queries run at once. This is also the reason the "how many connections should the pool have" answer is surprisingly small.

Example 3: chat connections

If 50 million users open the app each day and each stays connected for an average of 30 minutes (1,800 s), the arrival rate of sessions is 50,000,000 / 86,400 = about 580 sessions per second, so L = 580 x 1,800 = about 1.04 million concurrent connections on average. That agrees with a direct calculation: 50 million users x 30 minutes / 1,440 minutes per day = 1.04 million. Little's Law gives the same answer from a different direction, which is a good cross-check. (The peak concurrency will be several times higher.)

Example 4: maximum throughput from a fixed limit

Rearranged, the law says throughput = concurrency / latency. A client with 50 concurrent connections to a service that takes 100 ms per call can sustain at most 50 / 0.1 = 500 requests per second. If it needs 5,000, it must either cut latency to 10 ms or raise concurrency to 500. That is the whole reasoning behind connection pools, pipelining, and parallel uploads.

Interview tip Little's Law is the quickest way to size anything that holds a resource per request: threads, sockets, database connections, in-memory sessions, or a queue. Say "arrival rate times time in system gives the concurrency" and then do the multiplication. It also gives you a way to check a claim: if someone says a server handles 10,000 requests per second with 500 ms latency, then it holds 5,000 requests at once, which tells you whether that is plausible.

14. Throughput versus latency, and why utilization matters

Throughput is how much work a system completes per unit of time (requests per second, megabytes per second). Latency is how long one piece of work takes. They are related but not the same, and optimizing one can hurt the other. A useful picture is a highway: latency is how long one car takes to drive from A to B; throughput is how many cars per hour pass a point. Widening the road (more lanes) raises throughput without changing the time for a single car. A lower speed limit raises latency without necessarily changing throughput. Making a convoy of cars wait for each other raises throughput per fuel burned (batching) but delays the first car.

14.1 Classic trade-offs

  • Batching. Grouping 100 writes into one disk or network operation greatly increases throughput, because the fixed cost per operation (a network round trip, a disk flush) is shared. But each write waits for the batch to fill, adding latency. Kafka producers, database group commit, and Nagle-like buffering all work this way.
  • Parallelism. More concurrent workers raise throughput until a shared resource saturates; they do not make any single request faster.
  • Caching. Improves both, by serving most requests from fast memory.
  • Pipelining and asynchronous work. Do not wait for the result of one operation before starting the next; throughput rises toward the capacity of the slowest stage, which becomes the bottleneck.

14.2 The utilization knee

As a server approaches full utilization, the time requests spend waiting in the queue grows sharply. For the simplest queueing model (random arrivals, random service times, one server), the average response time is approximately S / (1 - utilization), where S is the service time with no waiting. At 50% utilization the response time is 2 S, at 80% it is 5 S, at 90% it is 10 S, and at 95% it is 20 S. Real systems with many servers behave less severely but the shape is the same: a flat region, a knee, and then a wall. This is the quantitative reason for headroom in section 9: you pay a small price in idle capacity to avoid a large price in latency, especially at the tail.

16. Worked estimation: a social photo-sharing app

Here is a full estimation as you would present it in an interview. The product is a photo-sharing app: users upload photos, follow other users, and scroll a feed of photos. We state assumptions first, then calculate, and then draw conclusions.

16.1 Assumptions

  • 100 million daily active users (DAU).
  • 10% of them upload one photo per day: 10 million uploads per day.
  • Each user views 40 photos per day in their feed: 4 billion views per day.
  • An uploaded original is about 2 MB on average. We also store a few resized versions (thumbnail, feed size, full screen) totaling about 0.5 MB, so about 2.5 MB per photo in storage.
  • A view downloads a feed-size image of about 200 KB.
  • Metadata per photo (ID, owner, caption, timestamp, locations of the files) is about 500 bytes.
  • Peak factor of 3. Replication of 3 for blobs. Photos are retained indefinitely.

16.2 Request rates

Upload QPS (average) = 10,000,000 / 86,400   ~ 116  -> call it 120
Upload QPS (peak)    = 120 x 3                ~ 350

View QPS (average)   = 4,000,000,000 / 86,400 ~ 46,300
View QPS (peak)      = 46,300 x 3             ~ 139,000

Read : write ratio   = 4,000,000,000 / 10,000,000 = 400 : 1

The ratio of 400 to 1 confirms this is overwhelmingly read-heavy. Uploads at 350 per second at peak are a small number, trivial for the application tier; the heavy part is delivering views.

16.3 Storage

Blob storage per day  = 10,000,000 x 2.5 MB   = 25 TB per day
Blob storage per year = 25 TB x 365            ~ 9.1 PB (logical)
With 3 x replication                           ~ 27 PB per year
With erasure coding (~1.5 x)                   ~ 13.7 PB per year

Metadata per day      = 10,000,000 x 500 B     = 5 GB per day
Metadata per year     ~ 1.8 TB (before replication and indexes)

The blobs need a distributed object store, with a tiering plan (old photos migrate to cheaper storage), and erasure coding would halve the raw disk requirement compared with triple replication. Metadata, at under 2 TB per year, fits in a modest sharded database, although its read rate is high (every view needs a lookup), so it is cached.

16.4 Bandwidth

Upload (ingress) at peak   = 350 / s x 2 MB     ~ 700 MB/s   ~ 5.6 Gbps
Views (egress) average     = 46,300 x 200 KB    ~ 9.3 GB/s   ~ 74 Gbps
Views (egress) at peak     = 139,000 x 200 KB   ~ 28 GB/s    ~ 222 Gbps
If a CDN serves 90% of views, origin egress at peak ~ 22 Gbps

Ingress is modest. Egress is enormous, and clearly impossible without a CDN. With a 90% CDN hit ratio the origin needs about 22 Gbps at peak, roughly two to four 10 Gbps links with room to spare, which is practical. A conclusion worth stating: the CDN hit ratio is the most important performance parameter in this system. If it fell to 50%, origin egress would be 111 Gbps.

16.5 Memory and servers

For caching metadata and feed data, assume 20% of the 4 billion daily views concentrate on hot photos, so we might cache metadata for the most recent and popular items. If we cache 200 million photo-metadata records at 500 bytes, that is 100 GB, spread over a handful of cache nodes. For the application tier, 139,000 peak QPS at 1,200 effective requests per second per server is about 116 servers, and about 180 once we add headroom to survive the loss of one zone (computed in section 9.3). Only the metadata lookups hit the application tier; the image bytes themselves come directly from the CDN.

16.6 What the numbers tell us

  • Use a CDN with a high hit ratio; plan for cache warming and for the long tail of rarely viewed photos that miss.
  • Store photos in a distributed object store with erasure coding and cold tiering; this is the largest cost.
  • Keep the application tier stateless and horizontally scaled; around 180 servers is not a hard problem.
  • Cache metadata; shard the metadata database by photo or user ID (see Chapter 25).
  • Upload processing (resizing into thumbnails) should be asynchronous through a queue, since 350 uploads per second at peak, each needing a few hundred milliseconds of CPU, is easily parallelizable by a pool of workers.

17. Worked estimation: a chat service

A chat service stresses different resources than a photo app. Messages are tiny, so storage and bandwidth are modest, but the system must hold a huge number of long-lived connections and deliver every message to each recipient in near real time.

17.1 Assumptions

  • 50 million DAU.
  • Each user sends 40 messages per day: 2 billion messages per day.
  • The average message is 100 bytes of text; stored with metadata (message ID, sender, conversation ID, timestamp, flags) it is about 200 bytes.
  • Most conversations are one-to-one, with some groups; assume an average of 2 recipients per message, so 4 billion deliveries per day.
  • At the busiest moment, 20% of DAU have the app connected at the same time: 10 million concurrent connections.
  • Peak factor of 3. Messages stored for 5 years with 3 replicas.

17.2 Rates and storage

Messages sent (average) = 2,000,000,000 / 86,400   ~ 23,000 per second
Messages sent (peak)    = 23,000 x 3                ~ 70,000 per second
Deliveries (peak)       = 70,000 x 2                ~ 140,000 per second

Storage per day  = 2,000,000,000 x 200 B   = 400 GB per day
Storage per year = 400 GB x 365            ~ 146 TB
5 years x 3 replicas = 146 TB x 5 x 3      ~ 2.2 PB

Writes of 70,000 messages per second at peak are beyond one relational primary, but easily handled by a partitioned, write-optimized store (partitioned by conversation ID, so that one conversation's messages sit together and can be read in order). 2.2 PB over five years is large but unremarkable for a distributed database. Read:write is near 1:1 on the live path, though history loads add reads.

17.3 Connections and memory

Concurrent connections at peak    = 10,000,000
Memory per connection (buffers, session) ~ 10 KB
Total connection memory           = 10,000,000 x 10 KB = 100 GB
Connections per gateway server    ~ 50,000 (conservative, tuned)
Gateway servers needed            = 10,000,000 / 50,000 = 200
With headroom (x 1.5)             ~ 300 gateway servers

The chat tier is limited by connections, not by CPU. Using Little's Law as a cross-check: if sessions arrive at about 580 per second and last 30 minutes on average, L is about 1 million connections on average, and 10 million at the peak is reasonable with the evening concentration. The connection gateway is stateful (each user is attached to one specific server), so the system also needs a presence and routing service that knows which gateway holds each user, so a message can be forwarded to the right server.

17.4 Bandwidth

Peak deliveries 140,000 / s x ~300 B on the wire (with framing and headers)
  = about 42 MB/s = 340 Mbps

This is tiny compared with the photo app. Chat is a latency and connection-count problem, not a bandwidth problem, until you add media attachments, which can be handled the way the photo app handles photos (upload to an object store, send only a link in the message).

17.5 What the numbers tell us

  • Dimension the gateway tier by connections per server and by memory per connection, not by requests per second.
  • Partition message storage by conversation and use a write-optimized store; 70,000 writes per second at peak rules out a single database node.
  • Fan-out is the multiplier: a group message with 500 members produces 500 deliveries, so group size limits matter and large groups need a different (pull-based) delivery path.
  • Bandwidth is low, so cost is dominated by the number of servers holding connections and by the storage over time.

18. How to use estimation in an interview

The calculations are simple; what separates a strong candidate is the way they are used. These habits come from watching what interviewers reward.

  1. Ask or state the inputs first. "I will assume 100 million DAU and 10% uploaders; tell me if you would like different numbers." This invites correction and shows the assumptions are explicit.
  2. Round aggressively. Use 100,000 seconds per day, 1,000 instead of 1,024. A calculation that needs a calculator is too precise to be useful.
  3. Separate reads from writes, and average from peak. Always produce both and say which one you size for.
  4. Estimate the dominant resource, not all of them. Photo app: storage and bandwidth. Chat: connections. Search: memory for indexes. Do the others briefly or skip them, and say why.
  5. Finish each estimate with a design consequence. "That is 140,000 peak QPS, so a single database cannot serve reads directly, and I will add a cache and read replicas."
  6. Sanity check against known sizes. If you find that a service needs 40 PB of RAM, you have probably dropped a factor. Compare against known machines: a server has 64 to 512 GB of RAM, tens of TB of disk, a 10 to 100 Gbps network card.
  7. Keep it short. Estimation should take about five minutes of a 45-minute interview. See Chapter 1 for where it fits in the overall time budget.

18.1 Common mistakes

  • Sizing for average load instead of peak.
  • Mixing bits and bytes, or per day and per second.
  • Forgetting replication, so storage is a third of reality.
  • Forgetting that reads dominate in feed-style systems and writes in logging-style systems.
  • Calculating precisely and then not using the result.
  • Quoting an average latency when the requirement is a tail percentile.
  • Treating availability numbers of independent components as if they were not multiplied together.

19. Interview questions and answers

Why do we use peak QPS rather than average QPS when sizing a system?

Users do not arrive evenly across the day. If you provision for the average, then during the busy hours the system would be overloaded, latency would climb, and requests would fail just when the most users are present. Peak QPS is the average multiplied by a peak factor (2 to 3 for a typical single-region consumer app, much more for event-driven traffic). Beyond the peak you also add headroom for failures, deployments, and forecast error. Average QPS is still useful for cost estimates, such as monthly data transfer.

You are told a service has 100 million DAU and each user makes 10 requests a day. How do you get QPS quickly?

Total requests per day are 100 million x 10 = 1 billion. Dividing by 86,400 seconds, which I round to about 100,000 for speed, gives roughly 10,000 requests per second, or more precisely about 11,600. I would then multiply by a peak factor of around 3 to get roughly 35,000 peak QPS and say that the figure supports a horizontally scaled stateless tier with a cache in front of the data store. I would also split it into reads and writes using an assumed ratio, since the two scale differently.

Why is the p99 latency more informative than the average, and how does fan-out make it worse?

The average is dominated by the typical fast requests and hides the slow outliers, so a service with a fine average can still make some users wait for seconds. The p99 tells you how slow the slowest one percent is, which is what users with bad experiences actually see and what SLOs are written against. Fan-out worsens it because a request that waits for many parallel sub-requests is as slow as the slowest of them: with 100 backends each slow 1% of the time, about 63% of requests encounter at least one slow backend. So the end-to-end median approaches the component p99, and you must keep component tail latency very low, using hedged requests, timeouts, and moderate utilization.

What does "four nines" availability allow in downtime, and what does it imply about how you must operate?

99.99% allows about 52.6 minutes of downtime a year, or roughly 4.3 minutes a month. That is shorter than a typical human response cycle of being paged, investigating, and fixing, so recovery must be automated: health checks, automatic failover, automated rollbacks, redundancy across zones, and gradual rollouts that limit blast radius. It also means that each dependency in the critical path must be at least as available as the target, because serial availabilities multiply. Practically I would remove single points of failure, make non-critical dependencies degrade gracefully, and define an error budget.

Three services in series each have 99.9% availability. What is the combined availability, and how can you improve it?

Because all three must work, availabilities multiply: 0.999 cubed is about 0.997, so 99.7%, or around 26 hours of downtime per year, worse than any component. To improve it, add redundancy to each stage so that two independent instances in parallel give 1 - (0.001)2 = 99.9999% per stage, and make sure instances sit in different failure domains so their failures really are independent. You can also shorten the chain, make some calls asynchronous, or make non-essential dependencies optional with fallbacks so that their failure does not fail the request.

What is the difference between availability and durability, and how do you achieve high durability?

Availability is whether the system can serve requests right now; durability is whether stored data survives over time without loss or corruption. A system can be down but durable, or up but losing data. High durability is achieved by writing to multiple independent devices before acknowledging, using replication or erasure coding across racks and zones, detecting and repairing silent corruption with checksums and scrubbing, and maintaining backups, snapshots, or versioning to protect against human error and bugs, since replication faithfully copies mistakes. Eleven nines of durability means roughly one object lost in 10,000 years for 10 million objects.

Explain Little's Law and give an example of using it to size a system.

Little's Law says that the average number of items in a stable system equals the arrival rate multiplied by the average time each item spends in the system: L = lambda x W. If a service receives 5,000 requests per second and each takes 200 ms, then 1,000 requests are in flight on average, so with 200 threads per server I need at least 5 servers just to hold the concurrency. It is also useful in reverse: throughput equals concurrency divided by latency, so a client with 50 connections and 100 ms calls cannot exceed 500 requests per second. And it explains cascading failure, because if W rises, concurrency rises proportionally and may exhaust threads or connections.

How would you estimate the storage required for a messaging system over five years?

I would state the assumptions: say 50 million DAU sending 40 messages a day gives 2 billion messages per day. Each stored message is about 200 bytes including metadata, so 2 billion x 200 B = 400 GB per day, about 146 TB per year, and 730 TB over five years of logical data. Then I would multiply by the replication factor, 3, to get about 2.2 PB raw, and add some overhead for indexes and free space, so around 3 PB. I would note that media attachments would be stored separately in an object store, and mention that older messages could go to cheaper storage tiers.

Why do servers need headroom, and how much?

Headroom exists because response time rises steeply as utilization approaches 100%, because failures take capacity away, because deployments temporarily remove servers, and because forecasts are wrong. A common practice is to run at 50 to 70% CPU at peak, and to size so that the loss of one availability zone does not overload the rest: with three zones each must stay under about two thirds utilization. In sizing terms I divide by a target utilization of 0.6 and multiply by N/(N-1) for N zones, which usually results in provisioning 1.5 to 2 times the raw peak requirement. Autoscaling reduces the cost of idle capacity but does not eliminate the need for reserve, because scaling takes minutes.

Your cache hit ratio improves from 90% to 99%. What does that do to the database?

The database sees only cache misses. At 90% hit ratio, 10% of requests go to the database; at 99%, only 1% do, so database load falls by a factor of ten, even though the hit ratio changed by only nine percentage points. For a 35,000 QPS read workload that is the difference between 3,500 and 350 queries per second reaching the database. This is why improving hit ratio at the high end (bigger cache, better eviction, pre-warming, caching negative results) is so valuable, and why you should always size the database for the miss traffic rather than total traffic, with extra margin for cache failures when everything falls through.

How do you decide how many application servers you need?

First compute peak QPS from DAU, actions per user, and a peak factor. Then estimate how many requests per second one server can sustain, from its CPU time per request (for 5 ms of CPU and 16 cores, about 3,200 requests per second at full CPU), and confirm it with a load test in real life. Divide peak QPS by per-server capacity times a target utilization of about 60%, then inflate for failure domains, so that the loss of a zone is survivable. For 139,000 peak QPS with 2,000 requests per second per server, that gives roughly 116 servers, or about 180 after zone headroom. I would also check that the servers' memory and connection limits are not hit first.

20. Recap

  • Estimation finds the order of magnitude of the problem so the design fits it; precision beyond a factor of two or three is wasted, but every number should end in a design decision.
  • Memorize the ladder (KB, MB, GB, TB, PB each 1,000 times the last), 86,400 seconds per day (round to 105), and the rule "1 million per day is about 12 per second".
  • Latency ratios: memory about 100 ns, SSD about 100 microseconds, same data center round trip about 0.5 ms, disk seek about 10 ms, cross-ocean round trip about 150 ms.
  • QPS = DAU x actions per day / 86,400; size for peak (typically 2 to 3 times average, much more for bursty events), then add headroom.
  • Storage = objects per day x size x 365 x years x replication; separate large blobs from small metadata. Bandwidth = peak QPS x payload size, in bits when compared to links.
  • Cache the hot fraction (often 20%); the hit ratio, not just the size, determines database load.
  • The read:write ratio decides where to invest: copies for reads (caches, replicas, CDN), partitioning for writes.
  • Servers = peak QPS / (per-server capacity x target utilization), inflated for zone failure. Response time explodes beyond 70 to 80% utilization.
  • Use percentiles, not averages; tail latency is amplified by fan-out and by many requests per page.
  • Availability: 99.9% is 8.76 hours a year, 99.99% is 52.6 minutes, 99.999% is 5.26 minutes. Series components multiply availability, parallel redundancy multiplies unavailability (if failures are independent).
  • Durability is about not losing data, not about being reachable; achieve it with replication or erasure coding, checksums, and backups.
  • Little's Law, L = lambda x W, sizes threads, connections, and sessions, and explains why a latency spike can cascade into an outage.
