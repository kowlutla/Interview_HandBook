Cheat Sheet: Numbers to Remember

The handful of numbers worth having in your head during a design interview: sizes, latencies, throughput of common components, availability budgets, and the quick formulas that turn them into an estimate in under a minute. All values are order-of-magnitude figures ("about", "typically"); the point is to be within a factor of two to three, not exact. Full derivations live in Chapter 2.

1. Powers of two and size units

Owner: Chapter 2, Estimation. Rule: every 10 powers of two is about a factor of 1,000.

Other useful constants: one day is 86,400 seconds, about 10^5 (round up to 100,000 for mental math). One month is about 2.6 million seconds (30 days), one year about 31.5 million seconds (about 3 x 10^7). A 32-bit integer holds about 4.3 billion values; a 64-bit ID is effectively unlimited. A UUID is 128 bits (16 bytes binary, 36 characters as text). Bits versus bytes: network speeds are quoted in bits per second, storage in bytes; divide by 8.

Rules drawn from the table. Memory is about 1,000x faster than SSD, SSD about 100x faster than a disk seek. A same-DC call costs about 5,000 memory references, so avoid chatty service graphs. Cross-region is paid per round trip: a TCP plus TLS 1.3 connection setup is about 2 round trips, 2 x 150 ms = 300 ms to Asia, which is the case for a CDN (Chapter 12) and connection reuse. Budget p99 across sequential calls, since tail latency compounds (Chapter 40).

4. Availability: nines versus downtime

Owners: Chapter 54 for SLOs and error budgets, Chapter 41 for achieving them. Downtime allowed = (1 - availability) x period.

AvailabilityNinesDowntime per yearPer 30-day monthPer day
99%twoabout 3.65 daysabout 7.2 hoursabout 14.4 min
99.9%threeabout 8.76 hoursabout 43 minabout 1.4 min
99.95%three and a halfabout 4.4 hoursabout 21.6 minabout 43 s
99.99%fourabout 52.6 minabout 4.3 minabout 8.6 s
99.999%fiveabout 5.3 minabout 26 sabout 0.9 s

Series versus parallel. Components in series multiply: three 99.9% services chained give 0.999^3, about 99.7%. Redundant components in parallel fail only when all fail: two independent 99% copies give 1 - 0.01^2 = 99.99%, assuming independent failures (shared deployments and shared dependencies break that assumption). Each extra nine costs roughly an order of magnitude more effort.

5. QPS conversions

Owner: Chapter 2. Use 86,400 s per day, rounded to 10^5 when speed matters.

Volume per dayAverage per secondPeak (assume 2-3x average)
1 millionabout 12/sabout 25-35/s

100 millionabout 1,150/sabout 2.5k-3.5k/s
1 billionabout 11,600/sabout 25k-35k/s
100 billionabout 1.16 million/sabout 2.5M-3.5M/s

Shortcut: requests per day divided by 10^5 gives requests per second; 1M/day is about 10/s, with the exact answer 11.6. Per-user shortcut: daily active users x actions per user per day / 10^5. Read:write ratio then splits the total (a social feed is often 100:1, a logging pipeline the reverse). Peak factor depends on the product: 2-3x for global consumer traffic, 10x or more for flash sales, ticket launches, or events (Chapter 60).

Storage estimation. Daily storage = writes per day x bytes per record. Yearly = daily x 365, about 400 for rough math. Then multiply by the replication factor (typically 3), and add 20-30 percent for indexes and overhead. Example per-user budgets: 1 KB of profile data; a chat user sending 40 messages per day at 200 bytes writes 8 KB per day, about 3 MB per year; a photo user posting 1 photo per day at 3 MB writes about 1 GB per year (plus thumbnails). 100 million such users produce about 100 PB of photos per year before replication, which tells you immediately that it needs object storage with erasure coding, not a database.

8. The 60-second estimation recipe

Do this out loud, writing each number down. State assumptions first, since the interviewer is grading the reasoning, not the digits (Chapter 1).

  1. Users (5 s). Assume 100 million monthly users, 20 million daily active (DAU).
  2. Actions (10 s). Each DAU does 10 reads and 1 write per day: 200M reads and 20M writes per day.
  3. Per second (10 s). Divide by 10^5: 2,000 reads/s and 200 writes/s average. Peak at 3x: 6,000 reads/s and 600 writes/s.
  4. Size (10 s). A write is 1 KB: 20M x 1 KB = 20 GB per day, about 7 TB per year, about 21 TB with 3x replication. Plan for 5 years: roughly 100 TB, which means sharding or a scalable store.
  5. Bandwidth (10 s). Reads return 5 KB: 6,000 x 5 KB = 30 MB/s, about 240 Mbit/s peak egress. Fits comfortably on a few 10 GbE hosts.
  6. Cache (5 s). The hot 20 percent of one day's reads touches maybe 10M keys x 5 KB = 50 GB. Fits in a small Redis cluster.
  7. Conclude (10 s). 6,000 reads/s is easy for a few app servers plus a cache; 600 writes/s fits one primary; the 100 TB over 5 years is the real constraint and drives the storage choice.
Interview tip Round aggressively (86,400 becomes 100,000, 365 becomes 400), say that you are rounding, and always finish with the design consequence: which number is large enough to force sharding, caching, or a queue.
Watch out Average QPS hides bursts; always state a peak factor. Bits versus bytes mix-ups give an 8x error. Throughput figures above are single-node ceilings, so quote them with headroom, never at 100 percent.

10. Recap

  • 10 powers of two is 1,000x; a day is about 10^5 seconds; 1M per day is about 12 per second.
  • Memory 100 ns, SSD 100 us, same-DC RTT 0.5 ms, disk seek 10 ms, cross-region 70-200 ms.
  • One Redis about 100k ops/s, one SQL primary a few thousand writes/s, one Kafka broker hundreds of MB/s, 10GbE about 1.25 GB/s.
  • Three nines is about 8.8 hours per year, four nines about 53 minutes; series multiplies, parallel redundancy helps.
  • L = lambda x W sizes pools and queues; keep utilization below 70 percent.
