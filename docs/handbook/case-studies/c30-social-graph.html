Design a Social Graph (Friends and Followers)

A social graph answers one family of questions: who does this person follow, who follows them, who are their mutual friends, and who should they meet next. Every other social feature (feeds, notifications, search, privacy) calls it thousands of times per second. This page designs it for one billion accounts: how edges are stored, how follow and unfollow stay correct across two copies of the data, how mutual friends and suggestions are computed cheaply, how an account with 100 million followers is kept from melting a single shard, and how everything is sharded by user.

1. Problem statement and clarifying questions

The prompt is short: "Design the friends and followers system of a social network." The word "graph" invites a graph database, and a weak answer goes straight there. A strong answer first decides what questions the system must answer and how fast, because the shape of those questions (almost all of them are one or two hops away from a single user) determines the whole design. Following the interview framework in Chapter 1, here are the questions to ask and the answers we assume for the rest of the page.

QuestionAssumed answer
How many users?1 billion registered accounts, 500 million daily active users (DAU).
What kind of relationship?Both. A follow is a directed edge (A follows B, B need not follow back), as on Twitter or Instagram. A friend is derived: A and B are friends when each follows the other. One edge model must serve both products.
How skewed is the graph?Average account follows 200 others (so 200 billion edges). The follower distribution has a very long tail: the median account has about 100 followers, but a few thousand accounts have more than 10 million, and the largest have about 100 million.

That justifies a sharded key-value or wide-column store with adjacency lists instead of a general graph database that is built for deep traversals.

2. Requirements and scope

2.1 Functional requirements

  • Follow and unfollow an account. Both are idempotent: repeating a request changes nothing.
  • Approve or reject a follow request for a private account; remove a follower; block and unblock an account (a block removes edges in both directions and prevents new ones).
  • Check whether A follows B (and whether B follows A) in one call.
  • List followers and followees of an account, in stable pages, newest first.
  • Return follower and following counts for any account.
  • List mutual friends between the viewer and another account, and the count of them.
  • Return "people you may know" suggestions for the viewer.
  • Publish graph-change events so that the feed fan-out service, notification service and search indexer can react.

2.2 Non-functional requirements

  • Read-dominated and latency-sensitive: about 200 reads for every write.
  • Highly available: a slightly stale list is acceptable, an error page is not. This is the usual availability-over-consistency stance (Chapter 28), with two exceptions: read-your-writes for the actor and prompt enforcement of blocks.
  • Scales horizontally by adding shards, with no query that needs to touch all shards.
  • Skew tolerant: one celebrity must not create a single overloaded partition for reads, writes or counters.
  • Durable: an acknowledged follow is never lost.
  • Self-healing: the several copies of the graph that we keep (out-edges, in-edges, friends, counters) must converge even after crashes.

2.3 Out of scope

Feed ranking and delivery (see Case study 6), content storage, the machine-learning model that scores suggestions (we show where it plugs in and what features it needs), full search over users, and trust-and-safety classifiers. We assume user ids are 64-bit integers issued by an id service (Chapter 32).

3. Estimation with real arithmetic

The estimation (Chapter 2) tells us the read rate we must serve, how large the edge data is, and whether it fits in memory. Numbers are rounded and reused consistently later.

3.1 Traffic

  • Each DAU triggers about 20 graph reads per day: viewing a profile (relationship state and counts), opening follower lists, "mutual friends" badges on cards, filters inside feed and search, and so on. That is 500 M x 20 = 10 billion reads per day.
  • Average read rate = 10,000,000,000 / 86,400 s = about 116,000 per second. Peak is about 3x average, so about 350,000 reads per second.
  • Follow and unfollow actions: 0.1 per DAU per day, so 500 M x 0.1 = 50 million per day, about 580 per second on average and about 1,750 per second at peak.
  • Read:write ratio = 10 B : 50 M = 200:1.
  • Each follow writes two edge rows (out-edge and in-edge), so peak edge-row writes are about 3,500 per second, plus friend rows and counter updates. This is modest for a sharded store. The difficulty is skew, not volume.
  • Fan-out reads: feed fan-out asks for the followers of each author in pages of 1,000. With 100 M posts per day and 200 followers on average that is about 20 M page reads per day, roughly 230 per second. Negligible next to user reads, but a celebrity post requires scanning 100,000 pages (100 M followers / 1,000), which matters for the hot-node discussion.

Nothing about the data volume forces exotic technology.

3.3 Cache sizing

  • Cache the following list of the 100 M most recently active users: 100 M x 200 ids x 8 B = 160 GB. Together with hot follower pages and counters (16 GB) that is about 200 GB. At about 50 GB of usable data per Redis node (Chapter 15) we need 4 primaries, and in practice about 12 nodes with replicas and headroom.
  • If the cache absorbs 90% of reads, the stores see 35,000 reads per second at peak. Spread over 64 primaries that is about 550 per second per node, well inside one node's capacity, and replicas can share it.

3.4 Edge cases worth stating

  • One 100-million-follower account: its follower list is 100 M x 8 B = 800 MB of ids. It must never be loaded as one object.
  • A celebrity announcement can trigger a burst, for example 20,000 new followers per second for several minutes. On a naive layout every one of those writes hits one partition.
  • Two-hop work: a user with 200 friends who each have 200 friends gives 40,000 candidates. Doing that online for 500 M users is too much; we will precompute.
Key idea The numbers say three things. Reads (350 K per second at peak) dominate writes (1.75 K per second), so cache and read replicas carry the design. The data (15 TB logical) is modest, so simple sharded storage is enough. And the only truly dangerous part is skew: the one account whose edges, counters and write bursts exceed what one shard can take.

4. API design

Internal RPC (for example gRPC) between services, plus REST for the public surface (Chapter 5). The actor id always comes from the authenticated token, never from the request body, so a user cannot forge a follow on behalf of someone else.

POST   /v1/users/{id}/follow            body: { "request_id": "uuid" }
  200: { "state": "following" | "requested" }        (requested = private account)
DELETE /v1/users/{id}/follow            idempotent unfollow
DELETE /v1/users/{id}/follower          remove a follower (soft block)
POST   /v1/users/{id}/block   DELETE /v1/users/{id}/block

GET /v1/users/{id}/relationship?with=VIEWER
  200: { "following": true, "followed_by": false, "friends": false,
         "blocked": false, "pending": false }

GET /v1/users/{id}/followers?limit=50&cursor=OPAQUE
GET /v1/users/{id}/following?limit=50&cursor=OPAQUE
  200: { "items": [ { "user_id", "since" } ], "next_cursor": "OPAQUE" }

GET /v1/users/{id}/counts          200: { "followers": 100412331, "following": 312 }
GET /v1/users/{id}/mutual?limit=20  200: { "count": 37, "sample": [ {user_id}, ... ] }
GET /v1/suggestions?limit=20        200: { "items": [ { "user_id", "reason", "mutual_count" } ] }

Internal (bulk, for other services):
POST /internal/relationships:batchGet   body: { "viewer": 7, "targets": [..up to 500..] }
GET  /internal/users/{id}/followers:scan?page_size=1000&cursor=...

Four design points. First, request_id plus the natural primary key (src, dst) makes follow idempotent: a mobile client retrying after a timeout cannot create two edges or two counter increments (Chapter 32). Second, the cursor is opaque and encodes the last key seen (a keyset cursor, not an offset), because offset paging over a list that is being modified skips or repeats rows and gets slower as the offset grows. Third, the batch relationship API exists because other services (feed, search, profile cards) need the relationship to 20 or 500 accounts at once; one batched call per page, not 500 calls. Fourth, the relationship endpoint is the hottest read in the system, so it is a single-shard point lookup by design (section 8).

5. Data model and storage choices

5.1 What we store

edges_out:  PK (src_id, dst_id)         | created_at | state (active|pending|blocked) | version
            partition key = src_id, clustering key = dst_id (or created_at desc)
edges_in:   PK (dst_id, bucket, src_id) | created_at | state | version
            partition key = (dst_id, bucket), bucket = hash(src_id) % B_dst
friends:    PK (user_id, friend_id)     | since
            partition key = user_id     -- derived from edges, capped at 5,000 per user
counters:   user_id -> followers, following   (sharded counters for hot accounts)
suggestions: user_id -> [ (candidate_id, score, reason) x 50 ], refreshed by batch job
outbox:     per-shard log of unpublished graph-change events

The version column is a monotonically increasing number (a timestamp from a hybrid clock or a per-edge counter). It lets idempotent replays and out-of-order messages settle on the latest truth: a stale "add" arriving after a newer "remove" is discarded (Chapter 27).

5.2 Why every edge is stored twice (or three times)

The two basic access patterns are "whom does X follow" and "who follows X". Each is a scan over all edges that share one endpoint. A single table partitioned by source serves the first with one partition scan, but answering the second would need a scan of every partition. Hence we keep edges_out, partitioned by source, and edges_in, partitioned by destination. Writing an edge means writing two rows, usually on two different shards. That is a deliberate denormalisation (Chapter 17): we pay extra write cost and a consistency problem to turn every read into a single-partition lookup. The friend table is a third, derived copy that makes "mutual friends" cheap, because it stores only reciprocal edges, which are bounded by 5,000 per user.

We choose a wide-column store (or sharded MySQL, which has the same logical layout) for the edge tables, an in-memory cache (Chapter 13) in front for adjacency lists and counters, Kafka (Chapter 34) for graph-change events, and an offline batch engine (Chapter 37) for suggestions with a key-value store for the results. The storage engine under the edge store is typically an LSM tree (Chapter 19), which suits the append-heavy write pattern but makes mass deletes (tombstones) something to watch, as discussed in section 13.

6. A first simple design, then the scaled architecture

6.1 The simple version

Start with one application server and one relational database holding a single table follows(follower_id, followee_id, created_at) with two indexes, one on each column. Follow is an insert. "Is A following B" is an index lookup. "Followers of B" is an index range scan on followee_id. Counts are COUNT(*). Mutual friends is a self-join:

SELECT a.followee_id
FROM follows a JOIN follows b ON a.followee_id = b.followee_id
WHERE a.follower_id = :me AND b.follower_id = :other;

This is correct and perfectly fine for a few million users. It fails at our scale for four separate reasons. The table (200 B rows) does not fit one machine. COUNT(*) for a 100 M-follower account scans 100 M index entries. A single hot account concentrates all its reads and writes on one B-tree page range. And the two indexes mean every follow updates two B-trees, which already hints at the split into two tables. So we shard, split the table by access pattern, cache, and move the heavy computations offline.

6.2 The scaled architecture

Responsibilities. The Graph write service validates (not self-follow, not blocked, under the follow cap, rate limit), writes the out-edge row, and records the change in an outbox in the same transaction. The edge workers consume EdgeChanged events and maintain the derived tables. The Graph read service is stateless: it picks the right table, consults the cache, and hides cross-shard details from callers. The Suggestion API reads a precomputed list and filters it live (already following, blocked, deactivated) before returning. Other services such as feed fan-out subscribe to the same events (Chapter 36) rather than polling the graph.

7. Request flows

7.1 Follow

  1. The gateway authenticates and applies a per-user rate limit (Chapter 16): follow spam is the most common abuse, so a user can only create a few hundred follows per day.
  2. The write service checks the block list (one point lookup in the target's out-edge partition for a block row) and the cap, then writes edges_out(src=A, dst=B, state=active) and an outbox row in a single transaction on A's shard. If B is private, the state is pending and no counters move yet.
  3. The call returns as soon as that transaction commits. The caller's own cache entry (A's following list, the relationship to B) is updated or invalidated synchronously so A's next page load shows the follow. This is read-your-writes for the actor only.
  4. A relay reads the outbox and publishes EdgeAdded(A, B, version) to Kafka, partitioned by dst so all events for one target are ordered (Chapter 35). This transactional outbox avoids the dual-write problem: the edge row and the event can never disagree because they commit together.
  5. The edge worker applies the in-edge row, the friend rows if reciprocal, and increments the two counters. Every operation is an upsert keyed by (src, dst) or a counter delta tagged with the event id, so redelivery is harmless.
  6. Downstream consumers (feed fan-out backfills A's feed with B's recent posts; the notification service tells B) receive the same event.

Unfollow is the same flow with a delete. In an LSM-based store a delete writes a tombstone. We write the out-edge tombstone first (the user-visible truth), then the event removes the in-edge, removes friend rows if they existed, and decrements counters. The version number protects against a quick follow-unfollow-follow sequence being applied out of order.

7.2 Read paths

  • Relationship check (A, B). Two point reads on the out-edge table: (A, B) and (B, A). Both are single-partition lookups on A's and B's shards and are cache-friendly. We do not use the in-edge table, so a celebrity B never sees this read concentrated on its shard.
  • Following list. Range scan of A's partition in edges_out, newest first, 50 rows per page, served from the adjacency cache when A is active.
  • Followers list. Range scan over the buckets of B's partition in edges_in. For ordinary accounts there is one bucket; for celebrities there are several (section 11), and the reader merges them.
  • Counts. One cache read. Counts are maintained incrementally and reconciled by a nightly job (section 13); we never run COUNT(*) online.

8. Deep dive 1: edge storage and sharding by user

8.1 Choosing the shard key

Sharding (Chapter 25) is a choice of key and of mapping. The candidates:

OptionSingle-hop query costBalanceProblems

We choose hash of the owner's user id for each table. Hashing removes the signup-order skew; owning each table by one endpoint makes "list for user X" one partition scan. Rather than mapping hash directly to machines, we use 1,024 logical shards (hash(user_id) mod 1024) and a small shard map from logical shard to physical node, with 16 logical shards on each of 64 primary nodes. When we add capacity we move whole logical shards between nodes without rehashing any key. This is the standard virtual-shard trick (Chapter 57 covers consistent hashing as an alternative).

8.2 Cross-shard consistency without distributed transactions

Because the out-edge and the in-edge live on different shards, a follow is a two-shard update. Running a two-phase commit (Chapter 31) for 1,750 follows per second is possible but would add latency, make availability the product of two shards' availability, and block on slow participants. The alternative is the pattern from section 7: commit the source of truth locally, then propagate asynchronously, and design the propagation to be idempotent and repairable. The window of inconsistency is typically seconds. During it, "A follows B" is true in edges_out but B's followers page may not yet list A. This is acceptable because the actor sees the right state through read-your-writes and nobody else can tell.

What if the worker dies for good, or an event is lost? A repair job (anti-entropy) periodically compares the two tables for a slice of the key space, either by sampling or by comparing per-partition checksums, and fixes differences (Chapter 33 describes Merkle-tree style comparison). Because edges_out is the source of truth, repair always rebuilds edges_in and friends from it, never the other way around. This also gives disaster recovery: edges_in can be rebuilt from scratch from edges_out, which is a useful tool when we change the bucket count.

8.3 Row layout and paging

Within a partition, rows are sorted by created_at descending, then by neighbour id, so the first page is the newest follows and a keyset cursor (created_at, id) continues from where the last page ended. A "relationship check" needs lookup by neighbour id, not by time, so we keep a secondary clustering order (a second small table or a local index) keyed by (src, dst), which serves the point lookup. In a sharded MySQL implementation that is simply two indexes on the same table; in a wide-column store it is two column families or a materialised view.

9. Deep dive 2: mutual friends

The query is: given viewer A and profile owner B, which accounts are friends of both? Because "friends" are bounded at 5,000 per user, this is the intersection of two bounded sets. Several designs exist.

9.1 Handling the follower model (non-reciprocal)

Not every product shows only friends. On a follower network the useful question is "people I follow who also follow B". The naive approach, intersecting following(A) (at most 7,500) with followers(B) (up to 100 M), is wrong for celebrities because it would load a giant list. The right approach is to iterate over the smaller side and do point lookups on the other: for each followee f of A (at most 7,500, typically 200), ask whether edge (f, B) exists. These are lookups in each f's out-edge partition, batched by shard (about 64 parallel batch calls at most). That is O(|following(A)|) regardless of B's popularity, and it uses the out-edge table, which is never hot for B. A bloom filter or the friend table can short-circuit some cases (Chapter 57).

9.2 Caching the answer

The result for a (viewer, owner) pair is cheap to compute and rarely repeated, so caching pairs is wasteful. Cache the inputs instead: friend lists of active users (at most 5,000 x 8 B = 40 KB each). A hot profile appears in thousands of viewers' requests per second, so its friend list stays in cache permanently. For list views that show only a count, compute from the cached lists and optionally cache the count per pair with a short TTL.

10. Deep dive 3: friend suggestions ("people you may know")

The classic signal is the friend of a friend (FoF): accounts that are two hops away. The more mutual friends two people have, the more likely they know each other. Additional signals include shared groups, school or employer, address-book matches, profile views, and recency of contact. A machine-learning ranker combines them, but the candidate generation, the hard systems part, is a graph computation.

10.1 Why not compute online

Suppose a user has 200 friends who each have 200 friends. The FoF set is up to 40,000 candidates before deduplication. Counting how many paths reach each candidate means reading 200 friend lists (200 shard reads) and aggregating 40,000 ids. Doing that per request for 500 M DAU at several opens per day would add millions of heavy operations per second, and a user with a friend who has 5,000 friends makes it worse. Online computing is only reasonable for tiny graphs. Instead we compute offline.

10.2 Offline pipeline

The batch runs on a distributed dataflow engine (Chapter 37). The two-hop join is the expensive step: for each middle node B it pairs every friend of B with every other friend of B, so a node with degree d produces d(d-1) pairs. This quadratic blow-up is the key problem, and the standard remedies are:

  • Degree capping and sampling. For nodes with degree over about 1,000 emit pairs only for a random sample of neighbours, or skip them (their path carries little information: a celebrity connects millions of unrelated people). Weighting each path by 1/log(degree of the middle node), the Adamic-Adar weighting, further reduces the influence of high-degree middle nodes.
  • Incremental recomputation. Recompute only for users active in the last week whose neighbourhood changed. Assume 100 M such users per day and 20,000 sampled pairs each: 2 trillion pair emissions. A cluster of 1,000 cores each producing about 1 M pairs per second finishes in about 2,000 seconds, under an hour. This is an estimate, not a benchmark, but it shows the job fits in a nightly window.
  • Keep only the top K. Maintain a bounded heap of 50 candidates per user during aggregation instead of materialising every FoF.

Every suggestion carries a reason ("12 mutual friends") that the mutual-friend machinery from section 9 supplies cheaply. Privacy matters here: suggestions can reveal relationships, so private accounts and blocked pairs are excluded in the pipeline and again at serve time.

11. Deep dive 4: celebrity hot nodes

One account with 100 million followers breaks the assumption that load is spread evenly. Three different hot spots appear, and each needs its own remedy.

11.1 Hot partition on writes (the in-edge table)

In edges_in the partition key is the target, so all 100 M rows for the celebrity live in one partition on one node, and a burst of 20,000 follows per second all lands there. A single node typically handles on the order of several thousand writes per second, so it saturates, replication lags, and reads of that partition slow too. The remedy is to bucket the partition key: the in-edge key becomes (dst_id, bucket) with bucket = hash(src_id) mod B, where B is chosen per account. Ordinary accounts have B = 1. When an account crosses a follower threshold (say 1 million), a background job raises its B to 16, then 64 for the biggest. Each follower's row goes to a bucket determined by its own id, so writes spread evenly across B different shards: 20,000 per second over 16 buckets is 1,250 per second per shard.

11.2 Hot key on counters

The follower counter for a celebrity is one key incremented 20,000 times per second. Atomic increments on one key serialise on one node. Use sharded counters: 16 sub-counters, each follower event increments counter[C][hash(src) mod 16], and a read sums the 16 values (cached for about a second). Better still, workers batch: aggregate increments in memory for a short interval (say 500 ms) and apply one delta per counter per interval. Counts then lag slightly, which no one notices on a number like 100,412,331. A nightly reconciliation job recounts from the edge table to correct drift (section 13).

11.3 Hot reads and giant lists

  • Profile and counts reads for a famous account arrive from millions of viewers. These are cacheable, and a few seconds of staleness is fine, so we cache them aggressively and add a tiny in-process cache in the read service with a 1 to 5 second TTL. For the very hottest keys, replicate the cache entry across several cache nodes and have clients pick one at random, to avoid one cache node taking the whole load (Chapter 14, the hot-key and stampede problems).
  • Follower list scans by fan-out. A post by a 100 M-follower account would need 100 M feed insertions, which is exactly why Case study 6 uses pull for celebrities. The graph service supports this by exposing "is_celebrity" and by paging the scan in 1,000-row pages read from replicas, never as one query.
  • Never offer "list all followers" as one response. The public follower list is paginated, and past a certain depth (for example 10,000 entries) the UI stops, as large networks do for abuse and cost reasons.
  • Mutual friends with a celebrity iterate over the viewer's smaller list (section 9.1), so the celebrity's size never matters.

11.4 Tradeoff

Bucket every account at a fixed B (for example 4)No migration, simpler logicEvery follower list of a normal account becomes 4 reads and a merge, wasting 99% of the benefit

We choose threshold-based buckets, with the bucket count stored in the user's metadata row and cached. Changing B requires re-writing the in-edge rows of that account, which is possible because edges_out is the source of truth: a background job streams that account's followers from their out-edge partitions (or from the old in-edge buckets) and writes them into the new layout, with dual reads during the cutover.

12. Scaling, bottlenecks and caching

12.1 Read path

Reads are 99.5% of operations. Layers, from the closest to the store:

  • Adjacency cache (Redis or Memcached, Chapter 13): per-user following list (as a sorted structure or compact blob), friend list, counters, relationship bits. Read-through with TTL (for example one hour for lists, seconds for celebrity counters). On a follow by A, the write path updates A's cached lists directly (write-through for the actor), and event consumers invalidate or patch B's follower-related entries.
  • Read replicas (Chapter 24): reads that miss the cache go to replicas, with the primary used only for the actor's read-your-writes window (tracked by a short-lived "recently wrote" flag that routes that user's reads to the primary for a few seconds).
  • Request coalescing: when many concurrent requests need the same missing key, one fetches and the others wait for it, preventing a stampede on the store.
  • Batching: the batch relationship API groups lookups by shard so a page of 50 profile cards costs at most a handful of RPCs.

12.2 Write path

With 1,750 follows per second at peak, a single logical shard does not need to be fast, but the system must absorb bursts and replays. Kafka partitions events by target; workers scale with partition count. Backlog on the queue converts a burst into a short delay in derived tables, never into a failure for the user. If follow volume grows 10x, adding nodes and increasing logical-to-physical spreading is enough.

12.4 Rebalancing and resharding

Because we use 1,024 logical shards, adding capacity means copying whole logical shards to new nodes, switching the shard map entry, and deleting the old copy. A shard is about 15 GB logical (15 TB / 1,024), so one move copies about 15 GB and takes minutes. If we ever need more than 1,024 logical shards (a possibility at 10x scale), we split each into two, doubling the count in a controlled, shard-by-shard migration (Chapter 26 covers migration strategies).

13.1 Consistency and idempotency decisions

  • Source of truth: edges_out, one shard per actor, committed with the outbox row in one local transaction.
  • Derived data (edges_in, friends, counters, suggestions) is eventually consistent and rebuildable. This is the core claim of the design and should be said explicitly in the interview.
  • Idempotency: follow and unfollow are set operations on a primary key, so repeating them is a no-op. Counters are the exception, because incrementing twice is wrong; we use the event id to dedupe or accept rare drift fixed by reconciliation.
  • Ordering: events for one target are ordered by partitioning, and the version in each row resolves races between a follow and an unfollow that arrive out of order.
  • Race: mutual follow. A follows B and B follows A at the same moment. Each worker checks the reverse edge, but both may check before the other's edge is visible and neither writes the friend rows. The fix is a re-check: after writing its own edge, each worker re-reads the reverse edge once more with a short delay, or the nightly reconciliation computes friends from edges. Friend rows are an idempotent derived fact, so duplicating the write is harmless.
  • Block semantics: a block is a strongly consistent write on the blocker's shard; the worker removes the opposite edge and the content services consult blocks at read time. Because of the delay, a block must never rely solely on derived tables.

13.2 Multi-region

Users are served from the region nearest them. The simplest correct approach is a home region per user: all writes for edges_out of user A go to A's home region, and other regions hold asynchronous replicas for reads (Chapter 26). A follow by A of B, where B is homed elsewhere, writes to A's home; the event is replicated cross-region to B's home to update edges_in and counters. Reads of "who follows B" are served from B's home region or from local replicas with seconds to minutes of lag, which is acceptable under our requirements. Active-active writes to the same edge row from two regions are avoided by the home-region rule, so there are no write conflicts to resolve for a single actor. Unfollow and block events use versions so the latest wins. For disaster recovery, replicas can be promoted, with a possible loss of a few seconds of recent edges; the outbox and Kafka mirror help replay them.

14. Security, abuse, monitoring and cost

14.1 Security and abuse

  • Authorisation: the actor comes from the token. Private-account lists (followers, following) are visible only to approved followers; the read service checks this per request (Chapter 55).
  • Follow spam and follow-farming: per-user and per-target rate limits (Chapter 16), daily caps, new-account throttling, and anomaly detection on follow velocity. Mass follow-unfollow patterns are a classic abuse signal.
  • Scraping the graph: deep pagination is capped, list endpoints are rate limited per token and per IP, and large bulk exports are not offered.
  • Information leaks: suggestions and mutual friends can reveal relationships of private or blocked users. Apply exclusion at both batch time and serve time, and do not show "mutual friend" names that the viewer is not allowed to see.
  • Deletion and privacy law: deleting an account must remove every edge in both tables and the derived rows (an offline delete job streamed from edges_out), and purge suggestion lists that include the user (Chapter 56).

14.2 Monitoring and SLOs

  • SLOs: relationship check p99 under 10 ms with 99.99% availability; list page p99 under 50 ms; mutual friends p99 under 100 ms; follow write p99 under 100 ms; derived-table freshness: 99% of events applied within 10 seconds (Chapter 54).
  • Health signals: Kafka consumer lag and oldest unapplied event age; replica lag per shard; cache hit ratio (alert below 85%); store read QPS; per-shard write QPS and the top 10 hottest partitions; number of accounts per bucket count.
  • Correctness signals: edges_out versus edges_in mismatch rate found by repair; counter drift found by reconciliation; suggestion exclusion violations (a suggested user who was already followed), which should be zero.

14.3 Cost

The dominant costs are replicated storage (about 45 TB, modest), the cache fleet (a few hundred GB of RAM, modest), and the offline suggestion job, which is the largest compute item and runs for under an hour per day on a few thousand cores. Levers: smaller rows (varint-encoded ids, delta-encoded timestamps), tiered storage for long-inactive users' lists, sampling in the batch job, and fewer replicas in cold regions.

15. Evolution, phasing and alternatives not chosen

15.1 What to build first

  1. Phase 1: one sharded relational cluster with two tables (out and in), synchronous writes of both within the same shard when possible, counters stored in the user row. This handles tens of millions of users.
  2. Phase 2: add the adjacency cache, read replicas, and the asynchronous derived tables via an outbox and a queue. Add friend table and mutual-friend service.
  3. Phase 3: add offline suggestions, repair and reconciliation jobs, and a counter service with batching.
  4. Phase 4: bucketed celebrity partitions, sharded counters, multi-region home-based replication.

15.2 At 10x scale (about 3.5 million reads per second at peak and 2 trillion edges)

  • Raise logical shards to 4,096 or 8,192; hundreds of primary nodes; stronger isolation of celebrity traffic.
  • Add a local per-host cache tier in the read service plus a regional cache tier, reducing store traffic further.
  • Suggestions move from daily recompute to incremental streaming updates fed by edge events, plus embeddings for candidate generation.
  • Consider graph partitioning for the batch jobs (not the serving path) so the two-hop join shuffles less data.

15.3 Alternatives we did not choose

  • A native graph database for serving: expressive but hard to shard and operate at this volume, and our queries are shallow.
  • Two-phase commit for both edge rows: would give immediate consistency of both views but harms latency and availability for a gain nobody sees.
  • One blob per user adjacency list: simple but unsuitable for celebrities and high write rates.
  • Fully precomputed mutual friends: quadratic storage.
  • Online suggestions: fresh but too costly and unpredictable.

16. Interview Q&A

Why not use a graph database such as Neo4j for this?

Graph databases shine at multi-hop traversals and ad-hoc pattern queries, such as fraud rings or recommendation research. Our serving queries are nearly all one hop from a known user id: list neighbours, check one edge, count. Those are key-range scans, which a sharded key-value or relational store does at lower cost and with simpler scaling. Partitioning a billion-node, 200-billion-edge graph across machines without a cheap natural cut is hard, and a hot celebrity node concentrates load. I would keep a graph engine for offline analytics or a trust-and-safety subgraph, and use sharded adjacency tables for the serving path.

How do you keep the out-edge and in-edge tables consistent?

I treat the out-edge table as the source of truth and write it, with an outbox row, in one local transaction on the actor's shard. A relay publishes the outbox event and a worker applies the in-edge row. Every step is idempotent, keyed by (src, dst) and a version, so redelivery is safe. Because events can still be lost or skipped, an anti-entropy job compares partitions and rebuilds the derived table from the source of truth. The user sees the right state through read-your-writes, and other users can only observe the delay of a few seconds. This avoids two-phase commit on the hot path.

How do you compute mutual friends quickly?

For a friend graph with a cap of 5,000, I keep a derived friend table so each user has a short, sorted list. The service fetches both lists, which are usually cached and each live on a single shard, and intersects them in one pass over at most about 10,000 entries. That is microseconds of CPU. For a follower graph, I iterate over the smaller side (accounts the viewer follows) and do batched point lookups to see whether each follows the target, so the cost depends on the viewer's list and not on a celebrity's follower count. If only the count is needed, a sketch or a bitmap intersection is enough.

An account with 100 million followers announces something and gets 20,000 follows per second. What breaks and how do you fix it?

Three things: the in-edge partition for that account is on one shard that cannot take 20,000 writes per second, the follower counter is a single key being incremented that often, and cached reads of its profile are hot. I bucket the in-edge partition key as (account, hash(follower) mod 16) so writes spread over 16 shards, about 1,250 per second each. I split the counter into sub-counters and batch increments over a short window, and I cache profile and count reads aggressively with a short TTL and replicated hot keys. Relationship checks are unaffected because they use the follower's own out-edge partition.

How would you shard the graph, and what are the downsides?

I hash the owner's user id into 1,024 logical shards and map them to physical nodes, with out-edges owned by the source and in-edges owned by the target. A list query is a single-shard range scan, and adding capacity moves whole logical shards without rehashing keys. The downsides are that each follow writes to two shards, so I need asynchronous propagation and repair, and that hash sharding ignores graph locality, so a two-hop query touches many shards. I mitigate this by batching lookups per shard and by moving two-hop work offline, where a dataflow engine handles the shuffle.

How do you generate friend suggestions at this scale?

Offline. A daily batch job reads an edge snapshot, joins it with itself to emit every friend-of-friend path, aggregates path counts per candidate, combines them with other signals in a ranker, and stores the top 50 per user in a key-value store. The risk is the quadratic blow-up from high-degree nodes, so I cap or sample neighbours of nodes above about 1,000 friends and weight paths by the inverse log of the middle node's degree. At serve time I re-filter the list so users already followed, blocked or deactivated never appear, and each suggestion shows a reason like the mutual friend count.

How do you maintain accurate follower counts?

I never run a count query online. Each event applies a delta to a counter, which is a sharded counter for hot accounts, and the worker deduplicates by event id. Because counters can still drift if an event is lost or applied twice, a nightly job recounts rows per partition from the edge table and corrects the stored value. For large numbers the UI rounds the display, so a small error is invisible. For exactness, such as enforcing the 7,500 follow cap, I count the actor's own out-edge partition, which is small and consistent.

What happens if a user follows, immediately unfollows, and follows again, and the events arrive out of order?

Each edge write carries a version, such as a hybrid timestamp assigned on the actor's shard, and the in-edge and friend upserts apply a change only if its version is higher than the stored one. A delayed "add" with an old version is ignored after a newer "remove" has been applied, and the final state matches the actor's last action. For deletes I keep a tombstone with its version until stale events cannot arrive any more, which is bounded by the queue retention. Partitioning events by target also keeps most of them in order.

How do blocks work, and why can't they be eventually consistent?

A block is a security and safety decision, so I treat it as a strongly consistent write on the blocker's shard, with the removal of edges in both directions done by events. Because propagation takes seconds, any service that decides whether one user may see another's content calls the graph service to check the block row at read time, reading from the primary or a replica verified fresh. The block bit has a very short cache TTL and is invalidated synchronously on write. Derived views such as suggestions exclude blocked pairs both at batch time and again at serve time.

How do you handle deleting an account with millions of edges?

I mark the account as deleted first, which makes it disappear from reads immediately because every read path filters on account status. Then an asynchronous job streams its edge rows from edges_out (the user's followees) and from the in-edge buckets (its followers), deletes the matching rows on the opposite sides, decrements counters in batches, and removes the user from friend and suggestion tables. For an account with 100 million followers the job runs in pages with throttling so it does not create a tombstone storm and slow other partitions. Completion is tracked so the process can resume after a crash.

How would the design change for a symmetric friendship network instead of follows?

A friendship needs a request-and-accept handshake and creates one logical relationship, but we can still store it as two directed rows (one per direction) in the friend table, written when the accepting user approves. There is a cap on friends per user, so lists are small and the celebrity problem mostly disappears, replaced by a separate "page" or "follower" concept for public figures. Mutual friends become the central query and are cheap because of the cap. The accept step needs a transaction across two shards or the same asynchronous pattern with a state machine (requested, accepted, declined), and the same repair job guarantees both rows exist.

17. Recap

  • The social graph is wide and shallow: 1 B nodes, 200 B edges, mostly one-hop queries. A sharded adjacency store plus caches beats a general graph database for serving.
  • Estimation: 350 K reads per second at peak versus 1,750 writes per second, about 15 TB logical and 45 TB replicated data. Reads and skew, not volume, drive the design.
  • Store every edge twice with different partition keys (out by source, in by target), plus a derived, capped friend table. The out-edge table is the source of truth; everything else is rebuildable.
  • Follow commits one local transaction (edge plus outbox), returns, and propagates asynchronously and idempotently with versions. Anti-entropy and nightly reconciliation catch lost events.
  • Shard by hash of the owner's user id into 1,024 logical shards mapped to nodes; list queries touch one shard, rebalancing moves whole logical shards.
  • Mutual friends are the intersection of two short sorted lists, or point lookups from the smaller side; suggestions come from an offline two-hop job with degree caps and a ranker.
  • Celebrities need bucketed in-edge partitions, sharded and batched counters, and aggressive hot-key caching. Relationship checks avoid their partitions entirely.
  • Be explicit about consistency: strong where safety needs it (blocks, the actor's own view), eventual everywhere else.
