Case study 06 · Worked solutions

Design a News Feed

A news feed shows each user a personalised, mostly recent list of posts from the people they follow. The hard part is that one write (a post) must become visible in millions of different lists, while one read (opening the app) must feel instant. This page designs it for 500 million users and works through fan-out, feed caches, pagination, ranking, deletes and failure modes.

How to approach this problem (read this first)

What the interviewer is really testing. A feed looks like a list query, but the interview is about a single trade: how much work you do when a post is written versus when a feed is opened. Three hard parts sit inside that. First, fan-out: one post must reach many follower lists, and the follower count is wildly uneven, so a celebrity breaks the simple design. Second, the read path: the first page must come back fast, so you must say what is precomputed and where it lives. Third, correctness over time: pagination while new posts arrive, and deletes and blocks that must take effect even though copies exist. Ranking and media are usually side topics, so name them and do not drown in them.

The thinking order, step by step

  1. Split the problem into a write path and a read path. Say: "A feed is two problems: distributing a post, and assembling a page. Which one is more frequent?" You do this first because every later choice is a decision about which path pays.
  2. Ask the questions that change the design. Say: "Is the follow graph one-way, how skewed are follower counts, and is the feed ranked or newest first?" Skew is the key one: it is what will force the hybrid later. Also ask how fresh a new post must be.
  3. Estimate read QPS and fan-out volume. Say: "300 million daily users opening the feed 10 times gives about 35,000 reads per second, 100,000 at peak. 100 million posts times 200 followers is 20 billion insertions a day if I push to everyone." Start with reads because they show the system is read-heavy, then fan-out because it shows the cost of the obvious fix.
  4. Draft the two APIs that matter. Say: "Publish a post with a request id so retries are safe, and get-feed with an opaque cursor." Do the cursor now, because it constrains the storage shape (a sorted set by score rather than a list by offset).
  5. Show the naive design and why it fails. Say: "Join followees to posts at read time. Fine for thousands of users, but 200 shard probes per page ruins tail latency at 100,000 reads per second." This justifies precomputation.
  6. Introduce push, then show its limit. Say: "Precompute each user's feed on write. A user with 30 million followers makes one post cost 30 million inserts." Now the interviewer expects the celebrity answer.
  7. Land the hybrid and the feed cache. Say: "Push for normal accounts to active followers only; pull celebrity posts at read time and merge." Add that feeds hold post ids, not bodies, so edits need no fan-out.
  8. Handle pagination, deletes and ranking. Say: "Cursors by value, deletes filtered at read time, ranking on a few hundred candidates."
  9. Finish with failure and scale. Say: "If a Redis shard dies, the feed rebuilds from the graph, rate limited."

The decision points

  • Push or pull? Ask: where is the cost? Reads outnumber posts 30 to 1 by request, so push for most accounts; the follower skew decides who is excluded.
  • List or sorted set for the feed? Ask: do I need stable cursors and removals? Yes, so a sorted set by score.
  • Store ids or full posts in the feed? Ask: what happens on an edit or a delete? Ids, because one record changes, not 200 copies.
  • Offset or cursor pagination? Ask: do new items arrive while scrolling? Yes, so cursors.
  • Strong or eventual consistency? Ask: what must a user never see? Their own post must appear at once and blocked content must not appear; everything else may lag seconds.

Common mistakes on this problem

  • Choosing only push or only pull. Fix: say what breaks each, then present the hybrid.
  • Ignoring celebrities. Fix: ask about follower skew in the first five minutes.
  • Using offset pagination. Fix: use a cursor of the last score and id.
  • Copying whole posts into every feed. Fix: store ids and hydrate from the post cache.
  • Pushing to every follower including inactive ones. Fix: push to active users and rebuild on return.
  • Treating ranking as the whole answer. Fix: spend a minute on candidates, scoring, a recency fallback, then return to storage.
Interview tip Put the two paths on the board before any numbers, and keep asking which path pays for each decision. If you can say "push for most, pull for celebrities, and here is the threshold and why", you have answered the central question.

1. Problem statement and clarifying questions

The prompt is usually one line: "Design the home feed of a social network." A strong candidate does not start drawing. Following the interview framework in Chapter 1, we first pin down what is being asked. Below are the questions to ask and the answers we will assume for the rest of the page.

QuestionAssumed answer
How many users?500 million registered, 300 million daily active users (DAU).
What is a follow?Directed and asymmetric (like Twitter or Instagram), not a mutual friendship. The average user follows about 200 accounts; the follower-count distribution is very skewed, and a few accounts have tens of millions of followers.
What goes in a post?Text up to a few thousand characters, optionally photos or short video. Posts can be edited briefly and deleted.
Chronological or ranked feed?Ranked by default (relevance model), with a "Following, latest first" toggle that is purely chronological. Both must work.
How fresh must a new post be?A normal user's post should appear in followers' feeds within a few seconds (target p99 under 10 s). A celebrity post within about 30 s is acceptable.
Latency of opening the feed?p99 under 300 ms server-side for the first page of 20 posts.
Do we need ads, search, comments, likes?No. We only expose hooks for them. Counters such as likes are shown but owned by another service.
Global?Yes, users on every continent; we design one region deeply and discuss multi-region in section 10.
Interview tip Say out loud that "feed" hides two different problems: a write path (distributing a post) and a read path (assembling a page). Most of the design is a decision about how much work to do on each path.

2. Requirements and scope

2.1 Functional requirements

  • A user can publish a post (text plus optional media).
  • A user can follow and unfollow another account, and block or mute accounts.
  • A user can open their home feed and scroll through it indefinitely, page by page.
  • A user can edit or delete their own post, and the change must propagate to every feed that shows it.
  • The system emits events that a notification service can consume (for example "an account you enabled alerts for just posted").

2.2 Non-functional requirements

  • Read-heavy and latency-sensitive: the first page must be fast; reads vastly outnumber writes.
  • Highly available: a stale or slightly incomplete feed is better than an error page. We choose availability over strict consistency (Chapter 28).
  • Eventually consistent propagation with one strong guarantee: a user always sees their own post right after posting (read-your-writes).
  • Scalable to skewed graphs: an account with 50 million followers must not melt the system.
  • Durable: an accepted post is never lost.

2.3 Out of scope

Comment and like storage, ad insertion, full-text search, direct messages, content moderation models (we only show where they plug in), and the internals of the media transcoder.

3. Estimation with real arithmetic

Estimation (Chapter 2) tells us which side of the system is the expensive one. We round aggressively and keep every number consistent for the rest of the page.

3.1 Traffic

  • DAU = 300 M. Each opens the feed about 10 times a day (many short sessions), giving 3 B feed loads per day.
  • Average feed QPS = 3,000,000,000 / 86,400 s = about 35,000 per second. Peak is about 3x average, so about 100,000 per second.
  • Posts: 1 post per 3 DAU per day gives 100 M posts per day, about 1,160 per second on average and about 3,500 per second at peak.
  • At the request level the read:write ratio is 3 B loads to 100 M posts = 30:1. Counting items shown (20 per load = 60 B post views per day) against posts created it is 600:1.

3.2 Fan-out volume

  • Average followers per account = 200 (500 M users x 200 follows each = 100 B edges, divided by 500 M accounts).
  • If every post were pushed to every follower: 100 M x 200 = 20 B feed insertions per day, which is 231,000 per second average and about 700,000 per second at peak.
  • We only push to active followers (opened the app in the last 7 days, about 60%). That is 12 B per day, or 140,000 per second average and about 420,000 per second at peak. Celebrity posts (section 7) are pulled instead of pushed, which lowers this a little further; we keep 420 K per second as the design number.

3.3 Storage

The feed cache is the largest memory item. At about 50 GB of usable data per Redis node, 5 TB needs about 100 primaries; with one replica each, about 200 nodes.

3.4 Bandwidth

Each feed load shows about 10 images of 150 KB, so 1.5 MB. Average: 35,000 x 1.5 MB = 52 GB/s, about 420 Gbps; peak about 1.2 Tbps. With a 95% CDN hit ratio (Chapter 12), the origin sees only 5%, about 60 Gbps at peak. The API payload (JSON for 20 posts, about 20 KB) at 100 K QPS is 2 GB/s, about 16 Gbps, which is a small part.

3.5 Read amplification

One feed load needs 20 post bodies. At 100 K peak QPS that is 2 M post lookups per second, which is why the post cache must hit above 95% and why we always fetch in batches.

Key idea The numbers say: 420 K feed insertions per second (write amplification of about 120x over the 3.5 K posts per second) buys a 100 K QPS read path that is a single cache read. That trade, doing work at write time to make reads cheap, is the core of the design, and the celebrity problem is where it breaks.

4. API design

REST-style endpoints (Chapter 5). All calls are authenticated; the user id comes from the token, never from the body.

POST /v1/posts
  body: { "text": "...", "media_ids": ["m_91"], "client_request_id": "uuid" }
  201:  { "post_id": "1871234567890123456", "created_at": "..." }

GET /v1/feed?mode=ranked|latest&limit=20&cursor=OPAQUE
  200: { "items": [ { "post_id", "author", "text", "media", "counts" } ],
         "next_cursor": "OPAQUE", "has_more": true }

PUT    /v1/posts/{id}      edit (within 30 min)
DELETE /v1/posts/{id}      delete
POST   /v1/users/{id}/follow     DELETE /v1/users/{id}/follow
POST   /v1/users/{id}/block      DELETE /v1/users/{id}/block
GET    /v1/users/{id}/posts?cursor=...    (profile timeline, not the home feed)

Three details matter. First, client_request_id makes post creation idempotent: a mobile client that retries after a timeout does not create duplicates (Chapter 32). Second, the cursor is opaque to the client so we can change its contents later. Third, media is uploaded separately (section 8) and only referenced by id, so the post API stays small and fast.

5.1 Schemas

posts:      post_id (PK, 64-bit time-ordered id) | author_id | text | media[] | created_at | edited_at | deleted(bool) | visibility
following:  (user_id, followee_id) PK, created_at     -- "whom do I follow", partition key user_id
followers:  (followee_id, follower_id) PK, created_at -- "who follows me", partition key followee_id
feed:       Redis ZSET  feed:{user_id}   member = post_id, score = rank_time or ranking score
user_flags: user_id | last_active_at | is_celebrity | follower_count

The post id is a time-ordered 64-bit id (Snowflake style: 41 bits of milliseconds, 10 bits of machine id, 12 bits of sequence; see Chapter 27). Time ordering means sorting by id is almost the same as sorting by creation time, so ids can double as scores and as pagination keys.

5.2 Why the graph is stored twice

Fan-out needs "all followers of the author", a scan over one partition of followers. Feed rebuild and celebrity pull need "everyone this user follows", a scan over one partition of following. One table cannot serve both access patterns with a single-partition scan, so on each follow we write both rows. The second write can lag, and a repair job reconciles the two tables (section 9).

6. A first simple design, then the scaled architecture

6.1 The simple version

Start with one application server and one database. Posting inserts a row. Opening the feed runs a query: find everyone I follow, fetch their latest posts, sort by time, return 20. This is fan-out on read in its purest form.

SELECT p.* FROM posts p JOIN following f ON f.followee_id = p.author_id
WHERE f.user_id = :me ORDER BY p.post_id DESC LIMIT 20;

It is correct and tiny to build, and it works for thousands of users. At our scale it fails: each feed load touches 200 authors' recent posts (200 index probes plus a merge), and at 100 K QPS that is 20 M probes per second on data spread across many shards, with the slowest shard deciding the latency. The tail latency of a scatter-gather over 200 partitions is poor. So we precompute.

7. Deep dive 1: fan-out on write, fan-out on read, and the hybrid

This is the question the interviewer is waiting for. There are two pure strategies and one practical compromise.

7.1 Fan-out on write (push)

When a post is created, copy its id into the feed list of every follower immediately. Reading a feed is then a single sorted-set range read. The cost moves to write time: a post by an account with F followers costs F insertions. For an average account (200 followers) that is nothing. For an account with 30 million followers it is 30 million insertions for one post, which at 100,000 insertions per second per worker pool of 100 workers takes 3 seconds of the whole pool, and repeats for every post that account makes. Worse, most of that work is wasted on followers who will not open the app today.

7.2 Fan-out on read (pull)

Store posts only once, under the author. At read time, fetch the follow list, get recent posts of each followee, merge. Writes are trivial and celebrity posts cost nothing extra. The price is the read path described in 6.1: many lookups per page, and heavy tail latency, for the 600:1 dominant operation.

7.3 The hybrid

The insight is that cost is driven by the skew. Most accounts are cheap to push. A tiny fraction of accounts (say above 100,000 followers, perhaps 0.01% of accounts) make push expensive. So:

  • Normal authors: push to active followers' feed caches (fan-out on write).
  • Celebrity authors (flag is_celebrity, set when follower count crosses a threshold, with hysteresis, for example on at 100 K and off at 50 K so accounts do not flap): do not fan out. Their posts live in a small, hot, separately cached "celebrity timeline" per author.
  • At read time, the Feed service reads the user's precomputed feed (pushed posts) and also pulls the recent posts from the celebrities this user follows, then merges them. A typical user follows only a handful of celebrities (say 5 to 20), so this adds a few hot-cache reads, not 200 probes.

We choose the hybrid. It keeps the 100 K QPS read path cheap, caps the write amplification, and treats the celebrity as the special case it is. The extra complexity is real: ranking must treat both candidate sources uniformly, and a user who just became a celebrity has old posts already pushed (harmless: the merge deduplicates by post id).

7.5 Making push efficient

  • Active-only push. Keep last_active_at; skip followers inactive for 7 days. When they return, the Feed service sees an empty or stale list and runs the rebuild path (pull from followees, section 9.3) and re-warms the cache. This turns 20 B into 12 B insertions per day.
  • Batching and pipelining. A worker reads 1,000 followers, groups them by Redis shard (hash of user id), and sends one pipelined batch per shard. Throughput of a single Redis shard is roughly 100,000 simple commands per second; our 420 K per second spread over 100 primaries is about 4,200 per second each, comfortable.
  • Partitioning the work. A single author with 90 K followers is split into chunk tasks (follower offset ranges) so one worker is not stuck; chunk tasks are idempotent because ZADD of the same member and score is a no-op.
  • Priority lanes. Separate topics for normal and large-author fan-out, so a burst from large accounts cannot delay everyone (Chapter 35).

8. Deep dives 2 to 4: feed cache and cursors, ranking, media

8.1 Feed cache structure: Redis list or sorted set?

The per-user feed is a bounded ordered list of post ids, not full posts. Storing ids keeps entries small (8 bytes) and means an edit or delete changes one record, the post, instead of 200 copies.

OptionStrengthsWeaknesses
Redis list (LPUSH then LTRIM to 500)Very cheap append at head, trivial trim, small memory.Position-based pagination only (LRANGE offset) which drifts when new items are pushed; cannot remove or re-rank a specific member cheaply (LREM is O(N)); no score so ranked mode needs a separate structure; late-arriving or backfilled items cannot be placed in order.
Redis sorted set (ZADD, ZREMRANGEBYRANK)Ordered by score; range by score gives stable cursors; ZREM of one post is O(log N); idempotent re-insert; backfill and repair insert in correct order; supports both modes.More memory per entry (skip list plus hash, about 2 to 3x a list); each insert is O(log N), fine for N = 500.

We choose the sorted set (Chapter 59 and Chapter 15 explain the structure). Each key is feed:{user_id}, member is the post id, score is the post id's timestamp (for latest mode) or a precomputed coarse score. After each insert we trim to the newest 500 entries. Keys have a TTL of about 14 days so abandoned accounts vanish, and a Lua script or pipeline does ZADD plus trim atomically enough in one round trip.

8.2 Pagination with cursors

Offset pagination (LIMIT 20 OFFSET 40) is wrong for feeds: when three new posts arrive between page 1 and page 2, every item shifts down by three and the user sees repeats. A cursor is a bookmark by value. For the latest mode the cursor is (last_score, last_post_id), base64-encoded and signed, and the next page is "20 items strictly older than this". Since post ids are unique and time-ordered, ties on the score are broken by id, so no item is skipped or repeated.

For the ranked mode the order is not a timestamp. We solve this by making the ranked list a short-lived snapshot: on the first request the ranker produces an ordered list of about 100 post ids and stores it under a session_id (Redis list with a 30 minute TTL); the cursor is then (session_id, position). This keeps order stable while scrolling, and when the snapshot runs out the next request builds a new one, excluding ids already shown (kept in a small "seen" set). The client also sends back the ids it has displayed so the server can drop duplicates even if the snapshot expired.

8.3 Timeline ordering and consistency

  • Ordering: the sorted set orders by score, and with time-ordered ids the order is creation order modulo small clock skew between id generators (a few milliseconds). Nobody notices.
  • Out-of-order arrival: if fan-out for post A is delayed behind post B (created later), ZADD still puts A in the right place, because position comes from the score, not arrival. A user mid-scroll may never see A if they already passed its slot; they will see it on next refresh. This is acceptable eventual consistency.
  • Read-your-writes: the author's own feed is written synchronously, and the client also optimistically inserts the new post locally.
  • Monotonic reads: a user hitting a lagging replica could see a post disappear. We read feed lists from the primary or use sticky routing to one replica per session (Chapter 24).

8.4 Ranking pipeline

Ranking is a separate service so the ranking team can iterate without touching storage. At a high level it has four stages.

Walkthrough. Four blue boxes in a row show stages left to right; each arrow means the output list of one stage is the input of the next. 1. Candidates: take the pushed ids from the sorted set plus celebrity posts, about 300 ids. This stage is cheap and aims not to miss good posts. 2. Features: fetch facts about each candidate, such as age, author affinity and engagement, from a feature store in batch. 3. Scoring: a model predicts the chance of a like, reply or long view and combines them into one score. 4. Re-ranking: apply rules such as diversity, dedupe and safety, and cut to the top 100 for the session snapshot. The funnel shape is the point: cheap steps see many items and the expensive step sees few. The note below gives a 100 ms budget and a recency fallback if the ranker times out. On a whiteboard, draw the funnel narrowing. Without the timeout fallback, a slow ranker makes the whole feed slow.
  1. Candidate generation. Union of the user's feed ZSET (newest 300) and recent celebrity posts. Optionally add "recommended" posts from accounts not followed, from a separate recommender.
  2. Feature fetch. For each candidate: post features (age, media type, text length, engagement so far), author-user affinity (how often this user interacts with that author), and user context (time of day, device). Features come from a feature store in batch calls.
  3. Scoring. A model predicts probabilities of actions such as like, comment, share and long dwell; a weighted sum gives one score. Models are trained offline on logged impressions and outcomes.
  4. Re-ranking. Rule-based adjustments: do not show five posts from one author in a row, remove near-duplicates, apply safety demotions, drop already-seen posts and insert sponsored slots.

The write path is unaffected: the sorted set only needs recency. Ranking happens at read time on a few hundred ids, which is affordable. Heavy users can have scores cached for a minute or two.

8.5 Media: upload, storage and CDN

Media never travels through the post service. The client asks for an upload URL, uploads the bytes straight to object storage using a pre-signed URL (Chapter 21), and gets a media_id. An asynchronous pipeline then virus-scans, generates resized renditions (thumbnail, 480, 1080 pixels; video transcodes into multiple bitrates), and marks the media ready. The post may only be published after the media is ready, or it is published with a placeholder and the client re-fetches. The feed JSON carries CDN URLs; the CDN pulls from object storage on first miss and keeps popular images at the edge (Chapter 12). URLs contain a content hash so they are immutable and cacheable for a year, and deletion works through the post record, not by purging every edge.

9. Deletes, edits, blocks and feed repair

The precomputed feed is a cache of references, so the design question is how to keep it truthful when the world changes.

9.1 Edits

Feeds hold only post ids, and the Feed service hydrates bodies from the post cache. An edit updates the post row and invalidates its cache entry; every feed reflects the edit on its next read with no fan-out. This is the main reason for storing ids rather than copies.

9.2 Deletes and blocks

9.3 Feed repair and rebuild

Walkthrough. Four boxes read left to right, with a red box first because it is the problem state. 1. Trigger: a cache miss, a lost Redis node, or an inactive user returning leaves an empty or old sorted set. 2. Get followees: read the user's following list from the graph store. 3. Fetch recent posts: take about the last 20 posts of each followed author from the author timelines. 4. Bulk ZADD: write the newest 500 into the sorted set, with their scores. The text under it gives the cost: 200 followees times 20 posts is 4,000 candidate ids, a few hundred milliseconds in parallel batches. The guards matter more than the boxes: a lock per user so only one rebuild runs, a global rate limit, and a partial feed shown early. This is the same logic as pure pull, run for one user on demand. If you remove the guards, a node loss turns into a stampede on the graph store, and if you remove the rebuild itself, an empty list is permanent.

Repair covers three situations. Lost cache: if a Redis shard dies, its users hit a rebuild on next read; rebuilds are single-flight per user and rate-limited so a node loss does not become a stampede on the graph and post stores (Chapter 14). Drift: a nightly sampled job compares a few thousand feeds against what a rebuild would produce and alerts if the difference is above a threshold. Lost fan-out events: the worker acknowledges the Kafka offset only after the pipeline succeeds, so a crash replays; because ZADD is idempotent, replays are safe (Chapter 32).

10. Scaling, hot users, sharding and failure modes

10.1 Sharding

DataShard keyReason and caveat
Feed cacheHash of user_id (consistent hashing or Redis Cluster slots, Chapter 25)All of one user's feed on one shard, so a read is one call. Uniform because users are uniform in feed size (bounded 500).

10.2 Hot users and hot keys

  • Celebrity timeline reads: one account's recent posts are read by millions of feed loads. Keep it in an in-process cache on every Feed service instance with a TTL of a few seconds, plus replicated copies in Redis. A celebrity post then reaches users within the TTL.
  • Viral post: the post body is read by many; the post cache uses local near caches and request coalescing, and counters are served from a separate counter service with sampled updates.
  • Hot Redis shard: a shard whose users are all very active. Detect with per-shard QPS dashboards; mitigate by adding read replicas for feed reads and resharding slots.
  • Thundering fan-out: a big event (a final, an election) makes many accounts post at once. Kafka absorbs the burst; workers autoscale on consumer lag (Chapter 43); backpressure drops the push for the least active followers first.

10.3 New-post latency budget

Target for a normal user's post: visible to active followers within 5 seconds p99. Budget: API and database write 100 ms, outbox relay 100 ms, Kafka 50 ms, queueing in fan-out 1 to 3 seconds in normal load, worker work (200 followers) 50 ms, so about 2 to 4 seconds. The metric to watch is end-to-end fan-out lag, measured as feed insertion time minus post creation time, which should alarm when p99 passes 10 seconds.

10.5 Multi-region

Run full stacks in several regions; each user has a home region (Chapter 26). Posts and graph writes go to the author's home region and replicate asynchronously to others. Each region maintains feed caches for its users, filled by consuming the replicated event stream. A follower in another region sees the post after replication lag (typically under a second, bounded by seconds). During a region failure, users are routed to a neighbour and rebuild their feeds from replicated posts and graph; the feed is stale for some minutes, which we accept (Chapter 41).

11. Security, notification hooks, monitoring and cost

11.1 Security and abuse

  • Authorization on read: private accounts and blocks are enforced at hydration time, not only at fan-out, because the feed cache can be stale (Chapter 55).
  • Spam and bots: post rate limits per account and per IP, follow rate limits and new-account quarantines at the gateway (Chapter 16). A spammer that follows 100 K accounts is also a fan-in abuse vector.
  • Cursor tampering: cursors are signed so clients cannot craft arbitrary positions or session ids.
  • Moderation: an asynchronous classifier consumes PostCreated events; flagged posts get a visibility flag, which read-time filtering enforces within seconds.

11.2 Notification hooks

The system does not send notifications itself. The same PostCreated topic is consumed by the notification service (Case study 8), which reads a per-follower "alert me when X posts" setting, applies rate limits and quiet hours, and sends push messages. Notifications for accounts with huge followings go through the same celebrity logic (batched, throttled, and only for opted-in followers). Other consumers, such as search indexing and analytics, subscribe to the same stream, so the feed does not need to know about them.

11.4 Cost

  • Feed cache RAM is the biggest compute cost: 200 nodes of 64 to 128 GB memory. Levers: shrink entries (store only 8 B ids with compact encodings), keep only 300 entries for casual users, shorten the TTL for inactive users, and push only to active followers (saves 40% of both writes and memory).
  • Media storage is the biggest storage cost: 60 TB per day, 22 PB per year. Levers: lifecycle rules moving media older than 90 days to colder tiers, efficient codecs (WebP or AVIF, newer video codecs), and not keeping all renditions of old content.
  • CDN egress is the biggest bandwidth cost: 52 GB/s average. A hit ratio of 95% versus 90% halves origin egress. Negotiated CDN rates and own edge caches are the long-term lever.
  • Ranking compute scales with feed loads: 100 K QPS x 300 candidates = 30 M scored items per second at peak; use lightweight models, cache scores, and skip ranking for latest mode.

12. Evolution, phasing and alternatives not chosen

12.1 What to build first

  1. Phase 1 (up to about 1 M users): one relational database, fan-out on read with an index on (author_id, post_id). Ship the product.
  2. Phase 2 (up to about 50 M): add Redis feed ZSETs and push fan-out via a queue; rebuild on miss. Add a CDN for media. Cursor pagination from day one, since changing it later breaks clients.
  3. Phase 3 (500 M, this design): shard everything, introduce the celebrity path, the ranking service, active-only push and multi-region.

12.2 At 10x (5 B users, 3 M feed QPS)

The graph store and the feed cache shard counts grow 10x; fan-out lag becomes the dominant risk, so we move to cell-based deployment (Chapter 42), each cell owning a slice of users with its own Redis, Kafka and workers. The celebrity threshold gets lowered (as the total fan-out is proportional to followers, the cost-benefit moves). More of the ranking runs on precomputed per-user candidate pools, and feeds shrink to ids plus a few features to reduce memory.

12.3 Alternatives not chosen

  • Pure pull: simple but fails the tail latency at 600:1 read ratio.
  • Pure push: a 50 M follower account makes one post cost 50 M writes; also wastes work on inactive users.
  • Storing full post bodies in the feed: faster hydration, but an edit or delete has to rewrite hundreds of millions of copies and memory grows by 30 to 100x.
  • A graph database for the follow graph: useful for multi-hop queries (friends of friends), which the feed does not need; a sharded key-value store is cheaper and scales more simply.
  • Offset pagination: rejected for duplicate and skip problems under concurrent inserts.

13. Interview Q&A

Why not just compute the feed on read with a join, like a normal database application?

A join works when a user follows a few accounts. Here a user follows about 200, and each feed load would need the newest posts from 200 different authors, spread over many shards. At 100 K feed loads per second that is 20 million partition probes per second, and the response time of each page equals the slowest of 200 parallel calls, so tail latency is dominated by the worst shard. Precomputing the list at write time converts that into one sorted-set range read. The price is write amplification, which we then bound with the hybrid design. I would still keep the pull code, since it is the rebuild path.

How do you decide who counts as a celebrity, and what happens at the boundary?

I use follower count, because it directly measures fan-out cost, with a threshold around 100,000 where a single post costs about 100,000 insertions. To avoid flapping, I use hysteresis, turning the flag on above 100 K and off below 50 K. Accounts near the line are fine either way, since both strategies work for them, only at different costs. Posts made before the flag flipped remain in followers' sorted sets and are deduplicated by post id when merged with celebrity posts. As a safety net, the fan-out worker enforces a hard cap on insertions per post and sends the rest of the followers to the pull path.

A user follows 3,000 accounts, 20 of whom are celebrities. How does the feed read change?

The pushed part is unchanged: one range read from their sorted set. For celebrities, the Feed service reads 20 celebrity timelines. Each is a small, very hot list that is cached in process and in Redis, so these are mostly memory hits and are issued in parallel, adding a few milliseconds. We take only the last few posts from each, merge by score, and dedupe against the pushed list. If a user follows hundreds of celebrities, we limit the pull to the most engaged 50 celebrities, plus a precomputed per-user "celebrity candidates" list refreshed every minute.

How does pagination stay correct when new posts keep arriving?

I use cursors that encode a position by value, not offset. In latest mode the cursor is the last score and post id seen, and the next page asks for items strictly older. New posts are added at the head so they do not shift older pages and nothing repeats. In ranked mode the order depends on a model, so I snapshot the top 100 ranked ids at first request under a session id and page through the snapshot by position, and build a new snapshot (excluding seen ids) when it runs out. The cursor is opaque and signed, so I can change the internal format later.

A user deletes a post, or blocks someone. How quickly does it vanish from feeds, and how do you guarantee it?

Feeds only store ids, and every page is hydrated against the post store or cache, where deletes are tombstones and block sets are checked. So the post disappears on the next read, regardless of how many feed copies exist, without a fan-out of removals. A background job then removes the stale ids from sorted sets to save memory. The important design decision is that correctness for privacy-sensitive changes lives in read-time filtering, and the physical cleanup is an optimisation. The page over-fetches to remain full after filtering.

What happens if a Redis feed shard fails?

A replica is promoted, typically in seconds, and replication is asynchronous so a few of the latest insertions might be lost. If both die, users on that shard have empty feeds, and the Feed service treats an empty or missing list as a miss and rebuilds it from the graph and author timelines. I guard that with single-flight per user and a global rate limit, so a node loss of about 3 million users does not turn into a stampede on the graph and post stores, and serve partial feeds first. The cache is therefore a derived, recoverable structure, not a source of truth, which is why we can run it without strong durability.

How do you keep fan-out from delaying the author's own experience or creating duplicates?

The post request returns after the durable write and the event is stored (using the outbox pattern), and fan-out is asynchronous, so the author never waits on it. The author's own feed gets the post synchronously for read-your-writes. Duplicates cannot occur in the feed because a sorted set member is unique, and a replayed fan-out simply re-adds the same member with the same score. Client retries are covered by a client request id that makes post creation idempotent.

How would you add a "recommended posts from accounts you do not follow" section?

That is another candidate source, not a change to the storage design. A separate recommender produces candidate post ids per user (from engagement signals, graph neighbours or topics) and writes them to a per-user candidate list or serves them on demand. The ranking service merges them with followed content, marks their source for the client, and the re-ranking stage enforces a mix ratio, for example no more than one in four. Because the feed stores ids and ranking operates at read time, this plugs in without touching the fan-out pipeline.

How would you handle the cold start of a new user, or a user returning after a month?

A new user has no follows, so we show an onboarding flow and a recommended or trending feed built from popular posts, and as they follow accounts we backfill each one's recent posts into the sorted set. A returning user was dropped from active-only push, so their sorted set is expired or stale. On first open the Feed service detects a missing or old list and runs the rebuild path, returning a quick partial result from the most recent authors and finishing the rest in the background. The cost is limited by the rebuild rate limit, and warming can be spread in advance by detecting an opened push notification or email.

If an interviewer asks you to make the feed strictly consistent, what do you say?

I would first ask what guarantee actually matters. For a social feed, the guarantees that matter are read-your-writes for the author, monotonic reads for a session, and that deletes and blocks apply promptly. We provide those with synchronous author writes, sticky replica routing and read-time filtering. A total order seen identically by all followers at the same instant would need cross-shard coordination on each post, which would cost latency and availability for no user-visible benefit, so I would explain the choice using the availability side of the trade-off in Chapter 28.

Recap

  • The feed is a write-versus-read trade: at 100 K peak read QPS and 3.5 K posts per second, precomputing with fan-out on write (about 420 K insertions per second at peak) makes reads one sorted-set range read.
  • Pure push fails on celebrities and pure pull fails on tail latency; the hybrid pushes for normal authors to active followers only, and pulls celebrity posts at read time.
  • Feeds hold ids in Redis sorted sets (about 5 TB, about 200 nodes with replicas), so edits are free and deletes and blocks are enforced by read-time filtering plus lazy cleanup.
  • Pagination uses cursors by value, with a session snapshot in ranked mode; offsets are rejected.
  • Ranking is a separate four-stage pipeline (candidates, features, scoring, re-ranking) with a recency fallback.
  • Failures are handled by treating the feed cache as rebuildable: single-flight rebuild, idempotent replayable fan-out, and graceful degradation to partial or recency-sorted feeds.
  • Media goes straight to object storage and is served by the CDN; the largest costs are feed RAM, media storage and egress.
